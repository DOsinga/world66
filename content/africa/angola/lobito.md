Lobito is Angola's most important port city, built on a remarkable natural feature: a thin sandbar spit called the Restinga that juts 5 km into the Atlantic, sheltering one of the finest natural harbours on the West African coast. The city grew up around this harbour in the early twentieth century, and the harbour remains its reason for being.

The [Benguela Railway](/africa/angola/benguela) — once the vital link between the Atlantic and the copper belt of central Africa — had Lobito as its western terminus. Built from 1904 onwards, it connected the port to the Democratic Republic of Congo and Zambia, carrying copper and other minerals to ships waiting in the bay. The railway fell into disrepair during Angola's long civil war but has been restored in sections, and goods traffic has resumed on parts of the line. Walking along the old Restinga to the port district, with its colonial-era warehouses and the constant presence of the sea, gives you a strong sense of the commercial ambition that built this place.

The Restinga itself is the social heart of the city — a narrow strip of land lined with markets, small restaurants serving grilled fish, and locals who come to escape the mainland heat. The harbour behind it is busy with fishing vessels and occasional larger cargo ships. [Benguela](/africa/angola/benguela), Angola's fourth-largest city, is only 30 km to the south and makes a natural day trip or onward stop.

Lobito is not a destination for sightseeing in any conventional sense — it has no major monuments and the postwar reconstruction is still uneven. But it is a working Angolan city with a distinctive character shaped by its port history, and for travelers heading into or out of the Benguela corridor, it is a place that rewards a day or two of exploration.
