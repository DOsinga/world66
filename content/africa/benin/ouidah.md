Ouidah is one of the most historically charged towns in West Africa. For nearly two centuries it was the principal port through which enslaved people were shipped to the Americas — estimates suggest more than a million people left from its shore. That history is inescapable here, and it lends the town a weight that few other places in the region carry.

Bruce Chatwin's novel *The Viceroy of Ouidah* brought the town to wider attention, telling the story of Francisco de Souza, a Brazilian slave trader who became one of the most powerful men on the coast. His descendants still live in Ouidah, and [his former compound](/africa/benin/ouidah/maison_de_souza) is among the sites worth visiting.

Ouidah is also the spiritual home of Vodun — the religious tradition that spread from here to Haiti and Brazil. The [Python Temple](/africa/benin/ouidah/python_temple), where sacred pythons move freely through the compound, is a genuine cult site and not just a tourist attraction. The annual Vodun Festival in January draws practitioners from across the diaspora. The [Route des Esclaves](/africa/benin/ouidah/route_des_esclaves), a three-kilometre path leading to the sea, is lined with sculptures and ends at the [Door of No Return](/africa/benin/ouidah/door_of_no_return), a memorial arch on the beach. The old Portuguese fort in the centre of town now houses the [Musée d'Histoire](/africa/benin/ouidah/musee_dhistoire), and the [Forêt Sacrée](/africa/benin/ouidah/foret_sacree_kpasse) holds sculptures of the major Vodun divinities.

The town is 40 km west of Cotonou and can be visited as a long day trip, though an overnight stay rewards you with a slower encounter with its temples, shrines, and colonial-era architecture.
