The tata somba is a small, ingenious piece of fortress architecture. Each compound is built of cob (mud and straw) reinforced with wooden poles, on a rough rectangular footprint with rounded towers at the corners and a single low entrance. The ground floor is dim, smoky, and given over to animals — a goat pen, a cooking hearth, sometimes a grain store. The upper level, reached by a notched log ladder through a hatch, is the living floor: sleeping rooms, an open terrace where the family eats and weaves, and bottle-shaped clay granaries with thatched conical caps.

Everything has a purpose. The thick mud walls keep the interior cool in the dry season and dry in the wet. The single low entrance can be defended by one person with a stick. The high terrace doubles as a lookout and a threshing floor. The granaries are sealed against rodents. None of this was designed in an architect's office; it evolved over many generations as the Batammariba refined a way of building that could survive both the climate and the raiders. The fact that families still live in tata today — even as cement and corrugated iron creep into the region — is a quiet vote of confidence in a design that works.
