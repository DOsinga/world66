The Makgadikgadi Pans are among the largest salt flats on earth — the dried beds of an ancient super-lake that once covered much of northern Botswana. Today they stretch vast and white toward the horizon, broken by the occasional baobab or island of palms. In the dry season (May to October) the pans are bleached moonscapes where mirages shimmer; in the wet season they flood and become a magnet for flamingos, pelicans, and wading birds.

Access from Maun is about two to three hours by road, making the pans a manageable overnight trip. Nata Lodge on the northern edge of the pans is the most common base. Self-drive is possible on the outer rim roads; venturing onto the pans themselves requires careful timing and an experienced guide — the salt crust can be dangerously thin.

Meerkats are the other big draw: habituated groups in the Makgadikgadi Pans National Park allow people to sit among them at dawn, watching the colony warm itself. The experience is informal and genuinely unusual — wild animals behaving normally with humans nearby because they have learned humans are not a threat.
