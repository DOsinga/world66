Eighteen interconnected lakes scattered across a hyper-arid stretch of northeastern Chad — a place that has no right to have water at all, yet does. The Lakes of Ounianga were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2012, the citation noting that they represent the largest permanent freshwater lake complex in the Sahara Desert.

The lakes persist because of a vast ancient aquifer beneath the desert. During the African Humid Period, roughly 10,000 years ago, this entire region was wet. As the Sahara dried, the groundwater retreated underground but never vanished. It still feeds the lakes today through underground seepage, moving so slowly that the water is sometimes tens of thousands of years old.

The visual impact is hard to convey without photographs. Deep blue and turquoise pools sit in bowls of pale sand, their surfaces framed by date palms and reed beds. The most saline lakes are stained red and orange by halophilic algae — colours that shift through the day as the light changes. Fish, migratory birds, and crocodiles have been recorded here, an implausible assemblage for the middle of the world's largest desert.

The lakes divide into two groups roughly 40 km apart: the larger [Ounianga Kébir](/africa/chad/ounianga/ounianga_kebir) group near the main settlement, and the more remote [Ounianga Sérir](/africa/chad/ounianga/ounianga_serir) group to the southeast.

story: >
  The survival of the Ounianga lakes puzzled scientists for decades. The lakes lose far more water to evaporation than falls as rain — annual rainfall here is under 2mm. The answer turned out to lie under the sand: a system of freshwater lenses floats atop denser saline groundwater, seeping upward at a rate that precisely replaces evaporative losses. Without this sub-Saharan fossil aquifer, the lakes would have dried up thousands of years ago.
