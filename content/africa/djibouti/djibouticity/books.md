---
title: "Books"
type: section
---

Djibouti occupies an unusual literary position: a tiny country at a major geopolitical crossroads, with a rich oral tradition in Somali and Afar that has been less well served by translated literature than its strategic importance might suggest. These books help fill in the picture.

**A Country of Words** by Abdourahman A. Waberi — Waberi is Djibouti's most celebrated novelist and a key voice in Francophone African literature. This collection of linked stories explores life in Djibouti City and the wider country through spare, precise prose. Waberi grew up in Djibouti before moving to France, and his outsider-insider perspective gives the work unusual depth. The city feels recognisable in his descriptions even decades after they were written.

**Passage of Darkness: The Ethnobiology of the Haitian Zombie** — not Djibouti specifically, but the broader Horn of Africa and Afar region is best understood through its geological extremity. Richard Estes's natural history writings on the Horn are excellent background for understanding what lies outside the city.

**In the Footsteps of Mr. Kurtz** by Michela Wrong — Wrong's reporting from across Sub-Saharan Africa provides essential context for understanding the political economy of the region, including the military-base dynamic that defines Djibouti's modern identity. Not about Djibouti directly, but indispensable for understanding how the place works.

**Red Sea Requiem** by Arthur Foss — an account of the French colonial presence in the region and the transition to independence. Foss draws on archival material to reconstruct the political manoeuvrings of the 1960s and 1970s that produced the improbable independent state. Dated in some respects but still the most detailed account of the independence period in English.

**The Looming Tower** by Lawrence Wright — the geopolitical position of Djibouti as a counterterrorism hub makes Wright's account of al-Qaeda's development in the Horn of Africa region essential context for understanding why so many foreign militaries have based themselves here and what Camp Lemonnier actually represents.
