  Al-Muizz Street was the ceremonial spine of Fatimid Cairo — the route by which the Caliph processed through the city on feast days, flanked by mosques, palaces, and market halls built to impress and overawe. The Fatimids founded Cairo in 969 CE and named it al-Qāhira, "The Conqueror," after Mars (al-Qāhir), which was rising at the moment they marked out the city's foundations. A thousand years of subsequent rulers each left their mark on this street.
---

Al-Muizz Street runs through the heart of Islamic Cairo and is arguably the greatest surviving collection of medieval Islamic architecture anywhere in the world. Along a single kilometre, the street passes through a thousand years of Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk, and Ottoman buildings — mosques, madrasas, mausoleums, and the gates of the original city wall.

The northern section is the richest: the Bab al-Futuh and Bab al-Nasr gates at the north end were built in 1087 and still stand, flanked by towers you can climb for views over the city. The Qalawun complex, the Barquq mosque, and the Sultan al-Muayyad mosque are the architectural highlights along the route. The street is closed to traffic and most atmospheric in the late afternoon, when the shadows lengthen and the light falls sideways on the carved stone facades.
