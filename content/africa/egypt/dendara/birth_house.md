  Birth houses — mammisi in Coptic — were a feature of Late Period and Ptolemaic Egyptian temples. They were small chapels dedicated to celebrating the divine birth of the king, connecting the reigning pharaoh to a divine parentage and legitimising his rule. The mammisi at Dendara was built during the Roman Imperial period, after Egypt had been absorbed into the Roman Empire, yet it still follows Egyptian architectural and religious conventions almost exactly — Roman emperors presented themselves as pharaohs and maintained the traditional religious forms well into the Common Era.

The Roman Birth House (mammisi) stands in front of the main Temple of Hathor, a smaller free-standing chapel built in the Roman period to celebrate the divine birth of Harsomtus, the child of Hathor and Horus worshipped at Dendara. It follows a standard mammisi plan but is decorated with carvings that mix Egyptian hieroglyphic tradition with touches of Greco-Roman style — a visible manifestation of the cultural blend that characterised Roman Egypt.

The mammisi's columns and exterior walls retain good painted reliefs showing ritual scenes of divine birth and nursing. An earlier Ptolemaic birth house stands nearby, incorporated into the outer enclosure wall. Together they illustrate how the birth house tradition evolved over several centuries of foreign rule, with Egyptian religious forms maintaining remarkable continuity.
