At the foot of the terraced Temple of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahri, a small garden contains myrrh trees said to be descended from those Hatshepsut brought back from her expedition to the Land of Punt around 1470 BC. The trees are unremarkable to look at — small, scrubby, drought-resistant — but their historical context makes them remarkable objects.

The temple reliefs record the Punt expedition in unusual narrative detail, showing the boats setting out, the reception at Punt, and the loading of the trees into the ship's hold with their root balls carefully wrapped. That any botanical tradition connecting modern trees to ancient ones is unprovable does not diminish the site. Standing in front of a myrrh tree in the shadow of Hatshepsut's temple, knowing the stories on the walls above, is one of those small moments that accumulate into a sense of what Luxor actually is.
