  The White Chapel of Senusret I, housed in the open air museum, is one of the oldest and best-preserved examples of Middle Kingdom art in Egypt. It was built around 1960 BC and later dismantled and used as rubble filling for a later pylon. When archaeologists removed the pylon in the 20th century, they found hundreds of decorated blocks — enough to reconstruct the chapel almost entirely. The reliefs show Senusret I in ritual embrace with the gods, carved with a delicacy that makes later New Kingdom work look slightly coarser.

The Karnak Open Air Museum occupies a courtyard in the northeast corner of the main Karnak enclosure, displaying monuments that have been reassembled from blocks found scattered across the site. It is a quieter corner of Karnak, typically bypassed by tour groups, but it contains some of the finest individual pieces in the complex.

The star exhibit is the White Chapel of Senusret I, a Middle Kingdom limestone kiosk with superbly preserved reliefs. There is also the Red Chapel of Hatshepsut (Chapelle Rouge), a pink granite sanctuary that was dismantled by later pharaohs and reassembled by archaeologists. The contrast between these two structures — one from the 12th dynasty, one from the 18th, separated by 500 years — shows the continuity and variation in Egyptian temple building. Entry is usually included with the main Karnak ticket; ask at the entrance.
