Equatorial Guinea is one of Africa's smallest and least-visited countries, and one of its most unusual. It is the only country in continental Africa where Spanish is the official language — a legacy of three centuries of Spanish colonial rule — and it is also the only country in Africa straddling both an island and a mainland portion. The island of Bioko, in the Gulf of Guinea, holds the capital [Malabo](/africa/equatorialguinea/malabo) and most of the population. The mainland section, [Rio Muni](/africa/equatorialguinea/riomuni), is covered in dense equatorial rainforest.

The discovery of offshore oil in the 1990s transformed the economy almost overnight. Equatorial Guinea became one of sub-Saharan Africa's largest oil producers, and the money reshaped [Malabo](/africa/equatorialguinea/malabo) and the mainland capital Bata with new roads, government buildings, and a small wealthy elite. But the wealth has not spread widely, and the country remains under the long-running rule of President Obiang, who took power in a coup in 1979. Visas are difficult to obtain and the bureaucracy is formidable, which keeps independent travel to a trickle.

For those who make it in, the rewards are real: Bioko's cloud forests shelter rare primates including drill monkeys, giant forest hogs, and the dense tropical interior of Rio Muni. The island of [Annobon](/africa/equatorialguinea/annobon), a remote volcanic speck 500 km to the southwest, is one of the most isolated inhabited islands in Africa. [Luba](/africa/equatorialguinea/luba) on Bioko's southern coast offers leatherback turtle nesting beaches and exceptional biodiversity.
