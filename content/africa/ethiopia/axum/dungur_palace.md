  The palace is universally known in Axum as the Queen of Sheba's Palace, though archaeologists date it to the 4th–6th centuries AD — roughly 1,500 years after the Queen of Sheba's supposed lifetime. The name has stuck regardless. Local guides will recount the legend in full detail, and there is something to the Aksumite habit of layering mythology onto historical sites: the story of the Queen of Sheba is so central to Ethiopian identity that the distinction between the historical and the legendary has become genuinely unimportant.

Dungur Palace stands on the western edge of Axum, across the road from the Gudit Stelae Field. The ruins are the foundations and lowest courses of walls of a substantial structure — at its peak it covered roughly 3,250 square metres and included a central hall, multiple courtyards, and around fifty rooms. It was clearly the residence of someone very powerful.

Walking the site gives a clear sense of the floorplan despite the low walls: the layout is methodical, the stone work precise. A raised platform at the entrance would have supported a formal reception area. The Gudit Stelae Field behind the palace is less visited than the Northern Stelae Field but contains some striking fallen obelisks and is worth crossing the road to see.
