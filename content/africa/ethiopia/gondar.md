Gondar was founded by Emperor Fasilidas around 1635 and served as Ethiopia's royal capital for over two centuries. It is famous for a remarkable complex of medieval castles — a walled enclosure called the Royal Enclosure (Fasil Ghebbi) — that looks more like something from medieval Europe than from equatorial Africa. UNESCO has recognised the site as a World Heritage area, and it remains the centrepiece of any visit to the city.

The church of Debre Berhan Selassie, a short walk from the enclosure, is considered one of the finest examples of Gondarene ecclesiastical art. Its interior walls and ceiling are covered in paintings, including the celebrated ceiling with its rows of cherub faces — hundreds of winged heads looking down in every direction. The church survived the 1888 sack of Gondar by the forces of the Mahdi reportedly because a swarm of bees drove the attackers away; locals regard this as divine protection.

Gondar is probably the most immediately impressive of Ethiopia's historical towns, though it may be less profoundly moving than Lalibela or Axum. The city is large by Ethiopian standards — around 350,000 people — and it has a reputation as one of the most welcoming stops on the northern historic circuit. The town centre retains some Italian colonial-era architecture from the occupation of 1936–41. Nearby, the village of Wolleka was home to the Beta Israel (Ethiopian Jewish) community before most emigrated to Israel in the 1980s and 1990s.

Gondar is well placed as a base for visits to the Simien Mountains, one of Africa's great highland wilderness areas and another UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city also hosts one of Ethiopia's most spectacular celebrations of Timkat — the Ethiopian Orthodox Epiphany — in January, when thousands gather at the Fasilidas Baths for a ritual blessing of the water.
