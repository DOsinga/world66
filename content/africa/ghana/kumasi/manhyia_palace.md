  Every six weeks on the Akwasidae Sunday, the Asantehene sits in state in the palace courtyard. Chiefs, sub-chiefs, and dignitaries arrive in palanquins, drummers beat the talking drums, and ordinary citizens come to greet their king. It is one of the few places in the world where you can witness a functioning pre-colonial monarchy at work.

The modern Manhyia Palace, built in the 1970s alongside the old palace that is now a museum, is the working residence of the Asantehene — the king of the Ashanti. The compound is officially private, but visitors can attend the public Akwasidae ceremonies and walk the gardens between events.

The Akwasidae itself is held every forty-two days. Schedules are published in advance; dress modestly and arrive early if you want a view. Even outside ceremonies, the gate guards are often willing to let respectful visitors take a photograph from the courtyard.
