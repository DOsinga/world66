Taiye Selasi's 2013 debut novel follows a Ghanaian-Nigerian family dispersed across continents — a surgeon father in Accra, children studying in America and Britain, a family held together and torn apart by migration, memory, and the particular silence that forms around things left unsaid. It is a novel about the Ghanaian diaspora written from inside it, and it captures something real about what Ghana means to people who grew up partly elsewhere: the pull of a country that is home in theory and foreign in practice.

The book is beautifully written and emotionally precise. For travellers coming to Ghana with some connection to the diaspora experience — as visitors of Ghanaian heritage, or simply as people curious about what it means to belong to a country you half-know — it offers an emotional map that no guidebook can. Selasi grew up between the US and London but identifies deeply as Ghanaian, and that double perspective gives the novel its particular quality: loving and clear-eyed at the same time.
