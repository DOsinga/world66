Techiman is the current seat of the Bono Traditional Area, the successor to one of the oldest kingdoms in what is now Ghana. The Bono state traces its roots to around the 11th century, making it one of the earliest organised polities in the forest-savanna zone of West Africa. According to oral tradition, the Bono people emerged from a sacred cave called Amowi near Techiman — the name "Bono" itself derives from "Abono woo," meaning pioneer or firstborn of the land.

The kingdom flourished on gold and trade. By the time Portuguese merchants arrived in 1471, Bono Manso — then the capital — was already an established urban centre with long-distance connections stretching to Djenné, Timbuktu, and North Africa. Kente cloth, pottery, and metallurgy were produced locally; gold dust was the currency.

In 1722, Asante forces under Opoku Ware I destroyed Bono Manso and ended the kingdom's independence. The Bono capital relocated to Techiman, which has served as the political and spiritual heart of Bono identity ever since. The Techimanhene (the paramount chief) remains an important figure in Ghanaian traditional governance. You will see his palace — a compound in the centre of town — though it is not generally open to visitors.

The cultural legacy shows up in festivals, particularly Apoo (a purification festival held in the early part of the year) when the town fills with drumming, processions, and royal regalia.

story: "When the Asante destroyed Bono Manso in 1722, they were not just conquering a rival — they were dismantling a kingdom that had traded gold with North Africa for at least 700 years. The Bono had been in contact with Muslim merchants from Djenné before the Portuguese ever reached the West African coast."
