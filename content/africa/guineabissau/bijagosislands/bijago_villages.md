  The Bijago society is one of the few matrilineal cultures in West Africa where women also control land, political succession, and the initiation of marriages. A woman courts a man by building him a hut — acceptance of the hut constitutes an agreement to marry. The seven-year male initiation cycle, which moves young men through a series of age-grades each associated with specific dances and responsibilities, is still practised across the islands.
---

The Bijago people have maintained a remarkably intact culture despite centuries of contact with the Portuguese and later the post-colonial state. Their villages on the inhabited islands are governed by traditional chiefs and organised around sacred forests that non-initiates may not enter. The society is matrilineal and matrilocal — property and authority pass through the female line, and young men move to their wives' villages at marriage.

Visits to Bijago villages work best when arranged through a local guide from Bubaque who has existing relationships with the community. Showing up unannounced at remote villages is considered disrespectful. With a proper introduction, visitors are generally welcomed and may be invited to witness dances or ceremonies. Photography should be asked about before pointing any camera. The sacred forests, visible as dense patches of old growth near each village, are off-limits to outsiders entirely.
