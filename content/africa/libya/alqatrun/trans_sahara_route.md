Al Qatrun sits on one of the historic trans-Saharan caravan routes that connected the Mediterranean coast of North Africa to the kingdoms and trading centres of the Sahel and sub-Saharan Africa. For centuries, caravans carrying slaves, ivory, salt, and gold followed routes through the Fezzan, with oases like Al Qatrun serving as water stops and resting points. The trade was dangerous, slow, and profitable, and it sustained a string of settlements across the desert interior.

In the 20th century the caravans gave way to trucks, and the trade routes became roads. Al Qatrun's Niger consulate became a practical facility for the overland travellers — mostly European adventurers and aid workers in the 1970s and 1980s — who drove south to West Africa via Libya. That era of relatively open overland travel through the Fezzan has closed, probably for the foreseeable future. The Libya-Niger-Chad triangle is now one of the world's more dangerous transit zones: the historic routes that carried caravans now carry migrants and weapons under conditions of armed conflict. Travel through this region requires not just logistics but a clear-eyed assessment of security conditions that cannot be provided by any guidebook.
