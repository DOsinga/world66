  The siege of Misurata lasted from February to May 2011 and became one of the
  most intense urban battles in recent Middle Eastern history. Gaddafi's forces
  surrounded the city and attacked it with tanks, snipers, and artillery over
  nearly three months. The defenders — mostly civilian volunteers — held out,
  receiving some aid by sea. The siege ended when NATO airstrikes degraded
  Gaddafi's armour and rebel forces broke through. The city emerged as one of
  the most militarily capable factions in post-Gaddafi Libya.

The War Museum of Misurata documents the 2011 siege with collections of weapons, vehicles, photographs, and personal testimonies from both sides. The museum was established quickly after the conflict by the city's revolutionaries, and it has the raw, immediate quality of a memorial created by the people who lived through the events. Tanks, artillery pieces, and other military hardware are displayed outside the main building.

The collection includes weapons and equipment used by both the defenders and Gaddafi's forces, along with documentation of the humanitarian crisis during the siege. It's a sobering museum that doesn't flinch from the scale of the violence, and it's an important record of one of the defining events of the Arab Spring. Visitors should be aware that the political context of the 2011 war remains sensitive in Libya.
