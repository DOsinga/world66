Toamasina — still commonly known by its French colonial name Tamatave — is Madagascar's largest port and its second city, sprawling along a narrow coastal peninsula between the Indian Ocean and a network of lagoons. It is not a city of grand monuments or polished tourist infrastructure, but it has a raw, tropical energy that rewards patience. The ocean is everywhere: the smell of salt, the rumble of container ships in the harbour, the fishing pirogues pulled up on the beach at dawn. Boulevard Joffre, lined with palms and crumbling colonial-era facades, gives the centre an almost Indochine atmosphere.

The port drives everything here. Toamasina handles the vast majority of Madagascar's international trade, and the economic activity that flows from it — or fails to flow — shapes daily life throughout the city. The central market, the Bazary Be, is the commercial heart of town: a labyrinth of stalls selling vanilla, cloves, raffia hats, and piles of dried fish. Markets are also where the city's Franco-Malagasy culinary character shows itself most clearly, with vendors offering brochettes, coconut rice, and the rum-heavy local drink known as toaka gasy.

The single most compelling excursion from Toamasina is the Ivoloina Wildlife Park, about 12 kilometres north of the centre. Set within a fragment of coastal rainforest beside a river, it is run by the Madagascar Fauna and Flora Group, a consortium of zoos and universities that has been breeding and releasing endangered lemurs since the 1990s. Thirteen species are present, including black-and-white ruffed lemurs that range freely in the surrounding trees. A visit makes a strong argument for why the east coast of Madagascar matters beyond its port.

For travellers crossing Madagascar on the legendary Fianarantsoa–Côte Est railway, Toamasina is the natural end point: a city to breathe in for a day or two before heading north towards the canal country of Pangalanes or south to Île Sainte-Marie.
