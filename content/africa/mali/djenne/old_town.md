The old town of Djenné is one of the great surviving examples of pre-colonial West African urbanism. Inscribed by UNESCO in 1988 along with nearby Djenné-Djenno, the dense network of two-storey mud-brick houses, narrow lanes, and small courtyards has been continuously inhabited for at least eight hundred years. Walking the streets is the main experience of Djenné — there is no specific itinerary, just an endless unfolding of doorways, facades, rooftops, and small open squares that connect them.

A local guide is essential to make sense of what you see. Many houses carry features that mark the origin or status of the family inside: Tukulor styling with engaged columns and corner horns, Moroccan-influenced facades with pointed arches and floral plasterwork, modest Bozo houses near the river. Children study the Quran on wooden tablets in small madrassas tucked between the houses. Climb to a rooftop terrace late in the afternoon for the moment when the mud walls warm to the colour of fire and the call to prayer rises across the town.
