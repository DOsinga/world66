The ruins of Koumbi Saleh represent the most significant archaeological discovery in Mauritania and quite possibly in all of West Africa. This was once the capital of the Ghana Empire — not the modern Republic of Ghana but the medieval Wagadou state that dominated the Sahel from roughly the 6th to the 13th century, controlling the gold and salt trade routes that linked the forests of the south to the markets of North Africa. At its zenith around 1000 CE, this was one of the largest cities on the continent.

What you see today is partial and requires imagination to complete. French excavations in the early 20th century uncovered substantial stone structures: the outline of a large mosque, residential buildings, and evidence of a sophisticated urban layout. The stone construction itself is remarkable given the region's building traditions — the wealth of the gold trade translated into durable architecture. Pottery, glass weights used for measuring gold, and imported goods point to a city deeply integrated into long-distance commerce. A small museum near the site displays some of the finds, though the most significant objects have migrated to the national museum in Nouakchott.

The Ghana Empire collapsed in the late 11th century after raids by the Almoravid confederation disrupted the trade networks that sustained it, and Koumbi Saleh faded from prominence. Its rediscovery gives a physical anchor to a chapter of West African history that often remains abstract. If you have any interest in pre-colonial African civilizations, this site is the real thing.
