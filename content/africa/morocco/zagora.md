At first sight Zagora is a dusty, single-street town. What saves it from being just another sun-bleached crossroads is its position: this is where the road from [Ouarzazate](/africa/morocco/ouarzazate) finally runs out of valley, and the Sahara begins. The famous painted sign — "Tombouctou, 52 jours" — once pointed from somewhere near here across an open desert that you could in fact cross by camel, and which still gives Zagora its identity as the threshold of the south.

The town itself is a strip of low pink concrete along Boulevard Mohammed V. The reason to come is everything around it: the palm groves of Amezrou across the river, where silversmiths still work in what used to be the Jewish Mellah; the Almoravid ruins on Jebel Zagora, the flat-topped hill behind the town; the green-glazed pottery and Sufi library at Tamegroute, twenty kilometres south; and the dunes — small and accessible at Tinfou, vast and remote at [Erg Chigaga](erg_chegaga) beyond M'Hamid.

Zagora is also a working agricultural place. Wednesday is market day, and Berbers and the few remaining nomads come in to trade dates, livestock and rugs. The dates are exceptional — the chewy black Mejhoul, the honey-coloured Boufeggous — and a vacuum-packed box is a fine thing to take home.

It can be hard work for the unprepared. Persistent children will want to guide you, sell you something, or both; in summer the heat is genuinely dangerous. Take it slowly, drink, and try not to lose your temper. The big yearly event is the Moussem of Moulay Abdelkader Jilali, a religious festival drawing pilgrims from across the Draa.
