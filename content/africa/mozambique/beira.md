Beira is Mozambique’s second city, sprawling across a flat peninsula where the Pungwe River meets the Indian Ocean. It is above all a port — and has been since the Portuguese founded it in 1891 as the main outlet for landlocked Central Africa. The Beira Corridor, a road and rail link running west to [Zimbabwe](/africa/zimbabwe), still carries freight from [Zimbabwe](/africa/zimbabwe), [Malawi](/africa/malawi), and [Zambia](/africa/zambia) to the sea.

The city wears its history visibly. The [Cathedral of Beira](/africa/mozambique/beira/cathedral) was built partly using stone from the sixteenth-century Portuguese fort of São Caetano at Sofala, erected between 1505 and 1512 — one of the earliest European structures in southern Africa. Sofala itself has long since been swallowed by the Pungwe estuary, but the stone lives on in Beira’s walls. The [railway station](/africa/mozambique/beira/railway_station), a late modernist masterwork from 1966, is the other architectural anchor of the city.

[Macuti Beach](/africa/mozambique/beira/macuti_beach), at the northern tip of the peninsula, is the most pleasant part of town. A Portuguese-era [lighthouse](/africa/mozambique/beira/macuti_lighthouse) stands at the point next to the wreck of a beached cargo ship, and the beach gets lively in the evenings. At the southern edge of town, the abandoned [Grande Hotel](/africa/mozambique/beira/grande_hotel) — once Africa's largest, now a vertical squatter settlement — has become one of the city's strangest landmarks. Beira is also a natural base for visiting [Gorongosa National Park](/africa/mozambique/gorongosa), one of Africa's great wildlife recoveries, roughly three hours to the northwest.
