  The black rhino nearly vanished from Etosha in the 1980s, reduced to fewer than 300 individuals by poaching. Namibia's response was one of the most effective wildlife law enforcement campaigns on the continent, backed by community conservancies that made local people partners in protection. By the 2020s, the population had recovered to over 1,800 — nearly 40% of the world's remaining black rhinos.
---

Etosha is one of the last places on earth where you have a genuine chance of seeing a black rhino in the wild, and the southern parts of the park near Okaukuejo and Rietfontein waterhole are the best areas. The animals are most active in the early morning and late afternoon, and at night at the floodlit waterholes.

Black rhinos differ from white rhinos in size, temperament, and habitat preference. They are smaller, noticeably more aggressive, and prefer dense bush to open grassland. Encounters at waterholes can be dramatic — a black rhino will often approach other animals with its head lowered and horn forward, causing lions and elephants alike to give way. Seeing one at close range is one of those experiences that makes the distance and expense of reaching Etosha feel entirely justified.
