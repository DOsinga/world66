story: "The Bilma salt trade was one of the great trans-Saharan commercial arteries. Caravans of thousands of camels — the azalai — would carry salt cones and natron north to Fezzan and south to the markets around Lake Chad, returning with millet and dates. The trade was already old when Arab geographers first documented it in the medieval period, and the Tuareg and Toubou peoples who controlled access to the oases derived considerable power from managing this exchange. Salt from Bilma was used not just for food but for preserving meat and treating livestock — it was not a luxury but a necessity across the central Sahara."

The salt flats at Bilma are one of those rare places where the present and a very distant past occupy the same space without contradiction. Salt is still extracted here by hand from shallow evaporation pans cut into the pale desert floor — the same basic technique used for centuries. The crusted white and ochre surface of the flats, broken into geometric pools, is striking on its own terms, and the modest scale of the operation makes it feel genuinely ancient rather than staged. Workers from the local Toubou community do most of the harvesting, shaping the salt into the distinctive cones and blocks that have been the region's signature export goods for as long as anyone has kept records.

You are free to walk around the outer edges of the working area, but ask before you wander into the middle of an active extraction. The people working here are not putting on a demonstration — this is a livelihood, and the quantities involved are small enough that every block matters. The best light is in the morning, when the low sun picks out the texture of the salt crust and the surrounding desert looks almost gentle. Don't expect signs or infrastructure; there are none. That is part of why it's worth seeing.
