  By 1996 the entire wild population of West African giraffes had been reduced to just 49 individuals, all of them in the Kouré area. Poaching and habitat loss had eliminated every other group across the region. A conservation programme involving local communities — who receive economic benefit from the giraffes' continued presence — helped the population recover to over 600 animals by the early 2020s. It is one of the few unambiguous conservation success stories in the Sahel.

The encounter itself is unlike most wildlife experiences. You walk into the acacia scrubland with your guide, and at some point — sooner than you expect — a giraffe is simply there, standing a few metres away, watching you with calm curiosity. The animals are habituated to human presence and do not bolt. You are close enough to hear them chew.

The West African giraffe (*Giraffa camelopardalis peralta*) is visually distinct from the more familiar East and Southern African subspecies: its patches are a lighter, more orange-tinted brown, and the patterning extends lower on the legs. Adults stand up to 5.5 metres tall.

A licensed guide from the village is mandatory and costs a fixed fee (confirm the current rate on arrival — it has varied between 5,000 and 10,000 CFA francs per vehicle historically). Tips are appreciated. The guides' income is directly tied to the giraffes' survival, which is part of why the conservation model has worked.
