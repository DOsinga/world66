Isandlwana is the site of the most dramatic military defeat in British colonial history. On a January morning in 1879, a Zulu force outflanked and overwhelmed a British camp at the base of a distinctive sphinx-shaped hill, killing over 1,300 imperial troops and their African allies. The battlefield has been preserved remarkably well — the white cairns marking mass graves of the fallen are still scattered across the hillside, and the landscape looks much as it did that day.

The site is best visited with a guide, preferably a Zulu guide who can explain the battle from both perspectives. The terrain is the key to understanding what happened: the British camp was spread too thin to defend, the Zulu commanders exploited every fold in the ground, and the timeline of the battle unfolded faster than anyone on either side expected.

An hour's drive away, Rorke's Drift tells the other half of the day's story — where just over a hundred British soldiers held off the same Zulu army for twelve hours, earning eleven Victoria Crosses in a single action. The combination of the two sites makes for one of the most compelling history days in South Africa.
