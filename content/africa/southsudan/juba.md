Juba is the capital and largest city of South Sudan, the world's youngest country, which split from Sudan in 2011 after decades of civil war. It sits on the west bank of the White Nile in the far south of the country, close to the borders with Uganda, Kenya and the DR Congo — a raw, hot, sprawling frontier town that became a national capital almost overnight.

The city boomed and then buckled. Independence and oil money drew NGOs, contractors and traders, and for a while Juba was one of the most expensive cities in the world for foreigners, with hotel rooms and bottled water at prices that shocked new arrivals. The renewed civil war from 2013 reversed much of that, and the city still carries the marks of conflict, displacement and a fragile economy. Infrastructure is minimal: power comes from generators, most roads are unpaved, and the rainy season turns large parts of town to mud.

For the traveller, Juba is a base rather than a sight. Most foreigners here are working — journalists, aid workers, researchers — and independent tourism barely exists. The riverfront, the lively central market and a cold drink watching the Nile slide past are the simple pleasures. Anyone visiting should check their government's travel advice carefully, arrange trusted local contacts in advance, carry US dollars in small denominations, and keep a low profile, especially after dark.

This is travel at the edge of the map: difficult, expensive and not to be taken lightly. But for those drawn to the newest nation on earth, Juba is the only practical way in.
