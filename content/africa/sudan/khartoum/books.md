title: Books
type: section
---

A handful of books help make sense of Sudan, its capital, and the long arc of Mahdist, colonial, and independent history.

**Season of Migration to the North** by Tayeb Salih. A short, devastating novel about a Sudanese man returning home from London, set partly in a village on the Nile north of Khartoum. Widely considered the most important Arabic novel of the 20th century and the best introduction to Sudanese sensibility.

**The River War** by Winston Churchill. Churchill served as a young cavalry officer in Kitchener's 1898 reconquest of Sudan and produced a long, vivid account of the Mahdist war that culminated in the Battle of Omdurman. The prose is grand and the imperial perspective unflinching — a window into Victorian Britain as much as into Sudan.

**Khartoum** by Michael Asher. A fast-paced account of the 1884-1885 siege and General Gordon's death, drawing heavily on Mahdist sources to balance the British version. The best single book for a traveller standing on the Republican Palace steps.

**God's Spies** by Stephen Talty. A non-fiction reconstruction of the Mahdist uprising told largely through the eyes of the captives held in Omdurman, including the Austrian missionary Joseph Ohrwalder. Reads like a thriller.

**Travels to Discover the Source of the Nile** by James Bruce. The 18th-century Scotsman's swaggering account of his journey to find the Blue Nile's source. Bruce passed through what is now Khartoum a century before the modern city existed and his descriptions of the region remain a great period piece.
