  In June 1915, a British force of around 1,500 troops under Brigadier-General
  James Stewart landed near Bukoba and fought its way into the town, destroying
  the German wireless station and stores before withdrawing. It was the first
  significant Entente victory against German forces in Africa, and the news was
  welcomed in London at a time when much else was going wrong. The German garrison
  held the position for three years more, but the wireless tower — which had been
  relaying signals across German East Africa — was never rebuilt.

Bukoba was a colonial administrative centre for German East Africa from the 1890s, and a handful of buildings from that era survive near the lakeshore. The most visible are the old boma (administrative fort) and related structures close to the Lake Hotel — solid, functional German colonial architecture built to project permanence in a place that the Kaiser's government intended to keep. The scale is modest compared to Mwanza or Dar es Salaam, but the buildings are well-preserved and the lakeside setting makes them worth seeking out.

The broader context is the First World War campaign in East Africa, which touched Bukoba directly. The town sat at the north-western corner of a vast territory that the German commander Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck defended with a tiny force for the entire duration of the war, tying down Allied troops until after the Armistice in Europe. Walking the lakeshore around the old colonial quarter, it is easy to see why the position mattered: the lake was a highway, and whoever controlled the harbour controlled communications and supply lines across the western shore of Victoria.
