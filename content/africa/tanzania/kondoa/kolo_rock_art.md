The Kondoa rock art sites, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2006, are among the most significant concentrations of prehistoric rock paintings in Africa. Hundreds of sheltered granite overhangs across the Irangi Hills hide images in red, orange, and white pigment: antelope, giraffe, elephant, stylised human figures, and mysterious geometric forms that no one has fully decoded. The oldest paintings are estimated at 20,000 to 30,000 years old, though most visible images are considerably more recent.

The access point is the village of Kolo, roughly 30 km north of Kondoa along the main road to Babati. Here the Department of Antiquities office issues the compulsory permit (a small fee) and assigns a government guide, who is essential — the sites are scattered and unmarked, many in dense bush on private farmland. The standard tour visits three or four overhangs within walking distance of Kolo; a longer excursion can take in sites further into the hills. A full day is worth it.

The paintings are best viewed in the early morning or late afternoon when low-angle light intensifies the colours. The most dramatic panel shows a procession of elongated human figures, arms raised, that archaeologists associate with trance-dance rituals still traceable in Sandawe oral tradition. Even those who visit purely for the landscape — the rolling acacia-dotted hills, red soil, and sudden silence — come away with a strong sense of deep time.
