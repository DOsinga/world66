The Sandawe people of the Kondoa district are one of the most historically remarkable communities in Africa. Linguistically distinct from their Bantu-speaking neighbours, they speak a click language that connects them to the San (Bushman) peoples of the Kalahari — separated by thousands of kilometres and thousands of years, but sharing a linguistic ancestry that predates most of the migration patterns that shaped modern Africa.

Today the Sandawe number around 60,000, most living in villages in the hills west and south of Kondoa. They have largely adopted agriculture and Islam alongside their older practices, but traditional ceremonies — including trance-dance rituals closely related to the imagery in the rock art — are still performed in modified form. The rock paintings attributed to Sandawe ancestors are the most visible legacy of their long presence in this landscape.

Visitors interested in Sandawe culture should engage through the Department of Antiquities at Kolo, whose guides can provide context and, in some cases, arrange visits to villages where elders are willing to discuss the old ways. It is worth approaching this with patience and respect — the Sandawe have been studied by outside researchers for decades and can be understandably reserved with casual tourists who treat them as an exhibit.
