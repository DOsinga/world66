  The Kabyé people are the second-largest ethnic group in Togo and were historically displaced from their hillside villages by German and French colonial authorities who used them as a source of forced labour in the south. Many returned after independence, and the villages on and around Mont Kabyé have maintained traditional practices — including initiation rituals and terraced farming methods — that survived the colonial disruption.
---

The hills west of Kara, often loosely called Mont Kabyé, form a landscape of terraced farmland, rocky outcrops, and traditional Kabyé villages. Hiking through them takes you past compounds where daily life has changed little in the essentials — millet is still ground by hand, yams are harvested by extended families working together, and elders oversee the same agricultural rhythms that have shaped the landscape over centuries.

The terrain is not dramatic by Alpine standards, but the combination of the hills, the worked landscape, and the villages makes for rewarding walking. You will need a local guide — not least because the paths are unmarked and the villages rarely see independent travellers. Kara's hotels can arrange guides, or you can ask at the prefecture office in town. The best time to go is early morning before the heat builds.
