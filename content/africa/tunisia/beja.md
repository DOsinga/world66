Béja sits in the green, rolling hills of northwestern Tunisia, about 100 km west of Tunis. This is the breadbasket of the country — wheat fields, olive groves, and grazing land spreading out from the town in every direction, a striking contrast to the desert south. The Romans called the place **Vaga** and prized it as a grain depot; the Carthaginians, Numidians, Vandals, Byzantines, and finally the Arabs all in turn took the town for the same reason.

The town itself is a working provincial capital of about 60,000 people, not a polished tourist stop. A **[Byzantine kasbah](/africa/tunisia/beja/kasbah)** crowns the hill above the centre, looking out over the surrounding plains, and the **[Great Mosque](/africa/tunisia/beja/great_mosque)** in the old quarter dates back to the early Islamic period — built, like much of the kasbah, with stones quarried from the [Roman city](/africa/tunisia/beja/roman_remains) that preceded it. There are a few quiet cafés in the **[medina](/africa/tunisia/beja/medina)** and a busy weekly market that draws farmers from across the governorate.

Most travellers pass through Béja on the way to **[Bulla Regia](/africa/tunisia/bullaregia)**, the Roman site with its remarkable underground villas 50 km west, or **[Dougga](/africa/tunisia/dougga)**, the spectacular hilltop Roman city 50 km south. Béja makes a sensible, low-key base for visiting both, and the countryside between them — rolling hills, cork-oak forest, and Roman ruins half-buried in fields — is some of the prettiest in Tunisia. Do not expect tourist infrastructure: come for the landscape, the history, and a glimpse of ordinary Tunisian provincial life.
