story: >
  Vaga was important enough to Rome that, when Jugurtha massacred its Italian merchant community in 109 BCE during the Jugurthine War, the Romans considered it a major provocation — the historian Sallust treated the event as one of the catalysts of the eventual Roman victory. After the war the town was rebuilt as a Roman colony and became one of the main grain depots of North Africa.
---

Modern Béja sits directly on top of ancient Vaga, and as a result the Roman city itself is largely invisible — its stones long since reused in the kasbah, the mosques, and the medina houses. What remains is scattered: occasional carved stones built into walls, fragments of columns set up as ornaments, and an inscription or two visible to those who know where to look. The town is a study in how Roman North Africa survived not as ruins but as building material.

A small **archaeological collection** in the town displays what has been recovered locally — mosaics, statuary fragments, oil lamps — and helps put the surrounding countryside into context. If you are headed onward to **[Bulla Regia](/africa/tunisia/bullaregia)** or **[Dougga](/africa/tunisia/dougga)**, a stop here gives a sense of how dense Roman settlement was across this entire region: every town was once another Vaga.
