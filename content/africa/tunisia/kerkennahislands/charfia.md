  UNESCO inscribed the charfia fishing technique on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2020. The traps work on the principle that fish moving with the tide follow walls — palm-frond fences arranged in long V shapes funnel them toward a chamber from which they cannot easily escape. The fences have to be replaced every two or three years; a small island ecology of palm-frond suppliers, weavers, and fishermen has built up around them.

Walk along the shore of either island at low tide and you will see them — long V-shaped lines of palm fronds stuck into the seabed, running out into the shallow water for hundreds of metres. The charfia is the traditional fishing trap of the Kerkennah Islands, and it has been used here in essentially the same form since Roman times. Each weir belongs to a family or a small co-operative; the fishermen check them at low tide, walking out across the sand and shallow water to collect the catch.

The best way to see the charfia in action is to ask one of the local boatmen at Sidi Frej or Mellita to take you out for a morning. You can watch the fishermen at work, see the geometry of the trap from above, and often buy fresh fish on the spot. The system is fragile — overfishing, plastic pollution, and the rising cost of palm fronds all threaten it — but for now it remains the dominant way of fishing the shallow waters around the islands.
