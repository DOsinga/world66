The Ribat of Sousse is one of the oldest surviving Islamic military monuments in North Africa. Built around 796 CE under the Aghlabid dynasty and expanded over the following century, it served as both a coastal watchtower and a religious retreat — a place where warriors combined military readiness with religious observance. From its tower, sentinels once scanned the sea for Byzantine ships; now you climb the same stairs for panoramic views over the Medina rooftops and the harbour below.

The building is strikingly austere: thick walls, a central courtyard with arcaded galleries, cells for the warrior-monks along the upper level, and a prayer hall facing Mecca. It is exceptionally well preserved, partly because successive Islamic rulers repaired and expanded it rather than replacing it. The Ribat sits just beside the Great Mosque — the two buildings were conceived together as the spiritual-military core of early Islamic Sousse.
