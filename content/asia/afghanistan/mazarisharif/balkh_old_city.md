Twenty kilometres west of Mazar-i-Sharif, Balkh was for two thousand years one of the most important cities of Asia. The Greeks called it Bactra and made it the capital of their Bactrian kingdom; the Arabs called it Umm al-Bilad, the Mother of Cities; the Buddhists made it a centre of monastic learning that rivalled anywhere east of the Hindu Kush. Genghis Khan destroyed it in 1220 and although it was rebuilt, it never recovered its old importance — by the nineteenth century it had been almost completely abandoned in favour of nearby Mazar.

Today the ruins consist of an enormous ring of crumbling earthen walls, several Timurid-era shrines, a partially restored mosque (the Masjid-i Sabz, or Green Mosque), and the tomb of the female poet Rabia Balkhi. The remains of the No Gumbad mosque, possibly the oldest Islamic building in Afghanistan, sit a few kilometres away. The whole site has the strange, slightly elegiac quality of a great city reverted to fields.
