The Mazu Temple complex sprawls across the spine of Meizhou Island, a layered compound of halls, pavilions, and incense courtyards accumulated over ten centuries of reconstruction and expansion. The oldest surviving structure dates from the Song dynasty, though most of what you see was rebuilt in the 20th century. The atmospheric core is the main hall housing the principal statue — a gold-lacquered figure that is dressed in elaborate robes and crowned with a phoenix headdress, attended by incense smoke so thick it sometimes obscures the ceiling.

The annual birthday festival on the 23rd day of the third lunar month is the event that defines the island. Pilgrims arrive from Taiwan, southeast Asia, and across mainland China in numbers that far exceed the island's capacity, sleeping on boats and in improvised camps, performing religious rites through the night with drums, firecrackers, and ceremonial floats. On ordinary days the atmosphere is calmer but still genuine — the offerings are serious, the worshippers largely pilgrims rather than tourists, and the views from the upper terraces across the Taiwan Strait are quietly dramatic.
