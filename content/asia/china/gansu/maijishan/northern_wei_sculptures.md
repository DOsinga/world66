  The Northern Wei artists who carved and modelled the earliest Maijishan sculptures in
  the fifth century were working in a tradition that had arrived from India via Central
  Asia only recently. Their figures show the uncertainty and excitement of a style being
  invented in real time: the faces are elongated, the drapery stylised in ways that
  owe nothing to Chinese artistic tradition, the expressions caught between spiritual
  abstraction and something approaching portraiture. Nothing quite like them was made
  before, and nothing quite like them was made after.

The Northern Wei-period sculptures at Maijishan, dating to the late fourth and fifth centuries, are considered among the finest early examples of Buddhist art in China. Carved from the sandstone cliff face and built up in clay over wooden armatures, they display the characteristic Northern Wei style: elongated figures with serene, slightly archaic faces, rendered in an aesthetic that blends Indian Buddhist iconography with early Chinese artistic conventions.

Cave 133 is often cited as the most important single space at the site — a large grotto containing multiple figures and murals from several dynasties, but with Northern Wei work at its core. Access to this and other key caves is managed through the tiered ticketing system; the standard ticket covers the walkway views, while a premium ticket with a guide gives entry to the most significant interiors.
