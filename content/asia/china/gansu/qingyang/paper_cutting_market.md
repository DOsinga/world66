  Qingyang paper-cutting is a women's art form — historically passed from mother to
  daughter, the patterns memorised and cut freehand without drawing or templates.
  The imagery draws from a symbolic vocabulary that predates written Chinese culture:
  fish and frogs representing fertility, tigers for protection, the twelve animals
  of the zodiac rendered in variations that identify the hand of different village
  traditions. A master cutter can produce a complex twelve-centimetre design in
  under three minutes, without measuring, without marking, and without mistakes.
---

The markets and craft shops in Qingyang's old streets sell paper-cutting, embroidery, and cloth work that represents a living folk art tradition. The paper-cutting ranges from simple decorative pieces sold as souvenirs to elaborate compositions that take hours to make and cost accordingly. If you have any interest in folk arts, this is one of the best places in China to buy directly from the makers.

Several workshops in the city allow visitors to watch — and try — the technique. The tourist bureau and the municipal museum can direct you to master cutters who give demonstrations. The work is genuinely difficult: the cuts are made with small scissors into folded paper, and the patterns exist only in the maker's memory and hands. Watching someone do it fluently is impressive regardless of your interest in the finished product.
