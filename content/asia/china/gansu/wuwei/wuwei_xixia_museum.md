  The Western Xia (Xixia) kingdom developed its own script — one of the most complex
  ever devised, combining Chinese structural principles with Tibetan phonetic elements,
  and resembling Chinese writing without being remotely readable by Chinese speakers.
  The Tangut people who invented and used it were wiped out so thoroughly by the Mongols
  in the 1220s that the script was entirely forgotten for six centuries. When Western
  scholars began reconstructing it in the nineteenth century, they had to start from
  scratch. Much of what is now known about the Tangut language was worked out using
  bilingual Tangut-Chinese texts found at Wuwei.

The Wuwei Xixia Museum is the only museum in China outside Yinchuan dedicated specifically to Western Xia culture, the Tangut kingdom that controlled much of the northwest from the late tenth to early thirteenth century. The Western Xia left a distinctive material culture — its own script, its own Buddhist iconography, pottery styles that blend Chinese and Central Asian elements — and this free museum covers it intelligently and in depth.

The collection includes Buddhist sculptures and texts in the Tangut script, decorative ceramics, and artefacts that document the kingdom's court culture and military organisation. The Western Xia state was militarily powerful enough to hold off both the Song Dynasty and Genghis Khan for decades before finally falling to the Mongols in 1227. Its cultural legacy was deliberately suppressed, making what survives at Wuwei and Yinchuan unusually precious. Admission is free.
