  Mati means "horse's hoof" in Chinese, and the name refers to a legend that a heavenly horse
  landed on the cliff here, leaving its hoofprint in the rock. The grottoes were first carved
  during the Northern Liang dynasty in the fifth century and were used continuously by Buddhist
  monks for over a thousand years. At various points in their history they were also used by
  Tibetan Buddhist communities, and the mix of Han and Tibetan iconography inside reflects that
  layered history.

Mati Si is a complex of cave temples carved into a sheer cliff face south of Zhangye, in the Qilian Mountains foothills at about 2,000 metres altitude. The grottoes are connected by tight vertical passages cut through the rock, with ladders and narrow tunnels linking chambers stacked up the cliff at several levels. The interior spaces contain Buddhist statues, painted murals, and carvings in various states of preservation, some dating to the fifth century.

The surrounding valley is also beautiful — a green gorge with the monastery and grottoes appearing almost as an afterthought against the scale of the cliffs. The journey from Zhangye takes about an hour and a half by bus or taxi; a visit works well as a half-day excursion, combining with the grassland scenery of the Qilian foothills on the way. The complex can be cold even in summer — bring a layer.
