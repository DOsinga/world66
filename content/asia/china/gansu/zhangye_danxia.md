The Zhangye Danxia National Geological Park contains the most visually disorienting landscape in China, which is saying something in a country not short of geological theatre. Forty kilometres west of Zhangye, erosion has cut through millennia of layered sedimentary deposits to expose horizontal bands of red, orange, yellow, grey, green, and white — colours that look applied rather than natural, as if someone has cross-sectioned a stack of coloured paper. The Chinese name translates roughly as "seven-colour danxia," and while the count is approximate, the description is not.

The geological story begins about two hundred million years ago, when this area was a basin collecting sediment from the surrounding mountains. Different minerals — iron oxides for the reds and oranges, chlorite for the greens, gypsum for the whites — precipitated in different layers as conditions changed. The Qilian Mountains then rose, tilting and exposing the whole sequence, and water cut the current landscape over the past ten million years or so. The result is hills that look painted from a distance and, up close, reveal grain and texture that confirms they are unmistakably real rock.

The park has four main viewing areas connected by shuttle buses. The most dramatic views come from [Platforms 3 and 4](/asia/china/gansu/zhangye_danxia/viewing_platforms), where the ridgelines catch afternoon light best, typically between 4 and 6 in the evening. [Sunrise light on Platform 1](/asia/china/gansu/zhangye_danxia/sunrise_platform) is also widely photographed. The park is busiest on summer weekends; weekday visits in May or September offer more room to move.

The site is purely for looking at: the formations are roped off and the paths keep you on elevated walkways. [Zhangye](/asia/china/gansu/zhangye) city, forty kilometres east, is the logical base, with accommodation across all price ranges and a high-speed rail connection to Lanzhou.
