Cangzhou has two things it is genuinely famous for across China, and both are worth taking seriously. The Iron Lion, cast during the Five Dynasties period around 953 CE and standing nearly six metres tall, is one of the oldest and largest iron castings in Chinese history. The technical achievement of making it is considerable — the casting method required pouring molten iron in sections and fusing them, a process complex enough that the lion shows no seams, or at least very few. It has been standing in the open air for over a thousand years, developing a thick rust patina that gives it a geological presence. The bas-relief inscription across its abdomen includes the founding text of the Diamond Sutra.

The martial arts claim is backed by genuine history. Cangzhou has been producing martial artists since at least the Ming dynasty, when the strategic location along the Grand Canal made the city a hub for soldiers, bodyguards, and caravan escorts. Over fifty distinct styles of Chinese martial arts either originated here or have particularly strong traditions in the region, including forms of the Iron Sand Palm training that was taken up across northern China. The city's acrobatics tradition is related — many of the physical disciplines practiced here developed alongside the combat arts rather than separately from them.

The Grand Canal passes through Cangzhou on its route between Beijing and Hangzhou. The section here has been developed as a heritage corridor with restored towpaths and period architecture, offering a different perspective on the system that connected northern China to the Yangtze delta for over a thousand years. The canal is still partially active for freight.

Cangzhou is an easy day trip from Beijing or Tianjin and sits on the main rail line south toward Jinan.
