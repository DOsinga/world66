The Tangshan Earthquake Memorial Museum and associated ruins park in the northern part of the city are dedicated to the earthquake of 28 July 1976, which struck at 3:42 in the morning and killed somewhere between 242,000 and 300,000 people — the deadliest earthquake of the twentieth century. The museum, opened in 2009, treats the subject with appropriate gravity and considerable detail.

The exhibition covers the seismology of the event, the immediate disaster response, the reconstruction of the city, and the broader history of earthquake preparedness in China. The photographs and personal accounts are harrowing. What the museum does not shy away from is the political context: the Cultural Revolution-era government initially refused international aid, suppressed information about the death toll, and framed the disaster response as a triumph of socialist self-reliance even as hundreds of thousands of people were dying in the ruins.

The ruins park preserves sections of destroyed buildings as they were found — collapsed walls, twisted structures, rubble — within a formal memorial landscape with walls bearing the names of those killed. The combination of ruins and inscription is powerful and quiet. The monument square in the city centre has a separate anti-seismic monument and is a common gathering place.

Entry to the museum is free.
