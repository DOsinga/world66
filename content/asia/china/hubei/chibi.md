Chibi — "Red Cliffs" — takes its name from the rocky bluffs on the Yangtze where one of the decisive battles of Chinese history was fought in the winter of 208–209 CE. Cao Cao, the chancellor of the declining Han dynasty, had unified the north and moved south with a fleet estimated at hundreds of thousands of men. The combined forces of Sun Quan and Liu Bei, vastly outnumbered, used fire ships driven by a favourable wind to destroy the northern fleet. The defeat halted the reunification of China for sixty years and established the three-way division — Wei, Shu, Han, Eastern Wu — that the Three Kingdoms period is named for. The battle has had an extraordinary afterlife in Chinese culture, appearing in the fourteenth-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms and in John Woo's 2008 film Red Cliff, among hundreds of other adaptations.

The [Red Cliffs scenic area](/asia/china/hubei/chibi/red_cliffs_scenic_area) is a series of riverside bluffs bearing inscriptions and pavilions, with a museum that covers the battle's military tactics and historical context. The ochre-coloured cliffs dropping to the Yangtze are genuinely atmospheric, particularly in the morning mist. There is ongoing scholarly dispute about whether this is actually the battle site — Huanggang, upstream, also makes a claim — but Chibi has had the name officially since 1998, when the city was renamed from Puqi specifically to capitalise on the association.

The city sits in the Xianning prefecture in southeastern Hubei, near the border with Hunan, on the main railway and road corridor between Wuhan and Changsha. It is small by Chinese standards — fewer than half a million people — and moves at a pace noticeably slower than the provincial capital. The surrounding countryside grows significant quantities of tea; the [Yangloudong](/asia/china/hubei/chibi/yangloudong_tea) area in the southwest has been an important tea production and trading centre for centuries, and the local tea culture is a quieter reason to linger than the battle site alone.

Chibi is two hours by road south of Wuhan and sits on the high-speed rail line, making it a feasible day trip. The riverside location and the battle story are the main draws; combine it with the hot springs and forest parks of the broader Xianning area if you are spending a night.
