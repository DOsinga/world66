  The Battle of Red Cliffs in 208–209 CE is one of Chinese history's most consequential upsets: Cao Cao, commanding a northern fleet estimated at hundreds of thousands of men, was destroyed by fire ships driven by a timely southeasterly wind against the combined forces of Sun Quan and Liu Bei. The defeat halted the reunification of China under one ruler for sixty years and gave rise to the Three Kingdoms period that has fascinated Chinese writers and readers ever since. Whether these specific cliffs were the battle site is disputed by historians — Huanggang, upstream, also claims the honour — but Chibi has held the official designation since 1998.

The Red Cliffs scenic area is a section of ochre-red riverine bluffs above the Yangtze, carved with large inscriptions and topped with pavilions that overlook the broad river. The cliffs themselves are genuinely atmospheric, particularly in the early morning mist when the Yangtze is slow and wide below and the rust-coloured rock faces pick up the flat light. The largest inscription, "Red Cliffs," is said to have been cut by the Zhou Yu — the Eastern Wu commander who devised the fire-ship tactic — though the historical provenance of this claim is disputed.

The museum at the scenic area covers the battle's military tactics and historical context with maps, models, and artefact displays that make the strategy reasonably clear even without specialist knowledge. The park also includes a reconstruction of the naval encampment used in the battle, which is more educational than dramatic. The site as a whole takes about two hours; it is most rewarding if you have some context from reading about the Three Kingdoms period beforehand, since the landscape alone, without the story, is pleasant but not exceptional.
