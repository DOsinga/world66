  Su Dongpo's First Red Cliff Rhapsody, written in 1082, contains one of the most famous passages in Chinese prose: "The water and the moon — how are they like this? The former passes on, and yet is never gone; the latter waxes and wanes, and yet in the end neither increases nor diminishes." The question is ostensibly about the river and the moon visible from this spot; the actual subject is how to live with loss and impermanence. The rhapsody was composed on a boat, floating past the cliff in the dark.
---

The rocky Yangtze outcrop at Huangzhou that Su Dongpo associated with the Battle of Red Cliffs — and used as the occasion for his two great prose poems — is preserved as a small scenic spot near the old city embankment. The cliffs themselves are modest by scenic standards: a reddish-brown rock face above the river, inscribed with calligraphy and fitted with a pavilion. What matters is not the visual impact but the literary connection: this is where one of the greatest writers in Chinese history sat, exiled and broke, in the autumn of 1082, and produced a meditation on time, loss, and the consolation of the natural world that has been read by every educated Chinese person for nine hundred years.

The site is peaceful and uncrowded compared to its literary significance, which either reflects how few tourists read classical poetry or how completely the Chibi name transfer has redirected attention downstream. Either way, it is a genuinely quiet spot on the Yangtze embankment, and if you have read either of the Red Cliff Rhapsodies before visiting — which is easy: both are short and available in translation — the experience is considerably heightened.
