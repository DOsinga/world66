story: >
  The village was designed according to a principle the Chinese call "a house within a house" — concentric layers of defence and privacy moving from the outer wall to the clan hall at the centre. The covered internal streets were built wide enough for two sedan chairs to pass, which was the measure of social distinction in Qing-dynasty residential planning. A family's position within the clan hierarchy could be read from which covered street their door opened onto.
---

The Zhangguying Village complex covers 50,000 square metres and contains 1,732 rooms in a continuous warren of interconnected buildings, courtyards, and covered streets. It was founded by Zhang Guying, a Ming-dynasty official, and expanded over successive generations into a complete clan settlement. The outer walls are whitewashed, the rooflines step in the characteristic Hunan and Jiangxi pattern of rising and falling gables, and the internal lanes are stone-flagged and so narrow that no vehicle can enter.

Walking the complex is a sustained architectural experience: carved wooden screens in the main hall, stone lintels with bas-relief decorations, a drum tower for signalling, wells shared between family groups, and small shrines in various states of active use. The site's designation as a National Protected Cultural Unit (2001) and Famous Historical and Cultural Village (2003) is well-deserved — comparable ensembles elsewhere in China have lost critical elements; Zhangguying's structural completeness is genuinely unusual. Admission costs around ¥40.
