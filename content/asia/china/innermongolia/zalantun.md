Zalantun is a small city in the southeastern foothills of the Greater Khingan Mountains, in a corner of Inner Mongolia that most visitors never reach. Known locally as the "Little Jiangnan of Saibei" — Jiangnan being the lush, waterlogged region around Suzhou and Hangzhou — the nickname points at something genuine: Zalantun is greener, wetter, and more forested than anywhere in the grassland heartland to the west. Rivers cut through wooded gorges, fishing is good, and the landscape has a layered beauty that is entirely different from the open steppe.

The Greater Khingan range rises behind the city, and the forests that cover its slopes are among the largest remaining old-growth taiga in China. Hiking trails push into the hills from several points accessible by taxi from the city; the [Pobuqi Gorge](/asia/china/innermongolia/zalantun/pobuqi_gorge) sections are particularly good in autumn, when the birch and aspen turn gold and the rivers run full. The [Yalu River](/asia/china/innermongolia/zalantun/yalu_river) (a different Yalu from the one on the Korean border) flows through the district and is the basis for fishing trips and slow boat excursions. The surrounding land belongs largely to Daur, Oroqen, and Evenki communities, whose hunting and fishing cultures have shaped the landscape for centuries.

The city of Zalantun itself is modest — administrative buildings, a main street, the usual Chinese urban furniture — but the area around it is the draw. [Hot spring resorts](/asia/china/innermongolia/zalantun/wunuerzhen_hot_springs) have appeared in the surrounding hills, offering comfortable bases for exploring the forests without committing to camping. The local food is influenced by the hunting traditions of the region: wild mushroom dishes, freshwater fish, and venison appear on menus more often than the lamb-based cuisine dominant further west in Inner Mongolia.

Zalantun has a small airport — Zhalantun Chengjisihan Airport — opened in 2016, and sits on the Harbin-Manzhouli Railway, making it accessible by train from both Harbin and Hailar. It is a natural stop for travellers crossing Inner Mongolia by rail who want to break the long journey with a day or two in the mountains. Summer is the peak season, though autumn is arguably better for scenery.
