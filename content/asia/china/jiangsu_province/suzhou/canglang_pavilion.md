He spent his own money to buy the site on the canal's edge, built his pavilion, and named it after lines from a folk song: "When the water of Canglang is clear, I can wash my hat-strings; when the water is muddy, I wash my feet." The implication was that a wise man adapts to the times rather than fighting them.

The Canglang Pavilion is the oldest of Suzhou's classical gardens, predating its famous neighbours by several centuries. Founded in 1044 during the Northern Song dynasty, it has a distinctive quality compared to the later Qing-era gardens: rougher, denser, and more naturalistic, with ancient trees casting deep shade over moss-covered earth. The mood is of a scholar's retreat rather than a pleasure garden.

The design makes ingenious use of a canal that runs along the garden's north edge. Rather than walling it out, the designers incorporated the water view through an open lattice fence — so the garden appears to extend beyond its actual boundaries. This trick of borrowed scenery is handled here with particular elegance. The pavilion at the top of the central hill is one of the oldest wooden structures in Suzhou.
