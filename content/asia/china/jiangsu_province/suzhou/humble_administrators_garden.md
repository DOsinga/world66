The Humble Administrator's Garden is the largest and most famous of Suzhou's classical gardens, and one of the four greatest gardens in all of China. Spread over four hectares, it is divided into three sections — east, central, and west — each with its own character. The central section is the most celebrated: a series of pavilions, covered walkways, and islands connected by zigzagging bridges, arranged around a large central lake so that water seems to be everywhere at once.

The design relies on a technique called "borrowed scenery," where views of structures outside the garden walls are incorporated into the composition as if they belong to it. Walk to the right angle in the right pavilion and the Northern Pagoda of the nearby temple appears perfectly framed in an arched window, though it stands well beyond the garden boundary. This kind of spatial trickery, repeated throughout the grounds, is what makes Chinese classical gardens so difficult to photograph and so rewarding to walk through slowly.

The garden was created in the early 16th century by a retired imperial censor who had the grounds laid out with help from the painter Wen Zhengming, who also painted a series of pictures of the completed garden. His sketches survive and are displayed in the museum on the property. The garden changed hands many times over the following centuries and was partially redesigned with each new owner; the current layout dates primarily from the 19th century.
