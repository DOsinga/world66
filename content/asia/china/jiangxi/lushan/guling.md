  Guling was developed in the late nineteenth century after the British missionary Edward Little discovered the mountain in 1885 and recognised its potential as a summer retreat from the Yangtze valley heat. He negotiated a ninety-nine-year lease on land and sold plots to foreign missionaries, diplomats, and businessmen. By the 1920s there were over 500 foreign villas on Lushan, and the mountain hosted the summer sessions of several Protestant missions. The leases were never renewed after 1949, but the stone buildings remain.

Guling (牯岭) is the main settlement on Lushan — a small town at around 1,000 metres elevation built primarily during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as a foreign summer retreat. The colonial-era stone villas that line the streets are now mostly guesthouses, restaurants, and administrative buildings, but the architecture is intact and the street pattern unchanged. Walking through Guling feels genuinely different from any other Chinese mountain town.

The town has a central street with shops, restaurants, and a small square, and the surrounding lanes of old villas are worth wandering without a specific destination. Many of the former missionary residences have simple name plaques identifying their original owners. The climate at this altitude is genuinely cool in summer — averaging around 22°C at the hottest — which explains both the original appeal and the continued summer popularity.
