  Zhu Da (朱耷, 1626–1705), who painted under the name Bada Shanren (八大山人), was a descendant of the Ming imperial family who watched his dynasty collapse when he was nineteen. He became a Buddhist monk, then a Taoist, and eventually channeled his grief and rage into some of the most distinctive ink paintings in Chinese art history — fish with defiant upturned eyes, rocks leaning at impossible angles, birds perched on thin branches, all painted with a ferocious economy of line. He may also have feigned madness for decades to survive under the Qing.
---

The Bada Shanren Memorial Museum (八大山人纪念馆) occupies the Qingyunpu Taoist complex in southern Nanchang, where the painter Zhu Da lived and worked during the late Ming and early Qing dynasties. The monastery's traditional architecture — courtyards, covered walkways, lotus ponds, ancient plum trees — has been carefully restored and provides an appropriately contemplative setting for the paintings on display.

The collection includes original works and high-quality reproductions of Bada Shanren's most significant paintings. Even visitors unfamiliar with Chinese ink painting tend to find the work arresting: the compressed, spare compositions convey an emotional charge that needs no cultural context to feel. The museum is one of the genuine cultural highlights of Nanchang, and one of the least crowded. Take metro Line 1 to Qingyunpu station.
