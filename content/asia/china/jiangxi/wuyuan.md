Wuyuan (婺源) is the most photographed corner of Jiangxi — a cluster of ancient [Huizhou-style villages](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/huizhou_architecture) with whitewashed walls, grey tile roofs, and carved wooden interiors, set among tea fields and forested hills. In spring, [rapeseed fields](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/rapeseed_fields) turn the valley floors a violent yellow, and the combination of blossoms and old architecture draws enormous crowds of Chinese photographers and tour groups. If you're going in March or April, accept the crowds or don't go.

The villages are scattered across a fairly wide area rather than concentrated in one place. [Likeng](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/likeng_village) has a canal running through it and is popular enough to have souvenir shops clogging every alley, but the houses themselves are genuine Song-to-Qing dynasty merchant architecture. [Sixi Yancun](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/sixi_yancun) offers a quieter version of the same, with maze-like alleys that actually feel like a living village rather than a theme park. [Xiaoqi](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/xiaoqi_village) is better still — the upper hamlet especially has rice paddies, fewer visitors, and the unhurried atmosphere that is increasingly hard to find in the lower valley. [Wangkou](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/wangkou_village) has the finest ancestral hall in the region.

The [Wuyuan green tea](/asia/china/jiangxi/wuyuan/wuyuan_green_tea) is legitimately good and sold everywhere at a range of prices. The local dish worth ordering is the steamed pumpkin with rice flour. Bus connections from [Jingdezhen](/asia/china/jiangxi/jingdezhen) are straightforward (about 2 hours), and high-speed rail links to Shanghai, Hangzhou, and other major cities make the region easy to reach. Very little English is spoken; a translation app will carry most of the weight.
