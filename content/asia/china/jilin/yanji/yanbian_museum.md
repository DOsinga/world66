  The mass migration of Koreans into what is now Yanbian was one of the largest population movements in nineteenth-century Asia. Famine in Korea's northern provinces in the 1860s and 1870s drove the first waves across the Tumen River, followed by larger flows after Japan's annexation of Korea in 1910 removed political reason to stay. The community that formed here survived Japanese occupation of Manchuria, the Korean War fought just across the border, and the Cultural Revolution's assault on minority culture — and still came through speaking their own language and cooking their own food.
---

The Yanbian Museum is the essential stop for understanding how this bicultural city came to be. Its ten thousand-odd exhibits document the history of the ethnic Korean community in Yanbian from the first migrations across the Tumen River in the nineteenth century through to the present — a story of displacement, survival, and cultural tenacity that national Chinese museums rarely cover with this level of care.

The museum's handling of the community's origins is particularly good: the nineteenth-century migration patterns, the formation of farming settlements, the shock of Japanese occupation, and the complicated wartime years when the community was caught between Japanese Manchuria and the Korean peninsula. Labelling is in both Korean and Chinese, which reflects the bilingual character of the city itself. Allow two hours for a proper visit; the collection is extensive and the historical context rewards patience.
