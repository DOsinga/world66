Liaoyang is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the northeast, settled for over two thousand four hundred years and serving at different points as a major administrative centre for dynasties ranging from the Han to the Qing. It was, briefly, the Manchu capital: Nurhachi transferred his court here in 1621, calling it Dongjing — the Eastern Capital — before relocating to Shenyang four years later. The city has been quietly accumulating history ever since without particularly announcing itself to the outside world.

The [White Pagoda](/asia/china/liaoning/liaoyang/white_pagoda) is the landmark. Rising seventy metres above White Pagoda Park, it was built during the Liao Dynasty and is the tallest ancient pagoda in China, a thirteen-tiered octagonal structure whose proportions are visible from most of the city. The park around it is pleasant without being remarkable, but the tower itself is worth the trip — seen at the right angle in the right light it's an unexpectedly striking piece of architecture. The [Guangyou Temple](/asia/china/liaoning/liaoyang/guangyou_temple) complex nearby claims origins in the Eastern Han Dynasty, though what survives is mostly later rebuilding, and houses a small museum of Buddhist artefacts.

The [Dongjing City site](/asia/china/liaoning/liaoyang/dongjing_city_site) preserves the footprint of the Manchu palace complex from Liaoyang's brief tenure as imperial capital — low earthworks and foundation remains across three-quarters of a square kilometre that reward visitors with an interest in early Qing history. The [Liaoyang City Museum](/asia/china/liaoning/liaoyang/liaoyang_city_museum) collects four thousand artefacts from the neolithic through the Qing period, providing context for the layers visible across the city.

Liaoyang is about forty kilometres south of Shenyang on the high-speed line, a twenty-minute ride that makes it a perfectly natural half-day excursion. It lacks the visitor infrastructure of Shenyang and Dalian, which is also part of its appeal: the streets around the White Pagoda are ordinary city streets, and the sites are un-touristed enough to feel like genuine discoveries.
