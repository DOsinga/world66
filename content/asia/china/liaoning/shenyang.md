Shenyang is the largest city in northeast China — a sprawling industrial metropolis that most travelers skip on the way to Beijing or Harbin. That's a mistake. Beneath the Soviet-era blocks and factory districts lies a city with an unexpected historical weight: this was the capital of the Qing dynasty before Beijing, and it still carries two UNESCO World Heritage Sites to prove it.

The city sits on the north bank of the Hun River, in the heart of Liaoning Province. It has been inhabited for over 2,300 years. But its defining moment came in the 17th century, when the Manchu leader Nurhaci moved his capital here in 1625, renaming the city Shengjing. His son went on to found the Qing dynasty in 1636 — the last imperial dynasty of China, which would rule for nearly three hundred years. When the Qing armies swept south and took Beijing in 1644, Shenyang became the secondary capital, a role it retained until the dynasty fell in 1912.

That history is still visible. The [Shenyang Imperial Palace](/asia/china/liaoning/shenyang/shenyang_imperial_palace) here predates the better-known Forbidden City additions, and the royal tombs — [Zhaoling](/asia/china/liaoning/shenyang/zhaoling_tomb) to the north and [Fuling](/asia/china/liaoning/shenyang/fuling_tomb) to the east — are among the finest Manchu-era funerary monuments in China. The [Zhang Xueliang Mansion](/asia/china/liaoning/shenyang/zhang_xueliang_mansion) and the [September 18th History Museum](/asia/china/liaoning/shenyang/nine_eighteen_museum) fill in the tragic twentieth century, when Manchuria was occupied and its people fought back. Outside the heritage sites, Shenyang is a working city — traffic-heavy, fast-moving, with good dongbei food and a straightforwardness that can be refreshing after the tourist circuits of Beijing and Xi'an.
