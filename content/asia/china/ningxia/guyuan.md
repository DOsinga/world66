Guyuan is the largest city in the southern half of Ningxia, set in the dry terraced hills of the Loess Plateau near the border with Gansu. It is less visited than Yinchuan or Zhongwei, but the surrounding area contains some of the most historically significant Buddhist art in the northwest: the [Xumi Mountain Grottoes](/asia/china/ningxia/guyuan/xumi_mountain_grottoes), carved into the sandstone flanks of Sumeru Shan about 55 kilometres to the northwest.

The Xumi grottoes comprise more than 130 chambers cut into the eastern face of the mountain, with Buddhist sculptures ranging from the Northern Wei period through the Tang dynasty — roughly the 5th to 10th centuries. The largest figure is a seated Buddha over 20 metres tall; the finest works are a set of Tang-era bodhisattva sculptures of considerable delicacy. The site is far less trafficked than the more famous cave complexes at Dunhuang or Longmen, which means you can often have entire grottoes to yourself.

Back in the city, the [Guyuan Museum](/asia/china/ningxia/guyuan/guyuan_museum) on Xicheng Road organises its collection chronologically from the Stone Age through the Silk Road period to the Qing dynasty, with particular strength in bronze vessels and Tang-period artefacts from local tombs. Admission is free. South of the city, [Liupan Mountain Forest Park](/asia/china/ningxia/guyuan/liupan_mountain_forest_park) offers pine-covered hills that make a striking contrast to the bare loess terrain lower down.

Guyuan is accessible by train from Yinchuan in about six hours and from Xi'an in eight, though the most useful connections are by bus — services to Yinchuan run frequently from around 7am to 6pm and take about four hours. The city's own character is defined by its large Hui Muslim population; as throughout Ningxia, halal restaurants serving hand-pulled noodles and lamb dishes are the backbone of everyday eating.
