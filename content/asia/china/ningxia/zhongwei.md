Zhongwei sits at one of the more dramatic junctions in Chinese geography — the point where the [Tengger Desert](/asia/china/ningxia/zhongwei/tengger_desert), the Yellow River, and the terraced loess hills all converge within a few kilometres of each other. It is a small city that punches well above its size as a travel destination, largely because of [Shapotou](/asia/china/ningxia/zhongwei/shapotou), the desert resort on the southern bank of the Yellow River where enormous sand dunes drop almost directly into the water.

At Shapotou, the juxtaposition is genuinely striking: the river is wide and fast-moving, the desert behind it is vast and pale, and the oasis cultivation on the opposite bank is impossibly green. Activities there run the full gamut of Chinese tourist-resort excess — camel rides, sand skiing, hot air balloons, inflated sheepskin raft crossings, and Yellow River drifting. The entrance fee is steep and the crowds can be punishing in peak summer, but the landscape underneath the spectacle is real and impressive. Go early in the morning.

The town itself is worth a half-day for the [Gaomiao Temple](/asia/china/ningxia/zhongwei/gaomiao_temple), an extraordinary multi-storey complex that compresses Buddhist, Taoist, and Confucian iconography into one densely layered building. Its most distinctive feature is a chamber modelled on a Chinese vision of the afterlife — a walk through the stages of hell illustrated in considerable sculptural detail. Film buffs may recognise the exterior as the shooting location for Pai Mei's compound in Kill Bill Vol. 2.

Zhongwei has its own small airport at Shapotou, 9 kilometres northwest of the centre, connecting to Yinchuan and several larger Chinese cities. The train station on North Street puts the city about three hours from Yinchuan and six from Lanzhou, and bus services cover both routes as well. As a Muslim city, pork is scarce; noodles — hand-pulled and served with lamb — are the staple, as they are throughout Ningxia.
