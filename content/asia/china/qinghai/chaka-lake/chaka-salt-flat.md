  Chaka's reputation as the "sky mirror" of China spread largely through social media after 2015, when photographers began sharing images of tourists appearing to walk across the sky reflected in millimetres of brine. The lake had been worked for salt for over three thousand years before it became a photographic phenomenon, and the two purposes — industrial salt extraction and Instagram pilgrimage — coexist with an oddly photogenic awkwardness.
---

The Chaka salt flat is the defining feature of the lake — a pale white expanse of thick sodium chloride crust covered in a thin, still layer of brine that reflects the sky with mirror-like precision on calm days. In good conditions, the horizon disappears: sky and ground merge into one uninterrupted blue, with mountains floating seemingly in mid-air. Early morning in June or September, before any wind rises, gives the best chance of undisturbed reflections.

The flat is walkable across the salt crust, which is thick and solid enough to bear weight. Rental wellington boots are available at the entrance — recommended, since the brine is ankle-deep in places and strongly corrosive to regular footwear. Small narrow-gauge trolleys run along tracks across the flats, a slightly surreal transport option that gives a sense of the lake's scale. The surrounding mountains shift colour through the day, from pale pink at dawn to deep ochre in afternoon light, and the mirror doubles everything.
