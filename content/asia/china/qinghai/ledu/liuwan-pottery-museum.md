  The dance basin — a shallow pottery bowl painted with five human figures linked in a continuous ring dance, unearthed from the Liuwan burial site — became one of the most reproduced images in discussions of Chinese Neolithic art and prompted decades of academic debate about whether it represents a ritual, a seasonal celebration, or something else entirely. The figures are schematic but unmistakably alive, painted around 4,000 years ago.
---

The Liuwan Ancient Painted Pottery Museum holds one of the largest collections of Neolithic painted ceramics in the world, drawn from a major burial site excavated at Liuwan village in the 1970s. Over 37,000 ceramic vessels were recovered from more than 1,700 graves at the site, spanning the Majiayao and Qijia cultures (roughly 3300–1500 BC). The museum displays thousands of these objects across a 5,000-square-metre exhibition space.

The ceramics themselves are extraordinary: hand-built vessels with geometric patterns of extraordinary precision — spirals, wave forms, and lattices painted in ochre and black on burnished red slip — alongside figurative designs including animals and the famous human dance scenes. The workmanship argues for specialised potters and considerable trading networks across the upper Yellow River valley. For anyone interested in Chinese prehistory, or just in objects of genuine beauty made before writing, this museum is worth the trip from Xining. Entrance around 20 yuan; the collection is largely well-labelled in Chinese with some English signage.
