Shaanxi (not to be confused with neighbouring Shanxi to the northeast) was the cradle of Chinese civilisation. Thirteen imperial dynasties made the Wei River valley their capital — the Zhou, the Qin, the Han, the Tang, among them — and the province is saturated with their remains. [Xi'an](/asia/china/shaanxi/xian), the provincial capital, is one of the most historically dense cities in Asia, and the Terracotta Army buried outside it is one of those things that genuinely exceeds expectation.

[Xi'an](/asia/china/shaanxi/xian) stands at the eastern end of the Silk Road. For over a thousand years it was Chang'an, the largest city in the world, and the point from which caravans departed for Central Asia, the Middle East, and ultimately Rome. The city walls — built in the fourteenth century on Tang foundations — are the best-preserved in China, wide enough to cycle on top of. The Muslim Quarter around the Great Mosque is a narrow labyrinth of street food and craftsmen that has been trading for over a thousand years. The Shaanxi History Museum holds artefacts from all thirteen dynasties in collections that would be world-famous if they were anywhere else.

The Terracotta Army is forty-five minutes east by bus, and it is everything the postcards suggest and more. The scale is what you cannot prepare for — over eight thousand individual warriors, each with a distinct face. The excavation is ongoing; less than a third has been unearthed.

The rest of the province deserves more attention than it gets. [Mount Hua](/asia/china/shaanxi/hua_shan) (Huashan), east of Xi'an, has precipitous cliff paths that are among the most terrifying and rewarding hikes in China. The [Yan'an](/asia/china/shaanxi/yan_an) Pagoda in the north was the Communist Party's wartime capital; the cave dwellings where Mao and others lived and planned are still there.
