Beiwudang Mountain rises from Fangshan County in Lüliang City, on the western edge of Shanxi above the Yellow River valley. The name means "Northern Wudang," drawing a deliberate parallel to the famous Wudang Mountains in Hubei — both are centres of Taoist practice, both are known for dramatic peaks and cliff-hanging temples, and Beiwudang makes a reasonable claim to being the northern counterpart in terms of religious significance. The mountain is sometimes called "the first mountain in Shanxi" for its 72 peaks and dramatic vertical relief.

The Taoist presence here has been continuous for centuries, and the main structures — the **Yiju Temple** and **An'guo Temple** — still function as active religious sites rather than museum pieces. The Yiju Temple preserves Tang-dynasty cave murals, a rarity in a region where most religious art dates to the Song or later. The mountain draws Chinese pilgrims and hikers in roughly equal numbers; the approach from the valley involves a bus ride from Taiyuan to Fangshan followed by a minivan transfer up into the mountains, about 120 km from the capital.

Beiwudang is best visited as a two-day excursion from Taiyuan, spending a night in the village near the base. The summit area offers views across the loess plateau and, on clear days, toward the Yellow River to the west. The mountain receives far fewer foreign visitors than Wutai Shan and the infrastructure is correspondingly basic — English signage is nonexistent and accommodation simple — but it is exactly the kind of lived-in, unhurried Taoist mountain site that more celebrated Chinese sacred peaks have largely ceased to be.
