  An'guo Temple's name means "Pacifying the Nation" — it was rebuilt during the Song dynasty after the founding emperor's conquest of the region, a pattern of temple naming that turns religious sites into markers of political consolidation. The tradition of military patrons funding Taoist mountain temples continued through the Yuan and Ming periods, which is why the architecture here looks grander than the remote location might lead you to expect.
---

An'guo Temple sits higher on the mountain than Yiju, reached by a stone staircase that climbs through pine forest before opening onto a terrace with views across the loess plateau. The main hall dates to the Song dynasty in its core structure, though it has been rebuilt and expanded several times since — the eaves, carved doors, and courtyard layout reflect successive layers of patronage rather than any single moment of construction. A small stone stele inside records donations from local gentry across several centuries, a common form of record-keeping in Chinese religious architecture.

The temple functions as the main active religious centre on the mountain, staffed by a small community of Taoist monks who conduct daily liturgy at dawn and dusk. The surrounding terrace makes a good resting point if you are hiking the main summit trail, and the views from here on clear mornings — looking west across the Yellow River valley — are better than from the summit itself, which is often hazy.
