Jincheng occupies the southeastern corner of Shanxi, squeezed between the Taihang Mountains and the provincial borders with Henan. It is not on most travellers' itineraries, which is partly its appeal — the area around the city contains a cluster of remarkably intact Ming and Qing fortified villages that receive far fewer visitors than the more famous courtyard architecture of Pingyao. The high-speed rail connection to Taiyuan (about 90 minutes) and Zhengzhou makes it accessible as either a day trip or an overnight stop.

The standout sight is the **[Huangcheng Chancellor's Residence](/asia/china/shanxiprovince/jincheng/huangcheng_chancellors_residence)**, a walled compound 40 km northeast of the city, built between 1632 and 1703 for Chen Tingjing, a high Qing official and scholar. The complex spreads across several hectares of courtyards, towers, and residential halls on a hillside above a stream — it is comparable in ambition and preservation to the Wang Family Compound further north, and considerably less crowded. Nearby, **[Guoyu Ancient Village](/asia/china/shanxiprovince/jincheng/guoyu_ancient_village)** preserves an entire walled settlement of Ming and Qing courtyard houses, and **[Xiangyu Castle](/asia/china/shanxiprovince/jincheng/xiangyu_castle)**, a 17th-century fortified hamlet, has barely changed since it was built.

The old city of Jincheng itself contains the **[Yuhuang Temple](/asia/china/shanxiprovince/jincheng/yuhuang_temple)**, a large Song-dynasty Taoist complex whose most unusual feature is a set of astronomical sculptures — representations of the 28 lunar mansions and celestial officials — that rank among the finest examples of Song religious carving. Autumn is the best season: the [Taihang Mountain](/asia/china/shanxiprovince/jincheng/taihang_mountain_scenery) edges east of the city turn vivid red and orange from October, and the cooler temperatures make walking the village sites far more pleasant than a summer visit.
