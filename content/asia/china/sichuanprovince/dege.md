Dege is a Tibetan town at 3,100 metres near the upper Jinsha River, not far from the border with Tibet Autonomous Region, and the journey to reach it is as significant as the destination itself. Two days by bus from Kangding, through passes that exceed 4,000 metres and along river valleys of extraordinary drama, Dege sits at the far edge of what most travellers reach in Sichuan. That remoteness is part of what has preserved it.

The reason to come is the [Dege Parkhang](/asia/china/sichuanprovince/dege/dege_parkhang), the Tibetan Buddhist scriptures printing house founded in 1729. It contains over 140,000 carved wooden printing blocks — among them texts that no longer exist anywhere else in the world, saved from destruction during the Cultural Revolution by monks who buried them. Workers still operate the press by hand, inking blocks and pressing sheets of paper in a rhythmic motion that has not changed in three centuries. The entire structure is a low building smelling of ink and old wood, and admission is cheap. Photography of the blocks is restricted but you can watch the printing freely.

The [Gonchen Monastery](/asia/china/sichuanprovince/dege/gonchen_monastery), founded in 1446, occupies the hillside above the printing house. It is a genuine working monastery rather than a tourist site, and the hilltop temple complex offers the best views of the valley and the surrounding peaks. The [old town](/asia/china/sichuanprovince/dege/dege_old_town) below has small temples and prayer-flag draped alleyways where Tibetan domestic life continues largely undisturbed by visitors.

Practical matters require some preparation. ATMs in Dege do not accept foreign cards, so bring sufficient cash from Kangding or Ganzi. The town is subject to periodic travel restrictions for foreigners during politically sensitive dates, which can happen without much warning. Altitude sickness is a genuine risk — the town itself is manageable for most visitors, but the passes on approach are more demanding.
