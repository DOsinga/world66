story: The Ramappa Temple's roof is built from specially fired "floating bricks" — lightweight ceramic blocks that actually float on water. This was not a decorative choice but a structural innovation: the reduced weight meant less stress on the corbelled stone courses below, allowing the tower to be built taller than conventional brick construction would have permitted.

The Ramappa Temple, about 70 km northeast of Warangal near the village of Palampet, is the finest surviving example of Kakatiya architecture and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2021. Built in 1213 CE under Recharla Rudra, a general of the Kakatiya king Ganapati Deva, the temple took 40 years to complete and is named not for a deity but for the sculptor who created it — an unusual distinction in medieval India.

The sculptural programme is astonishing: the bracket figures (madanikas) on the outer walls are considered among the finest female sculptures in Indian art, their poses naturalistic and sensual, their ornamentation meticulously detailed. The walls are covered with panels depicting scenes from the epics, animals, and mythological narratives. The temple sits at the edge of an artificial lake created by the Kakatiyas to supply water to the complex, and the reflections in the water make for excellent early-morning photography. A paved road connects Palampet to Warangal; the journey takes about 90 minutes each way, and the site deserves at least two hours.
