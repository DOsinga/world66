  Kamakhya has no idol in its inner sanctum. Instead, in a small cave below the
  main shrine, a natural cleft in the rock is venerated as the yoni of the
  goddess, fed by an underground spring. Once a year, in June, the spring water
  reportedly runs red — interpreted as the goddess's menstrual cycle. The temple
  closes for three days during this Ambubachi festival; on the fourth day,
  pilgrims receive scraps of red cloth said to have been used by Kamakhya.

Set high on Nilachal Hill above the Brahmaputra, Kamakhya is one of the 51 Shakti Pithas — places where, according to myth, parts of the goddess Sati's body fell when Vishnu dismembered her — and a centre of Tantric Hinduism for over a thousand years. The current structure dates from the 17th century, rebuilt by the Koch king Naranarayan after the original was destroyed by Muslim invaders; the distinctive beehive-shaped shikhara is a hybrid Nilachal style unique to this temple.

The temple is intensely active. Animal sacrifice is still practised — goats, pigeons, occasionally buffalo — in a courtyard to the side of the main shrine. The atmosphere inside is dim, smoky, crowded and overwhelming. Queues to enter the inner sanctum can run for hours; foreigners often opt for the priority ticket (₹500). Smaller shrines to the ten Mahavidyas — fierce forms of the goddess — surround the main temple and are quieter. Open dawn to dusk; come early. Carry a scarf for head covering; remove leather. Photography of the inner sanctum is prohibited.
