  The main tower, the deul, once rose more than 70 metres above the platform — taller than any other temple in India. It collapsed sometime between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, possibly because the loadstone capstone interfered with ships' compasses and was removed by frustrated Portuguese sailors. The dancing hall and the audience hall, which survive, are themselves only the outer reaches of what the original complex must have been.

The Sun Temple is one of the supreme achievements of Indian medieval architecture and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1984. Built around 1250 under Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, the entire temple was conceived as a colossal stone chariot in which the sun god Surya crosses the sky. Twenty-four exquisitely carved stone wheels surround the base, each functioning as a working sundial, and seven horses, almost all now broken, strain to pull the chariot eastward.

Every surface is sculpted. The bulk of the carving depicts elephants, musicians, dancers, hunting scenes, and the daily life of thirteenth-century Odisha; a smaller fraction is the explicit erotic statuary that has made the temple internationally famous. Three large green-chlorite Surya images, one for morning, midday, and evening, were positioned so that real sunlight catches each at the appointed hour — only one survives in place. Plan a full morning and bring a guide; without one, much of what makes the temple extraordinary slides past unread.
