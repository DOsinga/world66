The Konark Sun Temple is one of the supreme works of medieval Indian architecture, and for most visitors it is genuinely one of the most astonishing things they encounter in the subcontinent. Built around 1250 CE by King Narasimha Deva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, it was conceived as a colossal stone chariot for the Sun God Surya — 24 elaborately carved wheels arranged around the base, seven horses straining at the front, and three sculptures of the sun positioned to catch the morning, midday, and evening light in turn.

The scale is hard to grasp from photographs. The platform and audience hall together represent only a portion of the original structure — the main tower, which may have stood 60 metres high, collapsed sometime in the sixteenth or seventeenth century, and the interior of the remaining structure was filled with sand in colonial times to stabilise it. What stands is still extraordinary: every surface is covered in sculpture of the highest quality, depicting not only mythology but the textures of thirteenth-century Odishan life — musicians, dancers, soldiers, elephants, lovers, and animals in extraordinary variety and detail. The erotic carvings are startling but in context they represent a small fraction of the whole programme.

Entry fees and opening hours apply; the Archaeological Survey of India manages the site. Sunrise and the hour before closing are the quietest times to visit.
