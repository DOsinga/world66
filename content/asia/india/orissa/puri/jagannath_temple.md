The Jagannath Temple is the gravitational centre of Puri — and in many ways, of all eastern India. Its *deul* (main tower) rises 65 metres above the old city, visible from far out to sea and from every corner of town, its white walls crowned with the spinning wheel of Vishnu and a flag that never stops flying. It has stood here for over a thousand years, though the current structure dates mostly to the 12th century, built by the Ganga king Anantavarman Chodaganga Deva. Puri is one of the four *char dhams* — the four sacred pilgrimage sites that a Hindu should visit in a lifetime — alongside Dwarka, Badrinath, and Rameswaram. That status alone tells you what the temple means to Hinduism.

The temple complex covers roughly 4 hectares within a fortified wall. Four great gates face the cardinal directions — the eastern *Singhadwara* (Lion Gate) is the main entrance, guarded by two crouching lions and a standing Aruna (charioteer of the sun) pillar that was moved here from the Sun Temple at Konark. Through this gate, pilgrims have streamed for centuries, and it remains the busiest entrance. The entire complex contains over 120 subsidiary shrines, their carved walls a continuous surface of mythology in stone.

Non-Hindus may not enter the temple. This is absolute — there are guards, and the rule is enforced. But the roof terrace of the Raghunandan Library opposite the eastern gate provides a view into the compound, and for many non-Hindu visitors this glimpse of the inner sanctum is the most they will see. Don't be resentful about it; understand that the temple has operated on its own terms for a millennium and has no obligation to be a tourist attraction. Stand outside and let the energy of the place reach you — the bells, the chanting, the smell of flowers and camphor, the steady stream of pilgrims arriving with rice and coconuts.

What makes the Jagannath Temple remarkable even from the outside is its economic ecosystem. The temple employs around 6,000 *sevakas* (servants of the deity), each belonging to one of 36 orders and 97 *niyogas* — specialised groups responsible for specific ritual duties. Some families have held the same role for dozens of generations. The most famous service is the *mahaprasad* — the sacred food cooked daily in the temple kitchen, the largest in the world. Over 700 cooks prepare meals in 752 clay pots stacked over wood fires in a strict sequence. The result feeds tens of thousands of pilgrims each day. This food is sold at the *Anand Bazar* within the temple compound and is considered supremely holy: prasad that has been offered to the deity and returns to the devotee as blessing. It does not discriminate by caste — everyone eats together.

The *pandas* — hereditary priests whose families have been assigned specific pilgrims for generations — are a powerful presence in the city. When you arrive in Puri, your family's panda may already know you are coming; the system of tracking pilgrims by region of origin is ancient and meticulous. The panda will assist with rituals, serve as a guide within the temple, and accept a fee for his services. Non-Hindus will not encounter the pandas in the usual way, but you will see them everywhere in the streets around the temple, in their distinctive dress, negotiating the eternal business of devotion.
