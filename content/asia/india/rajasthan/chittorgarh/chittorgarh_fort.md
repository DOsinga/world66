  Chittorgarh Fort was sacked three times — in 1303 by Alauddin Khalji, in 1535 by Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, and in 1567–68 by Emperor Akbar. Each siege ended in jauhar: the mass self-immolation of the women and children in the fort to prevent their capture, while the men rode out in saffron robes for a suicidal last charge. Akbar was so shaken by what he found when he entered the silent fort that he reportedly wept, and he later wrote about the siege in his memoirs.

Chittorgarh Fort is the largest fort in India by area, stretching 13 kilometres around its hilltop perimeter and covering nearly 700 acres. It was the seat of the Mewar kingdom, whose Rajput rulers resisted Mughal expansion for generations — and whose story of repeated defeat, jauhar, and reconquest has made Chittorgarh the most emotionally charged site in Rajasthan.

Inside the fort walls lie ruined palaces, temples, towers, reservoirs, and the remains of an entire lost city. The main road winds up through seven gates (poles) to the plateau, passing increasingly dramatic views of the plain below. The sheer scale of the place — and its emptiness — is part of what makes it extraordinary. You can walk for an hour and see nothing but ruined walls and lizards.
