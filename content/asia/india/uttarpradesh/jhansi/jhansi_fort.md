Jhansi Fort stands on Bangra Hill, a granite outcrop that makes it visible from almost anywhere in the city. Virsingh Deo of Orchha built it in 1613, and it passed through Mughal, Maratha, and eventually British hands before becoming the stage for one of the most celebrated episodes in Indian history. The fort is substantial — roughly 3 kilometres of perimeter wall — with ten gates, a handful of temples inside, and the massive Kadak Bijli cannon still aimed at where the British lines once were.

The fort interior holds more than most visitors expect. Shiva and Ganesh temples are still active. The old water tanks and granaries show how long a garrison could have held out. The archaeological museum inside the fort displays sculpture from the wider Bundelkhand region, mostly medieval and some older — not flashy, but useful context for understanding what the Chandela and Bundela kingdoms produced. The views over the city and towards the Betwa plains make the climb worthwhile on their own.

Most visitors hire a local guide at the gate. Quality varies, but the best guides know the 1857 battle in granular detail — which British regiment came from where, which gate fell first, what Rani Lakshmibai ordered in her final hours inside these walls. Budget two to three hours. The fort is open daily except Monday; the museum charges a small entrance fee separate from the fort admission.
