Tidore is a small volcanic island in [North Maluku](/asia/indonesia/maluku), separated from [Ternate](/asia/indonesia/maluku/ternate) by a narrow strait. The two islands look almost like twins — each dominated by the perfect cone of a volcano rising straight from the sea — and for several hundred years they were the rival capitals of the world's clove trade. Where Ternate allied itself with the Dutch, Tidore took the Spanish side, and the contest between the two sultanates shaped European involvement in this corner of the archipelago.

Today Tidore is the quieter of the two. Most travellers cross over for the day from Ternate, take a bemo around the ring road, climb to one of the Spanish forts above Soasio for the view, and return on the late afternoon boat. Those who stay a night experience a slow, friendly island town with very little in the way of tourism infrastructure but plenty of historical depth — the [Kadato Kie](/asia/indonesia/maluku/tidore/kadato_kie) palace, the ruins of [Fort Tahula](/asia/indonesia/maluku/tidore/fort_tahula) and [Fort Tohula](/asia/indonesia/maluku/tidore/fort_tohula), and a coastline of dark-sand beaches and clove gardens.

The island's central volcano, [Gunung Kiematubu](/asia/indonesia/maluku/tidore/gunung_kiematubu), reaches almost 1,730 m and can be climbed in a long day with a local guide. Around its lower slopes the air is heavy with the smell of clove — Tidore still produces the spice that once made it one of the richest places on earth, and you can buy fresh clove buds in the markets of Soasio for almost nothing.

Crossing from Ternate is straightforward: speedboats run from Bastiong harbour to Rum on the north tip of Tidore every half hour or so, taking about ten minutes. From Rum, public bemos and motorbike taxis cover the island's single ring road.
