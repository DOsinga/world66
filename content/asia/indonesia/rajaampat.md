Raja Ampat — the Four Kings — is an archipelago of over 1,500 islands off the northwest tip of West Papua, at the point where the Pacific and Indian Oceans meet. Marine biologists say it contains more species of fish, coral, and invertebrate life than anywhere else on the planet. Every survey seems to confirm this. If you want to dive or snorkel, there is nowhere on earth that compares.

The name comes from four main islands: Waigeo, Batanta, Salawati, and [Misool](/asia/indonesia/rajaampat/misool). Waigeo is the largest and the administrative hub, with the main town of Waisai a 3-hour fast ferry from [Sorong](/asia/indonesia/irianjaya/sorong) on the mainland. [Wayag](/asia/indonesia/rajaampat/wayag), a cluster of mushroom-shaped karst islands rising from a lagoon in the far northwest, is the image that appears on every travel website about Indonesia. [Pianemo](/asia/indonesia/rajaampat/pianemo), a smaller version of the same landscape, is closer to Waisai and sees more visitors. Both are extraordinary.

The diving is what sets Raja Ampat apart from every other destination. The currents that sweep through the passages between the islands carry nutrients that feed the reef, and the reef repays this with a density of life that is simply overwhelming. Wobbegong sharks rest on the reef; pygmy seahorses live in fan corals; walking sharks — which actually walk along the reef floor on their fins — are found nowhere else. The visibility is often outstanding; the water is warm. Most people leave saying it was the best diving of their lives.

Visiting takes commitment. Getting here from Europe or Australia involves at least two or three flights plus a ferry crossing. Once here, most visitors stay on a liveaboard dive boat or at a small eco-resort built on stilts over the reef. There is no mass tourism infrastructure — no beach clubs, no resorts with swimming pools, no organised day-trip boats. The isolation is the point.
