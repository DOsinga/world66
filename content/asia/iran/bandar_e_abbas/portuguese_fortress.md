  The Portuguese arrived at Hormuz in 1507 under Afonso de Albuquerque, who
  recognised the strait as the key to controlling trade between the Indian Ocean
  and the Persian Gulf. They held the region for over a century, building a chain
  of forts from East Africa to the Gulf. The fortress at Bandar Abbas (then known
  as Gamru) was one of their mainland bases. In 1622, a combined force of Persians
  and English East India Company troops expelled the Portuguese — the English
  received trading rights in exchange for their naval support, a deal that would
  shape the region's history for centuries.

The ruins of the Portuguese fortress stand near the harbour, a red-brick reminder that Bandar Abbas was once a strategic prize in the contest for control of the Strait of Hormuz. The Portuguese built here in the 16th century to secure their dominance of Gulf trade, and the fortress was one of several mainland installations supporting their island stronghold on Hormuz.

The ruins are not extensive — much of the original structure has been built over or has crumbled — but the setting is evocative, and the harbour views from the site give a clear sense of why the Portuguese valued this location. The site can usually be visited without a fee; access may require asking a nearby resident or guard for the entrance.
