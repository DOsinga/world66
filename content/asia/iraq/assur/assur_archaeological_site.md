The ruins of Assur (ancient Ashur, modern Qal'at Sherqat) cover a rocky promontory above the western bank of the Tigris, roughly 100 km south of Mosul. The site was the first capital of the Assyrian state and served as the religious heart of Assyrian civilisation for over two thousand years, from at least the 3rd millennium BC until the city was sacked by the Medes and Babylonians in 614 BC.

German archaeologists excavated the site extensively in the early 20th century (1903–1914), revealing palaces, the great ziggurat of the god Ashur, temples, and the royal necropolis where Assyrian kings were buried in vaulted stone tombs. Many of the finest portable objects from Assur are now in the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin. What remains on site is impressive in scale if not always dramatic in appearance — foundations, eroded mud-brick walls, and the outline of one of the ancient world's most important cities. The setting on the Tigris, with the river below and the plains stretching away in every direction, gives a real sense of why this ridge was chosen for a city thousands of years ago.

Assur was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003. It was simultaneously placed on UNESCO's List of World Heritage in Danger due to a dam project that would have flooded the site; that project remains suspended but is periodically revived.
