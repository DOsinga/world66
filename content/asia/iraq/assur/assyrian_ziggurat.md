  The ziggurat at Assur was dedicated to Ashur, the city's patron god and supreme deity of the Assyrian pantheon. It was built and rebuilt over centuries, with significant construction under the Middle Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta I in the 13th century BC. The ziggurat stood at the highest point of the city, visible from the river for many kilometres — a deliberate architectural statement of divine and political power.

The ziggurat at Assur is the most prominent surviving structure at the site — a massive terraced platform of mud brick that once supported a temple to the god Ashur at its summit. The base dimensions are substantial, though the upper tiers and the temple itself are long gone. What remains gives a clear sense of the scale of Assyrian monumental architecture and the enormous investment of labour that went into maintaining the city's religious infrastructure.

Ziggurats (stepped temple towers) were a defining architectural form of ancient Mesopotamia, and the one at Assur is among the oldest. Standing on or near the base and looking out over the Tigris valley, you can understand why this location was chosen: commanding views in all directions, defensible, and situated on the river that was the main artery of Mesopotamian life. The German excavations of the early 20th century cleared much of the debris that had accumulated over the ruins, making the structure's outline legible to the visitor.
