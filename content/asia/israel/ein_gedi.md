Ein Gedi is a lush oasis nature reserve clinging to the cliffs above the Dead Sea, about 90 km southeast of Jerusalem. The contrast with the surrounding desert is dramatic: springs feed waterfalls, ibex pick their way along rocky ledges, and hyraxes sun themselves on the boulders.

The most popular trail follows Wadi David to [David's Waterfall](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/davids_waterfall) — an easy hour's walk with a rewarding payoff. A second trail through [Wadi Arugot](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/wadi_arugot) reaches a larger falls in a narrower canyon. Both trails are well marked within the [Ein Gedi Nature Reserve](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/ein_gedi_nature_reserve). Arrive early; the reserve gets crowded mid-morning and can be very hot by midday.

The former Ein Gedi Spa on the Dead Sea shore is now closed, a casualty of the [Dead Sea sinkholes](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/dead_sea_sinkholes) that have formed as the sea retreats. The [Ein Gedi Kibbutz botanical garden](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/kibbutz_botanical_garden) is still open and contains over 900 plant species. The kibbutz also has a guesthouse and a restaurant.

Between the wadi entrances, the [Ein Gedi archaeological site](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/ein_gedi_archaeological_site) preserves the ruins of a Byzantine synagogue with a remarkable mosaic floor. For serious walkers, the [Cave of Letters](/asia/israel/ein_gedi/cave_of_letters) in Nahal Hever — where letters of Bar Kokhba were found during the second Jewish revolt — is a strenuous half-day hike from the plateau.
