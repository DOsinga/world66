story: >
  When the museum opened in 1969, it was Japan's first open-air sculpture park — a concept that had been established in Europe (Middelheim in Antwerp, the Louisiana in Denmark) but was new to Japan. The founders had to persuade local authorities that outdoor sculpture could be considered a museum, which required some creative bureaucratic manoeuvring. It now attracts over a million visitors per year and has been widely imitated across Japan.
---

The Hakone Open-Air Museum (Chōkoku no Mori Bijutsukan — "Sculpture Forest Museum") sits on a slope of the Hakone mountains and combines large-scale outdoor sculpture with several indoor galleries in a setting of gardens, water features, and distant mountain views. It is one of the most enjoyable museums in Japan, particularly for visitors who are not normally drawn to art museums.

The outdoor collection ranges from Rodin bronzes to large abstract works by Henry Moore, Barbara Hepworth, and Jean Arp, displayed on lawns and terraces where the surrounding landscape becomes part of the experience. A Picasso pavilion houses works from his later period — ceramics, paintings, prints — in a dedicated circular building. The indoor galleries hold temporary exhibitions and rotating works from the permanent collection.

The museum is directly accessible from Chōkoku-no-Mori Station on the Hakone Tozan Line, which is the mountain railway out of Hakone-Yumoto. Allow at least two hours; it is easy to spend an entire morning here.
