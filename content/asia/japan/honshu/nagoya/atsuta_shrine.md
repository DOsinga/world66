  Atsuta is said to shelter the Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi, one of the three Imperial Regalia of Japan — a sword pulled from the tail of an eight-headed dragon by the god Susano-o and later wielded by the legendary prince Yamato Takeru. The sword has never been shown publicly; even emperors are forbidden to look at it. In 1945 it was secretly evacuated to the mountains of Nagano to escape Allied bombing, then returned in quiet ceremonies after the war.

Atsuta is one of the three or four most sacred Shinto sites in Japan, ranking just behind Ise and Izumo. The shrine claims a 1,900-year history and is dedicated to Atsuta-no-Okami, the deity that resides in the legendary Kusanagi sword. The grounds — about 200,000 square metres of camphor forest in the south of the city — feel a world away from the surrounding traffic. A 1,000-year-old camphor tree, planted by Kobo Daishi according to tradition, stands near the main path.

The main buildings are unpainted cypress in the simple shin-mei style of Ise, rebuilt about every twenty years by tradition. The shrine's treasure house holds over 6,000 artefacts including swords, masks and ancient documents — a small but excellent selection is on display in rotation. Best visited early morning or late afternoon. Nearby Hourai-ken serves the city's most famous hitsumabushi (grilled eel over rice) and has done since 1873.
