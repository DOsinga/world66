**Night on the Galactic Railroad** by Kenji Miyazawa (1927, published posthumously). A boy and his friend board a cosmic train and travel across the night sky. It is a children's story in form but something stranger in effect — Miyazawa was working through Buddhist ideas about death and compassion in the landscape of his beloved Iwate mountains. Essential reading before or after a visit to the memorial museum in Hanamaki.

**The Narrow Road to the Deep North** by Matsuo Basho (1689). Japan's greatest travel narrative, written by the haiku poet who walked the length of the Tohoku region in 1689. Basho's visit to Hiraizumi — where he stood amid the ruins of the Fujiwara's golden age and wrote his most celebrated poem — is one of the set pieces of Japanese literature. Read it before you stand at Takadachi Gikeido.

**Ghosts of the Tsunami** by Richard Lloyd Parry (2017). A British journalist covers the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami, focusing on the village of Okawa in neighbouring Miyagi Prefecture. The book's reach extends throughout the disaster zone, including the Iwate coast, and it is the most searching account of what happened and what the loss meant to the communities left behind.

**Confessions of a Mask** by Yukio Mishima (1949). Not set in Iwate, but Mishima spent formative time in the north and several of his early works engage with the Tohoku landscape and character. For readers who want context on the psychological weight of the region in postwar Japanese literature, this is the way in.
