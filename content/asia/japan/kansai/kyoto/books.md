title: Books
type: section
---

A few books that pay back the time spent on them before or during a Kyoto trip.

For a single readable introduction, *Kyoto: A Cultural and Literary History* by John Dougill (Signal, 2006) walks neighbourhood by neighbourhood through the city's history and literary associations and is a good companion in the field.

On the geisha quarters, Liza Dalby's *Geisha* (University of California Press, 1983) is the classic anthropological study by the only Western woman ever to train and work as a *geiko* in Kyoto. Mineko Iwasaki's autobiography *Geisha, a Life* (2002) is a corrective to *Memoirs of a Geisha* by an actual former Gion *geiko* who served as one of Golden's sources before publicly disowning the book.

For temples and gardens, Marc Treib and Ron Herman's *A Guide to the Gardens of Kyoto* (Shufunotomo, 1980 and various updates) remains the best single-volume garden guide. Donald Richie's *A Tractate on Japanese Aesthetics* (Stone Bridge Press, 2007) is short, idiosyncratic, and changes how you look at a moss garden.

For history, *The Tale of Genji* by Murasaki Shikibu, in either Royall Tyler's or Dennis Washburn's translations, is the eleventh-century novel set in the Heian court of Kyoto and is the closest you can come to inhabiting the city's earliest imagined world.

For atmosphere, Junichirō Tanizaki's *The Makioka Sisters* (set partly in Kyoto in the 1930s) and his essay *In Praise of Shadows* are still the best literary preparation for the visual mood of the city.
