Shikoku's largest city is also arguably its most interesting. Matsuyama has one of Japan's handful of original feudal castles still standing on its hilltop, a historic hot spring resort practically attached to the city limits, and a strong literary identity rooted in haiku — the poet [Masaoka Shiki](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/masaoka_shiki_memorial_museum) was born here, and his influence is still felt.

The city and the [Dogo district](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/dogo_district) are essentially continuous, which means you can move between [castle](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/matsuyama_castle), temples, and thermal baths in the same afternoon. [Dogo Onsen Honkan](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/dogo_onsen_honkan) is among Japan's most celebrated hot spring bathhouses, and its old wooden building has been drawing visitors for well over a century. [Isaniwa Shrine](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/isaniwa_shrine) perches on a hill above the onsen district, reached by a long staircase, and is one of only three shrines in Japan built in its unusual architectural style.

Literary Matsuyama is worth exploring too. The [Saka no Ue no Kumo Museum](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/saka_no_ue_no_kumo_museum), designed by Tadao Ando, is built into a slope near the castle and celebrates the epic novel about Meiji-era Japan. The historic [Botchan tram](/asia/japan/shikoku/matsuyama/botchan_train) still runs on the city's old tram network — named after the famous novel in which its fictional predecessor was memorably insulted.

Matsuyama also makes a good base for Shikoku. Mt. Ishizuchi, the island's highest peak, is about an hour by bus.
