title: Books
type: section
---

Jordan has produced distinctive literature in Arabic, and Amman's role as a refuge for Palestinian, Iraqi, and Syrian intellectuals gives the city an unusual concentration of writing about exile, identity, and the Arab world's upheavals. The following are the most useful books for understanding the city and its region.

**Arabian Sands** — Thesiger's account of crossing the Empty Quarter with the Bedu in the late 1940s is not strictly about Jordan, but it is the most vivid account ever written of the Bedouin culture that shaped the character of Transjordan and is still visible in Amman's older families and in the desert to the east. Thesiger spent time in Jordan before and after his Arabian journeys and understood the Bedouin world on its own terms.

The book captures a way of life that was ending even as Thesiger witnessed it — the camel-riding, tent-dwelling, desert-navigating culture of the deep Arabian peninsula. Reading it before visiting the Jordanian desert casts the landscape in a completely different light.

**Married to a Bedouin** — A New Zealand nurse met a Bedouin jewellery seller at Petra in 1978, married him three weeks later, and spent the next fifteen years living in a cave in the rose-red city — before the Jordanian government relocated the Bedouin community to a modern settlement at Wadi Musa. Van Geldermalsen's memoir is funny, honest, and deeply affectionate toward the Bdoul tribe, and it captures the texture of traditional Bedouin life in Jordan with the precision of an insider-outsider.

It reads as a companion piece to any visit to Petra, and it gives context for the Jordanian Bedouin culture visible throughout the country — including in Amman, where the tribal values of hospitality, reputation, and family loyalty still shape urban life among the older families.

**Mornings in Jenin** — Susan Abulhawa's novel follows four generations of a Palestinian family from their village in northern Palestine through the 1948 Nakba, to a refugee camp in Jenin, and eventually to the Palestinian diaspora. It is sentimental in places but its account of the daily reality of Palestinian refugee life — including life in Jordan, where many Palestinian families rebuilt their existence — is vivid and useful for understanding the population that makes up so much of modern Amman.

The novel is widely read in Jordan, particularly in the Palestinian community. It captures the longing for return and the ambivalence about integration that shapes Jordanian-Palestinian identity in ways that no non-fiction account quite manages.

**Seven Pillars of Wisdom** — Lawrence's account of the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918 — in which he served as a British officer advising the Hashemite forces under Emir Faisal — covers territory that is now Jordan in close detail. Aqaba, Wadi Rum, Azraq, and the Hejaz Railway that Lawrence spent years dynamiting all feature extensively. The book is part memoir, part history, part self-mythology, and deeply unreliable as a factual account, but as a piece of writing about the landscape and people of the region it remains unmatched.

Understanding Lawrence, and the Arab Revolt, is essential background for making sense of how Jordan came to exist as a state at all — the Hashemite kingdom that rules today is a direct product of the post-war settlement that Lawrence both shaped and bitterly opposed.

**The Question of Palestine** — Edward Said's 1979 argument for Palestinian rights and statehood, written from the perspective of a Palestinian intellectual who grew up partly in Jerusalem and Cairo before becoming a Columbia professor. It is the foundational text for understanding the Palestinian experience of displacement that has defined Amman more than almost any other factor — the city is majority Palestinian in origin, and the political and cultural tensions Said describes are present in everyday Amman life.

Said is a challenging read but precise and serious. This book, more than any other, explains why Amman looks and feels the way it does.
