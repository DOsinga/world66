story: >
  The petroglyphs at Tamgaly were created by cultures separated by thousands of years — the earliest images date to the Bronze Age, around 1400 BCE, while the newest additions are medieval. The Bronze Age figures are the most remarkable: elaborate compositions showing solar deities with rayed heads, hunting scenes, and rituals involving cattle and horses. The UNESCO committee that added Tamgaly to the World Heritage List described it as "one of the most representative collections of petroglyphs in Central Asia."
---

The Tamgaly Archaeological Landscape, a UNESCO World Heritage Site about 170 kilometres northwest of Almaty, contains over 5,000 petroglyphs carved into the dark desert varnish on the sandstone outcrops of a river gorge. The carvings span from the Bronze Age to the medieval period and document the cultures — nomadic, pastoral, and settled — that passed through this route over three millennia.

The drive from Almaty takes about two and a half hours through increasingly dry steppe landscape. The site itself is a narrow gorge with the petroglyphs scattered across the rock faces on both sides; a guide or the site map is helpful for locating the main compositions. The best preserved and most visually striking images are near the sacred grove at the upper end of the gorge. Go in spring or autumn — summer days in this part of the steppe are extremely hot, and there is no shade.
