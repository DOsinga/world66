The Cathedral of Our Lady of Fatima is the most unexpected building in Karagandy. A neo-Gothic Catholic church rising above the flat steppe city, it was built by and for the German and Polish deportees who were sent to the Gulag camps and special settlements around Karaganda in the 1930s and 1940s. They came from across Eastern Europe — devout Catholics stripped of everything, dumped in a place that had no place for them. They kept their faith anyway.

The cathedral you see today was completed after Soviet restrictions eased, but it stands on the foundation of decades of hidden worship. Inside, it is simple and moving: Catholic iconography in a setting that still carries the weight of what it took to build it. Karagandy's German community has largely emigrated to Germany since 1991 — a demographic shift you can trace in the shrinking congregation — but the cathedral remains, tended by the community members who stayed and by Kazakh Catholics. It is genuinely worth the trip across town. Few buildings anywhere carry this particular mix of grief, faith, and improbable persistence.
