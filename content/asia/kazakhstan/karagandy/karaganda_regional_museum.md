story: The Karlag — the Karaganda Corrective Labour Camp — was one of the largest camp complexes in the entire Gulag system. At its peak in the late 1940s it held more than 65,000 prisoners spread across a territory the size of a small country. The museum's Gulag section is one of the few places in Kazakhstan where this history is told with real seriousness. Coming here before walking the city streets gives everything else a different texture.

The Karaganda Regional Museum covers the full sweep of the region's history, from Neolithic settlements on the steppe through the Bronze Age cultures that produced some of the earliest horse-riders in the world, to the Russian Empire's expansion east, and on into the Soviet industrial period that shaped the city you are walking through. The collection is solid and well-organised, and the historical sweep is genuinely interesting even if you arrive knowing nothing about Kazakhstan.

The section on the Gulag era and the Karlag camp system deserves particular attention. It documents the mechanics of the camp system — how prisoners were transported, processed, put to work, and (if they survived) eventually released — and gives faces and names to what can otherwise feel like an abstraction. There are personal items, photographs, and testimonies. It is not comfortable material, but it is essential context for understanding what Karagandy is and where it came from. The museum also covers the post-independence period and Kazakhstan's emergence as a modern state, which provides useful counterbalance.
