The Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi is Kazakhstan's only UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the finest examples of Timurid architecture in the world. Built by Tamerlane beginning in 1389 to honour the 12th-century Sufi poet and mystic, it was intended to be the grandest mausoleum on the Central Asian steppe — a statement of Timurid power and piety in a region that revered Yasawi as its spiritual father. The building was never finished, but what was completed is breathtaking.

The exterior is dominated by a massive turquoise dome, 18 metres in diameter, rising from a rectangular base decorated with intricate tilework in geometric patterns. The building is enormous for its era: the main hall alone is about 18 metres across. Pilgrims from across the Turkic-speaking world come here in large numbers, particularly on Fridays and during religious holidays, and the atmosphere is genuinely devotional rather than merely touristic. Three visits to this mausoleum are said in local tradition to equal one hajj to Mecca.

Inside, the centrepiece is the great kazan — a bronze ritual vessel nearly two metres in diameter, hammered from an alloy containing gold and silver, used for ceremonial drinks distributed to pilgrims. The cauldron was brought here from Samarkand under Timur's orders. The tomb chamber of Yasawi himself is beyond, under the main dome, covered with hand-painted tilework in blue and white. The layout is complex — multiple chambers radiate from the central hall — and it takes time to absorb. Photography is permitted in most areas.
