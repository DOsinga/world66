  King Bagyidaw began Pahtodawgyi in 1820 as his great religious monument, intending
  it to surpass the famous Shwezigon Pagoda at Bagan. Construction was abandoned
  when an astrologer predicted that the king who completed it would die. Bagyidaw

  has stood this way for nearly two centuries.

Pahtodawgyi is one of Amarapura's most distinctive structures — a large unfinished Buddhist pagoda north of Taungthaman Lake, its truncated shape a visible reminder of superstition overriding ambition. The pagoda's base and lower terraces are intact and finely ornamented; the upper portion simply does not exist, ending abruptly where construction stopped.

The site is quiet compared to U Bein Bridge and has fewer visitors, which allows for a more contemplative visit. The terraces can be climbed and offer views over the surrounding countryside and toward the lake. Monks from the nearby monastery sometimes meditate here in the mornings. Several smaller shrines and Buddha images have been added around the base over subsequent generations.

The pagoda grounds contain a small museum with artefacts and inscriptions relating to Amarapura's period as capital. The surrounding area has several other minor pagodas and monasteries accessible on foot, making Pahtodawgyi a good starting point for a broader walk through the northern part of the old city.
