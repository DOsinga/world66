The Kumgang Mountains (Diamond Mountains) rise along North Korea's eastern coast, a range of granite peaks and gorges that have been celebrated in Korean poetry and painting for over a thousand years. From 1998 to 2008 they were the site of a remarkable experiment: a joint tourist project between North Korea and the South Korean conglomerate Hyundai Asan allowed South Korean tourists to visit the mountains overland or by sea, bringing limited contact between the divided peninsula's people.

The project was suspended in 2008 following the shooting of a South Korean tourist and has not resumed. During its decade of operation it gave tens of thousands of visitors a glimpse of North Korean mountain scenery — the valleys, waterfalls, and rocky faces that give the range its "diamond" name — as well as an unusual window onto daily North Korean life from the fenced tourist corridors. The experience of seeing North Korean guides wearing their lapel pins of the Great Leader, villages visible from the roads with oxen working the fields, and border formalities more thorough than anywhere else in the world, was unlike any other tourist experience on earth.

Whether access will reopen is impossible to predict. For now the Kumgang Mountains are visible from the south at Goseong Observatory near the DMZ — one of the few spots on the South Korean side with a clear view north into the range.
