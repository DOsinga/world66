  The Line of Control follows the ceasefire line established after the
  first Indo-Pakistani War in 1949. It was formalised by the Simla Agreement
  of 1972 but has never been accepted by either side as a permanent
  international border — a legal ambiguity that has kept the Kashmir question
  unresolved for more than seven decades.

The Line of Control lies only a few kilometres to the east of Hajira — close enough that military checkpoints are a routine part of the local landscape and the political geography is impossible to ignore. For travellers with an interest in contested borders and post-partition history, this proximity gives Hajira a particular character: you are at the edge of one of the world's longest-running territorial disputes.

Visiting the Line itself is not possible for civilians — the area is under military control and access is restricted. What you can observe are the checkpoints, the presence of army vehicles on the roads, and the occasional fortified position on a hilltop. The local population lives with this reality as ordinary life, and conversations about the border, about relatives on the other side in Indian-administered Kashmir, and about the 1947 partition are not unusual. Pakistan requires foreigners to register with local police when travelling in border areas; check the current requirements before heading out from Hajira toward the east.
