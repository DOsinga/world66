Makli is one of the most remarkable archaeological sites in Asia — a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering approximately 10 square kilometres on a rocky ridge above Thatta, containing an estimated 500,000 to one million graves. It is widely considered the largest necropolis in the world.

The site was used as a royal burial ground from the 14th century through the late 18th century, encompassing the periods of the Samma dynasty, the Arghun rulers, the Tarkhans, and the Mughal governors. Each dynasty left its architectural signature: Samma tombs tend to be relatively simple stone structures with intricate carved surface decoration; Mughal-period tombs are more architecturally complex, drawing on the full vocabulary of Mughal design.

The scale is genuinely difficult to comprehend until you're walking in it. The necropolis is not a compact monument but an entire landscape of tombs — massive royal mausoleums standing alongside humbler graves, with open desert between them. The larger tombs include elaborate entrance gateways, carved stone screens (jalis), domes, and inscriptions in Arabic and Sindhi. The quality of the stonecutting is exceptional.

The **Tomb of Jam Nizamuddin** and the tombs of the Tarkhan nobility are among the most elaborate. The site is maintained by the Archaeological Survey of Pakistan and entrance is charged. Early morning visits, before the heat and tour groups arrive, give the most atmospheric experience.

The necropolis is in a somewhat fragile condition — some tombs have been poorly restored, others are slowly eroding — but the overall scale and quality of what survives is overwhelming.
