In the dry season the Chocolate Hills are exactly what the name suggests: over a thousand chocolate-brown, cone-shaped mounds spread across the interior of Bohol. In the wet season they turn a vivid green and lose their chocolate resemblance, but they are still a remarkable sight. The hills are probably of karst limestone origin — marine stone uplifted and eroded into near-identical cones — though local legend claims they are the tears of giants who once lived on the island.

The main [viewing complex](/asia/philippines/bohol/chocolatehills/chocolate_hills_complex) is at Carmen, reached by bus or tour from [Tagbilaran](/asia/philippines/bohol/tagbilaran). The trip through the interior is as interesting as the hills themselves. Along the way you can stop at [Baclayon Church](/asia/philippines/bohol/chocolatehills/baclayon_church), one of the oldest in the Philippines (built in 1595), visit the [Philippine Tarsier Sanctuary](/asia/philippines/bohol/chocolatehills/tarsier_sanctuary) to see the tiny wide-eyed primates at close range, and take a floating restaurant cruise on the [Loboc River](/asia/philippines/bohol/chocolatehills/loboc_river) through jungle-draped gorge. The rice fields along the road are intensely green and worth the drive on their own.
