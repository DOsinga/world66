  Pamilacan's residents were once among the most skilled whale and manta ray hunters in the Philippines — the island's name comes from "pilak," the large hook they used to catch mantas. When marine mammals became protected by law in the 1990s, the community pivoted from hunting to watching, guided by a local organisation that now runs the dolphin-watching trips. Former hunters became guides. The transition is one of the more successful conservation stories in the country.

Dolphin-watching trips from Pamilacan leave just after dawn, when pods of spinner and bottlenose dolphins are reliably found in the waters between the island and Bohol. The trips last two to three hours and sightings are common — spinner dolphins in particular are often seen in large groups performing the aerial spins that give them their name. Whale sharks and Bryde's whales are present in these waters but not guaranteed; if you encounter them, it is a bonus.

The boats are basic outrigger bancas. You will get wet if the sea is running. Bring sun protection — the glare off the water at that hour is fierce — and a waterproof bag for your camera. Trips are arranged through the Pamilacan Island Dolphin and Whale Watching Organisation in the village. Confirm that your guide is affiliated with the official organisation rather than an independent operator; the community cooperative reinvests revenues into conservation and village development.
