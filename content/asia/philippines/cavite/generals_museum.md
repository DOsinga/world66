  Cavite City occupies a narrow peninsula jutting into Manila Bay — a geographic
  quirk that made it ideal as a Spanish naval base and later a target for American
  warships. The entire peninsula can be walked end to end in under an hour, and the
  density of historic sites relative to its size is extraordinary for a Philippine
  provincial city.

The old Cavite City peninsula, known locally as Cavite Proper, repays a slow afternoon on foot. The streets are narrow, the buildings are a mix of Spanish colonial, American-era, and mid-20th century architecture, and the atmosphere is genuinely unhurried compared to Manila. Several heritage sites are within easy walking distance of each other.

The Cavite Cathedral (Saint Peter and Paul Parish) is a solid 17th-century church that has been rebuilt several times after earthquakes and fires but retains its historic importance as a centre of Catholic life during the colonial period. Nearby, the ruins of the old ropewalk — where rigging was made for the Spanish galleon fleet — can still be traced in the street pattern. The Sangley Point area at the tip of the peninsula was an American naval air station from 1941 and has its own WWII history.

The Cavite City Museum, when open, covers the town's maritime history and independence-era significance with photographs and artefacts. Local guides available through the tourism office offer walking tours that connect the main sites and explain the peninsula's strategic importance across three centuries of colonial rule.
