Jubail is one of the most extraordinary urban projects of the twentieth century — a city of more than 100,000 people built from scratch in the desert on the Arabian Gulf coast, begun in 1975 and still expanding. Before the Royal Commission for Jubail and Yanbu arrived, there was a small fishing village here. Today it is the industrial heartland of Saudi Arabia, home to SABIC, Saudi Aramco, and dozens of downstream petrochemical facilities that together make Jubail one of the largest industrial complexes on earth.

It is not a conventional tourist destination. Access to parts of the city requires Royal Commission permission, and the landscape is dominated by refineries, cooling towers, and the King Fahd Industrial Port rather than monuments or markets. But for travellers who find themselves in the Eastern Province — or who are simply curious about the scale of what industrialisation looks like when a state decides to build a city in a generation — Jubail is genuinely worth a detour.

The coastline is the surprise. The Royal Commission's control over development has meant that the stretches of shore not given over to industry are unusually clean and unspoiled. The Jubail Wildlife Sanctuary protects dugongs, sea turtles, and flamingos. The Mangrove Nature Reserve has boardwalks through one of the healthiest mangrove stands on the Saudi coast. The corniche is well maintained, the beaches are good, and the Gulf here is warm and shallow. Families from Dammam and Al Khobar come north to Jubail's beaches on weekends specifically because they are less crowded than those around the larger cities.

The old fishing village of Al Jubail, absorbed into the modern city but still visible in the Al-Tawiya Tower and a few remaining traditional buildings, is a grounding reminder of what was here before the refineries arrived. And the Jubail Church, a 4th-century Christian site discovered during construction, is one of the most historically significant and least visited sites in Arabia.
