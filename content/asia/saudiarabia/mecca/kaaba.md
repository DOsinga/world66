  The Black Stone (al-Hajar al-Aswad) set into the eastern corner of the Kaaba is a meteorite fragment — or at least that is the scientific consensus, though its origin is a matter of religious significance rather than geological classification. It is kissed or pointed to by pilgrims at the start of each circuit of tawaf. The stone has been damaged, stolen, and repaired multiple times in its history: it was carried off by the Qarmatians in 930 CE and held for 22 years before being returned in fragments, which is why it now consists of eight pieces held together in a silver mount.

The Kaaba is the cube-shaped stone building at the centre of Masjid al-Haram and the most sacred structure in Islam. It measures approximately 13.5 metres high and 11.3 by 12.9 metres at the base. The building is draped in a black embroidered cloth (the kiswa) which is replaced annually during the Hajj season; the old kiswa is cut up and distributed to dignitaries and officials.

The interior of the Kaaba is only opened a few times per year for cleaning and for privileged guests of the Saudi royal family. Ordinary pilgrims perform tawaf — walking seven circuits around the Kaaba counterclockwise — in the course of both Hajj and Umrah. The circular movement of millions of pilgrims around the Kaaba is one of the most striking visual spectacles in the world; drone footage of the tawaf during peak Hajj season, with the circular movement of hundreds of thousands of white-clad pilgrims, is widely available and widely shared.
