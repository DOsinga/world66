  The Mina tent city is one of the largest temporary settlements on earth. During Hajj, approximately 2.5 million pilgrims live in an area of about 20 square kilometres for several days, in air-conditioned tents that replaced the original fabric tents in the late 20th century. The Saudi government has progressively upgraded the infrastructure — fireproof tents, electricity, water, waste management — following disasters including a catastrophic fire in 1997 that killed over 300 pilgrims. The crowd management challenges of moving millions of people safely between Mina, Muzdalifah, and Arafat remain one of the most complex logistical operations in the world.

Mina is a valley about 5 kilometres east of Mecca, used primarily during the Hajj pilgrimage. For most of the year it is virtually empty — a vast array of white tents that stand ready on permanent frames. During the five days of Hajj it becomes one of the most densely populated places on earth, as pilgrims camp here between the rituals at Arafat, Muzdalifah, and the Grand Mosque.

The central ritual at Mina is the stoning of the Jamarat — throwing pebbles at three stone pillars that symbolise the devil's temptation of Ibrahim (Abraham). Each pilgrim throws seven stones at each pillar on specific days of Hajj. The Jamarat Bridge was rebuilt as a multi-level structure to accommodate the millions of pilgrims who converge on the same location; the old single-level bridge was the site of several deadly crushes. The slaughter of animals during Eid al-Adha is also performed here, completing the Hajj rituals.
