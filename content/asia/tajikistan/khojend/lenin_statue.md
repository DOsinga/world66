  When Tajikistan became independent in 1991, most Soviet-era Lenin statues across
  the former USSR were toppled and scrapped. Khujand's Lenin — at 22 metres tall, the
  largest in Central Asia — was instead quietly moved from its central-square position
  to a park on the edge of town, where it stands today in splendid isolation,
  surrounded by empty space and a few park benches. It was simply too large and too
  expensive to destroy.

At 22 metres from base to fingertip, the Lenin statue in Khujand is the largest remaining statue of Lenin in Central Asia, a category of monument that was once common across the region but is now rapidly disappearing. Most post-Soviet governments demolished their Lenins within a few years of independence; Khujand's administration moved theirs instead, relocating the colossal figure from its original prominent position to a quieter park on the east side of the city.

The statue remains in good condition — the Soviet-era construction is solid — and is remarkable as a piece of Soviet monumental art as much as anything else. The base alone is several metres high, and Lenin's outstretched arm points toward a horizon that no longer holds the significance it once did. Local reaction to the statue is ambivalent: some residents regard it with genuine nostalgia, others with indifference. It attracts the occasional tourist curious to see what a genuinely large Soviet monument looks like in situ.
