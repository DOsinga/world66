story: >
  The Death Railway's official name was the Thailand–Burma Railway, and it was built between October 1942 and October 1943 — 415 kilometres of track through jungle and mountain terrain in thirteen months. The Japanese achieved this pace by working prisoners around the clock during the "Speedo" period of 1943, when guards were ordered to finish the line by the end of the year. Allied prisoners later calculated that roughly one man died for every sleeper laid on the most difficult sections. The Japanese estimate was lower; the true figure is still disputed.
---

The Thailand–Burma Railway Centre is the most comprehensive museum in Kanchanaburi covering the Death Railway, and a better starting point than the JEATH War Museum for visitors who want historical context before seeing the cemeteries. It is housed in a purpose-built building near the Kanchanaburi War Cemetery, with permanent exhibitions covering the construction of the railway from multiple perspectives: Allied prisoners, Asian labourers, Japanese engineers, and the Thai civilians who lived along the route.

The museum uses maps, photographs, personal diaries, letters, and physical artefacts to reconstruct what the construction involved — the terrain, the timetable, the conditions in the camps, and the medical consequences. A scale model of the railway's entire route gives a sense of the geographical ambition of the project. Particular attention is given to the Asian labourers, who are often overlooked in Western accounts of the Death Railway but died in far larger numbers than the Allied prisoners. Open daily; admission charged.
