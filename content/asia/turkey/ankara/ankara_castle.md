  The castle walls incorporate stone blocks removed from Roman temples, baths, and public buildings — ancient columns and carved fragments are visible embedded in the Byzantine masonry throughout the outer walls. This practice of re-using dressed stone from earlier structures was common across the medieval world, but at Ankara Castle you can see it with unusual clarity: bits of Roman Ancyra are literally built into the fortifications that replaced it.

The Ankara Castle (Ankara Kalesi) sits on a volcanic outcrop in Ulus, the oldest inhabited part of the city. The present walls are largely Byzantine, built in the 9th century on earlier foundations, with additions from the Seljuk and Ottoman periods. The castle is still partly inhabited — families live within the inner walls in traditional stone houses, some of which have been converted into restaurants, cafes, and small shops.

From the ramparts, the view over the city is excellent: old Ulus in the foreground, the wide modern avenues of the republican city beyond, and the hills stretching out to the horizon. The climb to the top is short and the interior of the outer walls, with its mixture of Byzantine stonework, Ottoman wooden houses, and cats, is one of the more atmospheric corners of Ankara. Allow an hour to explore.
