The Church of Mary, also known as the Double Church, is one of the oldest Christian basilicas in the world, converted from a Roman grain warehouse in the 2nd or 3rd century AD and expanded several times. Its particular historical significance lies in the Third Ecumenical Council of 431, which was held here and which settled the Nestorian controversy by declaring Mary to be the Mother of God — a decision that shaped Christian theology and eventually divided the Church.

The ruins are at the northern end of the Ephesus site, near the harbour baths. They are often overlooked by visitors focusing on the Library and the Theatre, but the setting among overgrown columns and apse walls is atmospheric. The successive building phases are visible in the masonry: Roman opus mixtum beneath the Christian additions, with later Byzantine repairs on top. A small museum sign near the entrance provides context on the Council of Ephesus.
