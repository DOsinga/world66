story: At its peak around 1000 AD, Ani was one of the largest cities in the world — larger than London or Paris at the time — and the capital of the Bagratid Armenian kingdom. Its decline was sudden and total: a Mongol invasion in 1236 was followed by earthquakes and the shift of trade routes away from the city, and by the 15th century it was largely abandoned. The combination of catastrophic history and physical isolation on the Turkish-Armenian border kept it frozen in time.
---

Ani sits on a windswept triangular plateau above the deep gorge of the Akhurian River, which now forms the border between Turkey and Armenia. The ruins cover more than 500 hectares and include the remains of churches, mosques, a citadel, caravanserais, and the great city walls that once enclosed a population rivalling Constantinople. The UNESCO World Heritage listing in 2016 brought increased attention and some conservation work, but the site remains relatively uncrowded.

The walk through the ruins is genuinely moving — the scale of what was lost is written into the landscape. The Cathedral of Ani (built 989–1001 AD) stands roofless but almost complete; the Church of the Redeemer is split clean in half by a lightning strike, its apse and part of the wall standing while the other half has collapsed entirely; the Church of St Gregory of the Tigran Honents contains faded but remarkable fresco cycles. The gorge views from the walls give a sense of why this spot was chosen. Allow at least three to four hours for a thorough visit.
