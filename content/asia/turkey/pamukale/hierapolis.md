  Hierapolis became a major Christian pilgrimage site after the martyrdom of the Apostle Philip here in around 80 AD. For over a century, archaeologists suspected a specific octagonal building on the plateau was his shrine but couldn't confirm it. In 2011, the Italian team excavating the site found coins and an inscription that definitively identified the structure as the Martyrium of Saint Philip — built to mark the spot of his execution. The find confirmed that Hierapolis had been one of the most important Christian holy sites in early Byzantium.

The ruins of Hierapolis spread across the plateau above the Pamukkale terraces, covering several square kilometres of a city founded by the Pergamene kings in the 2nd century BC and expanded by the Romans into a prosperous spa resort. The thermal springs that drew visitors then draw them now; the city's function as a place of healing and bathing gives it an unusual coherence as an archaeological site.

The main monuments include the [theatre](/asia/turkey/pamukale/hierapolis_theatre) — spectacularly preserved and worth climbing entirely — the enormous northern necropolis stretching along the road outside the walls, the colonnaded main street, the Byzantine basilica, the Domitian Gate, and the recently identified Martyrium of Saint Philip. The [Antique Pool](/asia/turkey/pamukale/antique_pool), where you can swim among fallen Roman columns in warm thermal water, and the [Ploutonion](/asia/turkey/pamukale/ploutonion) — the Gate of Pluto over a still-active CO2 vent — are two features found nowhere else in the ancient world. The [Hierapolis Archaeological Museum](/asia/turkey/pamukale/hierapolis_museum), housed in the restored Roman baths, holds sculpture from the site.
