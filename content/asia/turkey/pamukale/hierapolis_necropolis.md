  Hierapolis was famous as a spa city where the sick came to be healed. When they died — as many inevitably did — they were buried along the road outside the gates. The necropolis is so large precisely because of this: people came from across Asia Minor to seek cures, and those who did not recover were buried close to the sacred springs that had failed to heal them. The tombs of the wealthy are sarcophagi with elaborate carved portraits; the modest dead lie in simple tumuli barely visible above the grass.

The necropolis of Hierapolis stretches for nearly two kilometres along the road north of the city gates, with over a thousand tombs dating from the Hellenistic period through the late Roman and early Byzantine eras. It is one of the largest and best-preserved ancient cemeteries in the Mediterranean world, and easily the most overlooked part of the Hierapolis-Pamukkale site.

The tombs range from simple earthen tumuli to elaborate family sarcophagi carved with portraits and mythological reliefs, to small house-tombs with their doors still in place. Many still have their inscriptions intact, giving names, professions, and the occasional threat of a fine against anyone who disturbs the burial. Walking the road through the necropolis — away from the crowds at the theatre and terraces — takes about 45 minutes and gives a more intimate sense of the Roman city than any other part of the site.
