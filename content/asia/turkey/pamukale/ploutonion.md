  Strabo described the Ploutonion in the 1st century BC: a cave entrance where bulls led inside died immediately, while priests emerged unharmed. The explanation, unknown to the ancients, is that the cave releases volcanic CO2 which, being heavier than air, pools at ground level in a lethal layer. Priests stood above it; animals sniffed it. The site disappeared under rubble and was lost until 2013, when Italian archaeologists matched a marble inscription to Strabo's description and found the vent still active, still killing birds that venture too close.

In a corner of the Hierapolis plateau, partly hidden by other ruins, the Ploutonion is one of the most genuinely strange ancient sites in Turkey. It is a small sanctuary — a marble gate, broken columns of a small temple, a pool fed by the spring — built over a natural crack in the rock that still releases a steady flow of volcanic carbon dioxide. The CO2 is odourless, invisible, and heavier than air, so it settles in a shallow layer at the base of the enclosure. Anything that crouches near the rock — birds, rodents, incautious tourists — is silently poisoned.

The ancient Greeks and Romans understood the effect perfectly well as a divine attribute: Pluto's breath. Priests of Cybele who worked at the sanctuary apparently held their own breath as they walked through the cloud, or timed their movements carefully to avoid it — the trick was not supernatural immunity but controlled breathing. A viewing platform allows visitors to observe the gate and pool safely. Dead birds and insects are sometimes visible around the vent. The site is a short walk from the Antique Pool and is included in the main site ticket.
