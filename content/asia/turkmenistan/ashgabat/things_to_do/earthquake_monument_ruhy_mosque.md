---
title: "Earthquake Monument and Ruhy Mosque"
type: poi
---

On the night of 5–6 October 1948, an earthquake measuring 7.3 on the Richter scale destroyed Ashgabat almost completely. Soviet estimates put the death toll at 14,000; independent historians estimate between 110,000 and 176,000 dead — most of the city's population. Stalin's government suppressed the true scale of the disaster for decades, and the earthquake remained a state secret in the Soviet Union. The young Saparmurat Niyazov survived it as a child, losing his mother and brothers; the earthquake and his orphan origin story became central pillars of his later personality cult mythology.

The earthquake monument near the city centre is a bronze bull supporting the earth on its back, with a golden figure of a mother holding a child aloft — the child, by no accident, bearing resemblance to the young Turkmenbashi. The monument is both genuine memorial and political theatre, and that duality is worth sitting with. The site is architecturally striking and historically significant, and the story it tells — of survival, national trauma, and power — is inseparable from everything else you see in Ashgabat.

The Ruhy Mosque, a short distance from the monument, is one of the largest mosques in Central Asia, white-marbled and gold-domed, built under Niyazov's direction and named after his mother. It can accommodate 10,000 worshippers. It is not an ancient mosque but a state-commissioned one, and the distinctions between religious and political purpose blur throughout the complex. Non-Muslim visitors are usually admitted with appropriate dress and a guide.
