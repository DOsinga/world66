Emperor Ly Thai To founded Hanoi — then called Thang Long, "ascending dragon" — in 1010, and the Imperial Citadel was the heart of his capital. The site served as the imperial seat through the Ly, Tran, and Le dynasties, a continuous political and ceremonial center for nearly eight centuries. What survives today is a fraction of what existed — the Doan Mon gate, the Kinh Thien palace foundation, and the Hau Lau tower are the main above-ground remains, surrounded by gardens and excavation pits where archaeologists have been finding artifacts since the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2010.

The citadel compound is large enough to spend two hours in comfortably. The archaeological museum in the northern section displays ceramics, bricks, and architectural fragments from each dynastic period, arranged so you can see how materials and styles changed across the centuries. The D67 command bunker is accessible separately; ask at the ticket desk for tour times. The citadel grounds have the unusual quality of being genuinely quiet in the middle of a busy city — there are shade trees, archaeological dig sites you can peer into, and open space that's rare in central Hanoi.
