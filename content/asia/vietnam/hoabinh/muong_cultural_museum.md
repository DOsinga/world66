This small private museum, set on about two hectares of hillside about seven kilometres from the city centre, is the most serious attempt anyone has made to document Muong culture before it disappears. It was built by a Hanoi painter, Vu Duc Hieu, who spent years collecting artefacts from villages across Hoa Binh province and eventually constructed the museum himself. It opened in 2007.

The collection runs to around 5,000 items: bronze drums, gongs, pottery, weaving tools, agricultural implements, traditional costumes, and household objects spanning several centuries of Muong life. Four full-size stilt houses have been reconstructed on the grounds, arranged to reflect the old feudal social hierarchy — the nobleman's house, the craftsman's house, the farmer's house. The result is less a conventional museum than a preserved village, and it gives a vivid sense of what daily life looked like before the reservoir changed everything. The Muong are Vietnam's fourth-largest ethnic group, with about 1.3 million people, and this is the only museum in the country devoted to their culture.

story: >
  The bronze drum on display is believed to be over a thousand years old. In Muong culture, drums were not musical instruments in the ordinary sense — they were ritual objects owned by the village lord, beaten only at funerals and festivals, their sound understood to travel between the world of the living and the world of the dead.
