Phu Quoc is Vietnam's largest island, a 50-km-long teardrop of jungle and beaches lying in the Gulf of Thailand near the Cambodian border. Until the late 2000s it was a minor fishing outpost; since then it has been transformed by an airport, luxury resorts, and a casino-packed development zone in the south into one of Southeast Asia's most visited beach destinations. The transformation has been rapid and uneven — the north of the island remains mostly forested national park, the west coast has some excellent beaches, and the south is a construction zone of international hotel brands.

The west coast beaches — Long Beach south of Duong Dong town, and the quieter strips further north at Ong Lang and Cua Can — are the main draw. The water in the Gulf of Thailand is generally calmer and warmer than the South China Sea, and the sunsets here are spectacular. The [Phu Quoc National Park](/asia/vietnam/phuquoc/phu_quoc_national_park) covers roughly a third of the island's northern end, with the primary jungle largely intact. The [Phu Quoc Prison](/asia/vietnam/phuquoc/phu_quoc_prison), a museum operated on the site of the brutal Con Dao-style prison used by the South Vietnamese government and US forces, is the island's most historically significant site.

Phu Quoc is also known for two local products that are exported nationwide: nuoc mam (fish sauce), produced in large factories on the island's east coast using a centuries-old fermentation process, and Phu Quoc pepper, the black pepper variety cultivated on the island's interior that has earned a European geographical indication. The [fish sauce factories](/asia/vietnam/phuquoc/fish_sauce_factory) north of Duong Dong give tours, and the sharp, deeply savoury local sauce tastes different from what you find elsewhere in Vietnam.

Getting here is easy: Phu Quoc International Airport (PQC) has direct connections from Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, and a growing number of international destinations. The best time to visit is November to April in the dry season; from May to October, the southwest monsoon brings rain and rougher seas.
