story: >
  Hoq Cave contains ancient inscriptions in multiple scripts — South Arabian, Indian Brāhmī, Ethiopic, Palmyrene, and Greek — left by sailors and traders who sheltered here or made offerings over a period stretching from roughly the 1st century BCE to the 6th century CE. The inscriptions are evidence of Socotra's position on the ancient maritime trade routes linking the Mediterranean with India and East Africa. The cave was essentially a sailors' shrine for six centuries.
---

Hoq Cave is one of the largest cave systems in the Arabian Peninsula, a 3-kilometre deep system in the limestone cliffs of Socotra's northeastern coast. Visitors can explore up to about 1 kilometre of the cave on foot, where massive stalactite and stalagmite formations rise in chambers tall enough to walk through without stooping. The cave maintains a humid, cool environment very different from the arid island above.

The walk to the cave entrance involves a steep climb up the coastal escarpment, with views along the rocky northern coast. A local guide is necessary; the cave is not marked and the interior is genuinely dark. The ancient inscriptions near the entrance are a remarkable side attraction for anyone interested in maritime history — they represent one of the most remarkable multilingual assemblages of ancient graffiti in existence.
