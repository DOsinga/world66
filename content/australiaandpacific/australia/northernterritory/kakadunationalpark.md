Kakadu National Park is the great wilderness east of [Darwin](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/darwin): a huge World Heritage landscape of floodplains, sandstone escarpments, monsoon forest, crocodile rivers, and Aboriginal rock art. It is not a quick zoo-style nature stop. Distances are long, seasons matter, and the park rewards travellers who give it time.

The classic first-time route links the rock-art country of [Ubirr](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/kakadunationalpark/ubirr) and [Burrungkuy (Nourlangie)](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/kakadunationalpark/burrungkuy_nourlangie), the visitor services around Jabiru and Cooinda, and the wetlands of [Yellow Water Billabong](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/kakadunationalpark/yellow_water_billabong). If you have a 4WD and the roads are open, the southern waterfalls such as [Jim Jim Falls](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/kakadunationalpark/jim_jim_falls) and [Maguk](/australiaandpacific/australia/northernterritory/kakadunationalpark/maguk) add a wilder edge.

Kakadu is Aboriginal land, jointly managed with Parks Australia, and many places are living cultural sites rather than open-air galleries. Read signs, stay on marked paths, and do not photograph where notices ask you not to. The rock art is astonishing, but the strongest impression is the continuity: people have lived with this country for tens of thousands of years.

Most travellers visit from Darwin by rental car, campervan, or tour. A rushed day trip is possible but misses the point. Two or three days lets you see the main sealed-road sights; longer gives you room for seasonal wetlands, walks, and the harder-to-reach waterfalls.
