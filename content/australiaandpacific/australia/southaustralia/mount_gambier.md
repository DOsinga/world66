Mount Gambier is South Australia's second largest city, set in a landscape shaped by volcanic forces — craters, lakes, sinkholes, and limestone caves riddle the ground beneath the townsite and for kilometres around. The city sits on the slopes of a dormant maar volcano, and its most famous resident is the Blue Lake: a deep crater lake that turns a vivid, almost supernatural cobalt blue each November and stays that way until March, then quietly shifts back to grey. Nobody fully understands why.

Halfway between Adelaide and Melbourne on the Princes Highway, Mount Gambier makes a natural stopping point — but it rewards those who stay a day or two rather than just passing through. The surrounding Limestone Coast produces some of Australia's most respected cool-climate wines, especially Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, and the karst landscape offers unusual experiences you won't find elsewhere on the continent: diving in flooded caves, watching the lake change colour, wandering a sunken garden inside a city sinkhole.

The city itself is compact and easy to navigate on foot. The main commercial strip runs along Commercial Street, and the Cave Garden — a public park built around a genuine collapsed cave system — sits right in the centre of town. The region was first recorded by European eyes in December 1800 when Lieutenant James Grant of HMS Lady Nelson named the mountain for Admiral Lord James Gambier. Settlement followed decades later when Hastings Cunningham subdivided the first town lots in 1854.

The Boandik people are the traditional custodians of this country, and their connection to the volcanic landscape — particularly the Blue Lake, which they call Warwar — runs far deeper than European history here.
