  Of the 32 men hanged in the chapel-turned-execution-yard between 1856 and 1946, most were convicts and many were Aboriginal Tasmanians caught up in the violence of frontier settlement. The bodies were buried in unmarked graves under the floor of the adjacent quadrangle. The site was a working penal complex from 1831 until 1983, when the last court session ended — an unbroken 150 years of incarceration and punishment.

A sandstone complex on the rise above the CBD that began life in 1831 as a convict chapel, was converted to a women's prison and then a men's gaol, and ended as the Supreme Court of Tasmania. The dim, vaulted **underground tunnels** connect the cells to the courts and to the execution yard — guides take you through them by lamplight and the story is genuinely grim.

The standard 90-minute tour is excellent — knowledgeable, slow, unsensational. The evening **Ghost Tour** runs Thursday to Sunday and is the popular option (and a good one even for sceptics). The site is run by the National Trust and the ticket includes the courtroom, the chapel, and the cells.
