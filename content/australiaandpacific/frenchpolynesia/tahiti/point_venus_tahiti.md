Point Venus is a flat, narrow promontory on Tahiti's north coast, about 10 km east of Papeete, extending into the lagoon to create a sheltered bay with calm, swimmable water. A white lighthouse (built 1868) marks the point; a small monument commemorates Cook's 1769 observation; black-sand beaches run along the outer shore.

Captain James Cook first stopped here during his first Pacific voyage, on his way to the transit observation for which the point is named. He returned twice more (1773 and 1777), establishing Tahiti as the standard Pacific landfall for European explorers. The Bounty also stopped here in 1789 to collect breadfruit seedlings — the mission that ended in the famous mutiny just weeks after departure.

The black sand is volcanic in origin — not the white tropical sand of other Pacific destinations, but dark and coarse, which surprises first-time visitors. The beach is popular with local families on weekends and remains one of the better swimming spots north of Papeete. The short walk to the lighthouse tip and back gives good views over the lagoon, the reef, and the mountains behind.

A modest café and craft stalls operate near the parking area. Getting here by public transport is possible on the coastal bus from Papeete.
