  Majuro Atoll encloses one of the largest lagoons in the Pacific — about 300 square kilometres of water, yet nowhere deeper than about 50 metres. Traditional Marshallese navigators read the lagoon's currents, swells, and star positions to sail between atolls without instruments, using a system of stick charts that mapped the wave patterns rather than geographic outlines.
---

The Majuro Lagoon is the defining feature of the atoll and the best reason to linger here beyond a cursory visit. The water is extraordinarily clear and the coral, though affected by bleaching events in recent decades, still holds enough colour and marine life to make snorkelling worthwhile. Several guesthouses and dive operators can arrange boat trips out to better sections of the reef.

Kayaking across the lagoon from the D-U-D shore is one of the most pleasant ways to spend a morning in Majuro. You cover distance quickly in calm conditions, and the view back at the low-lying islets — barely a metre or two above sea level — brings home the fragility of these islands in a way that no text can convey. The lagoon is also a working waterway: fishing canoes and supply boats move through it constantly, and the contrast between the ancient atoll environment and the modern port is one of Majuro's most striking qualities.
