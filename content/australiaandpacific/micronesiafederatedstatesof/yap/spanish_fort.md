The Pope's 1885 ruling gave Spain sovereignty but granted Germany trading rights, a compromise that satisfied no one and lasted barely a decade. After Spain's defeat in the Spanish-American War of 1898, Yap passed to Germany for 25 million pesetas. The fort the Spanish built to assert their claim still stands, a reminder that even remote Pacific islands were once worth fighting over.

The Spanish Fort near Colonia is a modest but evocative set of ruins from one of the odder episodes in Pacific colonial history. Spain claimed Yap in the 1870s and 1880s as part of the broader Spanish East Indies administration centred on Manila, and constructed this fortification to give the claim a physical presence. The stonework, though weathered, gives a clear sense of the original layout — a small defensive position intended more to impress potential competitors than to withstand any serious military assault.

The fort is an easy visit from Colonia and rewards a slow walk around the perimeter. Plaques explain the colonial sequence — Spain, then Germany, then Japan, then the United States — that Yap passed through in the century before Micronesian independence. The views from the fort's elevated position look out across the harbour and give a sense of why this location was chosen. It is not a dramatic ruin, but it is a genuinely historical one, and the story behind it — a papal arbitration over a Pacific outpost — is stranger than anything most travel guides bother to tell you.
