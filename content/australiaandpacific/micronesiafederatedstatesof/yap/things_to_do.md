title: "Things to Do"
type: section
---

Yap punches well above its size when it comes to things worth doing. The island has three claims on your attention that are genuinely hard to match anywhere else in the world: the stone money banks scattered through the villages, the manta rays that cruise Mi'l Channel year-round, and a living traditional culture that has survived with more integrity than almost anywhere in the Pacific.

The stone money — giant limestone discs called Rai, some taller than a person — stands in rows beside village roads and meetinghouses across the island. These are not museum pieces. They are still legally recognised as currency, still transferred as part of marriages and settlements, still owned by families who can trace each disc's history back generations. Walking past a village stone money bank, you are looking at an active financial system that predates the Euro by several centuries.

Diving in Yap is defined by mantas. Mi'l Channel, a tidal channel off the southwestern coast, is a cleaning station where reef manta rays gather in numbers that can reach thirty at a single sitting. The mantas come year-round — this is one of the few places on earth where that is true — and they are habituated to divers, moving through the channel in slow, unhurried circles while cleaner wrasses work their gills. The diving here is gentle enough for beginners but extraordinary enough to draw experts from across the world.

On land, the Yap Living History Museum and the traditional villages of the island's interior offer a window into a social structure — complete with men's meetinghouses, caste hierarchies, and ceremonial dances — that remains genuinely intact. The Spanish and German colonial eras left their marks too, most visibly in the ruins of the Spanish fort near Colonia, and in the story of David O'Keefe, the Irish-American trader whose island home became the base of a strange private empire built on stone money.
