  The "pancakes" are limestone layers formed around 30 million years ago from fragments of 
  dead marine creatures that fell to the ocean floor. The layering occurred because 
  alternating beds of soft and hard material were compressed differently over geological 
  time — the soft layers eroded faster, leaving the distinctive stacked look. When the 
  tide comes in during a southerly swell, seawater is forced through the blowholes with 
  sufficient pressure to send columns of spray 10 metres into the air.

Pancake Rocks at Punakaiki, about 50 kilometres south of Westport, are the most visited natural attraction on New Zealand's West Coast — and one of the more extraordinary geological formations in the country. The limestone has been eroded into distinct horizontal layers that look precisely like stacked pancakes, rising from the sea in formations up to fifteen metres tall along a dramatic stretch of coastline.

The walkway from the Punakaiki visitor centre loops through the rock formations in about 30 minutes. At high tide in rough weather, the blowholes between the rocks fire jets of spray into the air with considerable force. Check the tide times before you visit: the blowholes are inactive at low tide. The surrounding Paparoa National Park has further walking tracks into the limestone karst country inland.
