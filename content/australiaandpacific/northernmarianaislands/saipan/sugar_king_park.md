Matsue Haruji arrived on Saipan in 1921 and, through the South Seas Development Company, turned much of the island into sugarcane fields and built a processing mill that made the island economically significant within the Japanese colonial empire. By the 1930s, Saipan was producing a substantial share of Japan's domestic sugar. The bronze statue in the park that now bears his name was erected after the war by Japanese and local citizens who credited him with developing the island's infrastructure. It remains standing today, a somewhat ambiguous monument given that the colonial development he oversaw also displaced Chamorro landowners.

The park itself is modest — a few trees, benches, and the statue — but it sits in the middle of Garapan and provides context for the Japanese colonial history that shapes the island's architecture and layout. The rusted remnants of the sugar mill's narrow-gauge railway can be found in various places around the island. For anyone interested in the pre-war period, the park is a logical stop alongside the NMI Museum of History and Culture a short walk away.
