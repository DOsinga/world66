Koror is Palau's main island and the country's commercial heart — the place where nearly everyone stays, eats, and gears up for the underwater adventures that make Palau famous. It is a compact, walkable town with a mix of Japanese colonial relics, concrete shophouses, and dive shops lining the main road. Don't mistake busyness for charm: Koror is a functional base, not a destination in itself.

The real draw is what surrounds it. The [Rock Islands](/australiaandpacific/palau/rockislands) stretch to the south — some 200 limestone outcroppings draped in jungle, with lagoons of implausible turquoise. The giant island of [Babeldaob](/australiaandpacific/palau/babeldoab) lies just to the north, connected by the Compact Road and largely undeveloped. Most divers base themselves in Koror and go out by boat each morning.

Within Koror, the [Belau National Museum](/australiaandpacific/palau/koror/belau_national_museum) is worth an hour of your time. It has a solid collection covering Palauan cultural history, traditional storyboards, and the islands' complicated colonial past — ruled in turn by Spain, Germany, Japan, and the United States before independence in 1994. The nearby [Etpison Museum](/australiaandpacific/palau/koror/etpison_museum) has more artefacts and some good underwater photography. The [Palau International Coral Reef Center](/australiaandpacific/palau/koror/palau_international_coral_reef_center) is a useful primer before any dive trip, and the jungle-overgrown ruins of the [Nan'yō Shrine](/australiaandpacific/palau/koror/nanyo_shrine) are a quiet reminder of the long Japanese colonial period. Day trips to [Jellyfish Lake](/australiaandpacific/palau/koror/jellyfish_lake) leave from the Malakal marina, which is also the liveliest part of town for nightlife.
