  The langi at Lapaha were built without metal tools or wheeled vehicles. The coralline limestone slabs — some weighing several tonnes — were cut from reef platforms using stone and coral tools, dragged to the site on log rollers, and fitted together with a precision that has kept them standing for centuries. How exactly this was achieved, and how the slabs were transported, remains disputed among archaeologists.
---

The Lapaha complex at Mu'a contains 28 langi — the tiered burial mounds of the ancient Tu'i Tonga rulers, built between roughly the 11th and 17th centuries. These are the most significant prehistoric monuments in Tonga and among the most important archaeological sites in the Pacific. The mounds are constructed from massive slabs of coralline limestone, fitted together without mortar, and are remarkably well-preserved given their age.

The largest mound, the Paepae 'o Tele'a, was built for the 28th Tu'i Tonga and rises in three tiers from the ground. The Namoala stands nearby — a three-tiered pyramid with a stone burial vault still intact at the top. Opposite is the Hehea mound, which contains two further burial vaults. The whole complex sits among modern Tongan houses and gardens with no fence or formal visitor centre; you simply walk up and look.
