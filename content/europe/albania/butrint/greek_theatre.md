The Greek theatre at Butrint is one of the most atmospheric in the Balkans. Cut into the hillside in the 3rd century BC, it seats perhaps 1,500 and has survived remarkably well — the lower cavea retains most of its stone seating, the orchestra circle is clearly legible, and the stage building, though ruined, still frames the view toward the lake.

The Romans altered the theatre in the 2nd century AD, converting the orchestra into a pit for aquatic spectacles by channelling in water — a lavish Roman innovation that strikes modern visitors as both ingenious and barbaric. Inscriptions carved into the seats record the names of donors who paid for specific rows, a practice common to Greek civic life.
