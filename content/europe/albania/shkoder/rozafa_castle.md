  The castle's name comes from a legend in which the builders kept finding their walls collapsing overnight. A wise man told them they needed to wall a person alive into the foundations. Three brothers made a pact: whoever's wife brought lunch the following day would be the sacrifice. Two of the brothers warned their wives; only the third kept the secret. His wife, Rozafa, agreed to be immured — but asked that her right hand, her right breast, and her right eye be left free so she could continue to nurse her infant son. Rozafa Castle occupies a dramatic rocky hill at the confluence of three rivers — the Drin, the Buna, and the Kir — three kilometres south of the city centre. The site has been fortified since at least the 4th century BC, when it was an Illyrian stronghold. The Venetians substantially rebuilt it in the 15th century, the Ottomans held it for four centuries, and it changed hands repeatedly during the wars of the early 20th century.

The walls are extensive and mostly intact, with towers, gates, and interior buildings in various states of ruin and restoration. A small museum inside the castle holds coins, weapons, and artefacts from across the site's long history. The views are the main reason to come: the rivers spreading out below, the mountains of Montenegro to the north, Lake Shkodër catching the light to the west. Allow at least two hours. The access road is driveable or you can walk up from the roundabout in about 20 minutes.
