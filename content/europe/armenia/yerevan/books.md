A few books worth packing for a trip to Yerevan or reading afterwards to make sense of what you have seen.

**The Forty Days of Musa Dagh** by Franz Werfel. The 1933 epic novel of Armenian villagers resisting the Ottoman deportations of 1915 — controversial when published, banned in the Third Reich, and still the single best literary entry point into the Genocide that shapes so much of modern Armenian identity.

**Black Garden** by Thomas de Waal. The clearest history of the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict, which has dominated Armenian politics since independence. Indispensable for understanding the present-day relationship between Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Russia.

**Rebirth: A Story of Love, Loss and the Promise of Armenia** by Sam Mardirossian. A diasporan memoir of moving from California to Yerevan in the 1990s — useful on the texture of post-Soviet daily life and the complicated relationship between the diaspora and the homeland.

**My Name Is Aram** by William Saroyan. Stories of Armenian-American family life in California from the great 20th-century Armenian-American writer. Not set in Yerevan, but Saroyan's voice — half of his ashes are buried at the Komitas Pantheon — is half of what the city's literary culture is built on.

**Goodbye, Antoura** by Karnig Panian. A memoir of an Armenian boy's survival of the Genocide through a Turkish orphanage in Lebanon. Short, direct, and unforgettable.
