Agstafa is a small provincial town in the far west of Azerbaijan, near the borders with Georgia and Armenia. It was founded in 1914 as Elisavetinka, essentially as a railway junction — the line from Baku splits here, one branch running north to Tbilisi via Rustavi and another running south-east to Yerevan via Qazax and Dilijan.

The Soviet collapse and the war with Armenia made Agstafa's role much smaller than it was meant to be. The rail and road connections to Armenia have been shut for thirty years and there is no sign of them re-opening soon. The Armenian border is similarly closed, but you can cross to Georgia at **Krasny Most** — Russian for *Red Bridge*, named for the 17th-century brick arch bridge on the Khrami river. The old bridge still stands; since 1998 the main crossing has moved a short distance to the modern TRACECA bridge built with EU funding.

Today Agstafa is a quiet town. In the centre you will find the pleasant **Samad Vurgun park** with restaurants, tea houses, and a cinema. Trains and buses run to **Baku** (overnight train or daytime bus) and *marshrutkas* go to **Qazax** and beyond. There is a small but comfortable hotel near the station on 20th January Street.

A geographic curiosity: just west of Agstafa, Azerbaijan has two tiny exclaves inside Armenian territory (Yukhari Askipara and Barkhudali, both under Armenian control), and a piece of Armenian land — the exclave of Artsvashen, south-west of Shamkir — sits inside Azerbaijan. Azerbaijan took Artsvashen in August 1992; the Askipara situation remains unresolved. It is the kind of Soviet-drawn border legacy you can drive across without noticing.

Agstafa is about 380 km north-west of Baku.
