Minsk, the capital of Belarus, is unlike any other European capital. The city was 80% destroyed in World War II — one of the most complete urban annihilations of the entire conflict — and rebuilt almost entirely from scratch between 1945 and 1955 according to Stalin’s personal vision of what a Soviet city should look like. The result is a masterpiece of Stalinist urban planning: wide ceremonial avenues, monumental neoclassical facades, vast squares, and a scale that is simultaneously impressive and slightly bewildering. The Stalinist city centre was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

[Independence Avenue](/europe/belarus/minsk/independence_avenue) — Praspiekt Niezaliezhnastsi — is the spine of the city, running six kilometres from the main railway station through [October Square](/europe/belarus/minsk/october_square) and the [Palace of the Republic](/europe/belarus/minsk/palace_of_republic) past the [KGB headquarters](/europe/belarus/minsk/kgb_building) to the Academy of Sciences. Walking it end to end takes about 90 minutes and gives you the full measure of what postwar Soviet architecture intended to accomplish. The [Great Patriotic War Museum](/europe/belarus/minsk/great_patriotic_war_museum), in a striking modern building near the Hero City obelisk, is one of the most significant WWII museums in Europe.

Beyond the monumental set pieces, the city has genuine warmth. [Troitsky Predmestye](/europe/belarus/minsk/troitsky_predmestye), the tiny reconstructed old town by the river, has cafes and galleries on a human scale. The [Isle of Tears](/europe/belarus/minsk/isle_of_tears), a memorial to Belarusian soldiers who died in Afghanistan, is one of the most quietly affecting memorials in Eastern Europe. The [Janka Kupala National Theatre](/europe/belarus/minsk/janka_kupala_theatre), the oldest in Belarus, is a living piece of Belarusian cultural identity that has stubbornly resisted Russification. For the historically curious, the apartment where Lee Harvey Oswald lived for three years after defecting to the Soviet Union is still [findable and plaque-marked](/europe/belarus/minsk/oswald_apartment), and the house where the Communist Party was effectively founded in 1898 is now a [small museum](/europe/belarus/minsk/first_congress_site).

The [Komarovsky Market](/europe/belarus/minsk/komarovsky_market) under its Soviet dome is the best place to eat and shop like a local. The [National Art Museum](/europe/belarus/minsk/national_art_museum) has 27,000 works and an underrated Belarusian art collection. The [National Library](/europe/belarus/minsk/national_library) is a giant faceted building that lights up at night. And the [Maly Trostenets](/europe/belarus/minsk/maly_trostenets) memorial on the city’s edge is one of the most significant Holocaust sites in Europe — less visited than it deserves and more important than most people know.
