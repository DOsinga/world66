  The Last Post ceremony has been performed here at 8pm every day since 1928
  — with one interruption. During the German occupation in the Second World
  War the ceremony was halted. It resumed on 6 September 1944, the very
  evening that the Polish 1st Armoured Division liberated Ypres. That night
  the ceremony was performed in the presence of the liberating troops before
  the echoes of the fighting had fully died away.
---

Every evening at 8pm, traffic on the road through the Menin Gate stops. The police halt the cars. Four buglers from the Last Post Association raise their instruments and play — as they have every evening since 11 November 1929 — in memory of the soldiers who passed under this gate on their way to the front and never came back.

The gate itself is a triumphal arch straddling the road on the eastern edge of the town. Its walls are covered, every panel of them, with the names of 54,896 Commonwealth soldiers killed in the Ypres Salient who have no known grave. These are not all the missing — there were too many names to fit — but they represent the scale of what the Salient consumed. Field Marshal Lord Plumer, at the dedication in 1927, tried to explain: "Here are the names of those who died and have no known grave. They are not missing; they are here."

The ceremony lasts about ten minutes. Come early to find a good position — the gate fills quickly on summer evenings and weekends. Afterwards, many people remain for a while, reading the names.
