Bosnia and Herzegovina occupies the heart of the western Balkans, a mountainous country of deep river canyons, Ottoman-era bazaars, and a recent history that colours everything. The 1990s war left visible scars — bullet-pocked facades still stand in [Sarajevo](/europe/bosniaandherzegovina/sarajevo) alongside rebuilt mosques and rebuilt cafés — but the country has re-emerged as one of the most compelling and least-touristed destinations in Europe.

[Sarajevo](/europe/bosniaandherzegovina/sarajevo) is the capital and heart of the country: a city where mosques, Orthodox churches, a Catholic cathedral, and a synagogue stand within a few hundred metres of each other, legacy of centuries of Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, and Yugoslav rule. The Baščaršija bazaar quarter is one of the most atmospheric old quarters in Europe. The 1984 Winter Olympics were held here; so was the assassination of Franz Ferdinand in 1914 that triggered the First World War.

[Mostar](/europe/bosniaandherzegovina/mostar), in the south, draws visitors to the reconstructed Stari Most bridge — the original 16th-century Ottoman bridge was destroyed in 1993 and rebuilt in 2004. The old town around it is genuinely beautiful, though increasingly crowded. Further south, [Trebinje](/europe/bosniaandherzegovina/trebinje) is a quieter, largely Serbian city with a good wine region nearby. [Jajce](/europe/bosniaandherzegovina/jajce) has a remarkable waterfall where the Pliva meets the Vrbas, right in the town centre.

The country is cheap by European standards, the hiking in the Dinaric Alps is excellent, and the food — grilled meats (*ćevapi*, *pljeskavica*), börek pastry, and strong Bosnian coffee served in small džezvas — is worth the trip on its own.
