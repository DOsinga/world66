Istria is the large triangular peninsula at the northern end of the Croatian Adriatic — a landscape of small hill towns, vineyards, oak forest, and a 450-kilometre indented coastline. It is the closest piece of Croatia to Italy, and the relationship shows: bilingual road signs, Italianate cuisine, and a string of Venetian-era towns along the western coast.

The west coast holds most of the visitor destinations. [Pula](/europe/croatia/pula) at the southern tip has a complete Roman amphitheatre that still hosts summer concerts. North along the coast are the Venetian harbours of [Rovinj](/europe/croatia/rovinj) — the photogenic baroque peninsula — and [Poreč](/europe/croatia/porec), whose 6th-century Euphrasian Basilica is a UNESCO site for its golden Byzantine mosaics. Further north sit [Umag](/europe/croatia/umag), [Novigrad](/europe/croatia/novigrad), and [Fažana](/europe/croatia/fazana), the gateway to the [Brijuni Islands](/europe/croatia/brioniislands) — Tito's former private island playground, now a national park.

The interior is quieter and very rewarding. Walled hill towns sit on isolated ridges: [Motovun](/europe/croatia/motovun) above the truffle forests of the Mirna valley, the smaller [Žminj](/europe/croatia/zminj), Grožnjan and Buzet. The Mirna and Raša valleys are serious wine country — *Malvazija Istarska* is the white to look for, *Teran* the red — and the white-truffle hunting season runs from late September to January. Inland from Pula, the regional centre of Pazin guards an extraordinary karst gorge that swallows its river.

For travellers, Istria works either as a beach base on the coast — Rovinj and Poreč are the obvious choices — or as a slower exploration of the wine villages, hill towns, and gastronomic stops in the interior. It is one of the most consistently rewarding parts of Croatia, less overwhelmed than Dalmatia and easier to reach than the islands.
