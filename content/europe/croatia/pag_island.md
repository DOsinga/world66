Pag is a long, narrow island in the north-Dalmatian archipelago, stretching some 60 kilometres between the Kvarner Gulf and the Velebit Channel. It is the fifth-largest island in Croatia, yet it feels worlds apart from the lush green islands further south. The landscape is stark and windswept — bare karst rock sculpted by the bora wind that howls down from the Velebit mountains, interrupted by pockets of green where salt-tolerant grasses and olive groves cling on.

What draws visitors is a combination of the unexpected. Pag is famous for its [sheep's cheese (*paški sir*)](/europe/croatia/pag_island/pag_cheese), sharp and salty from the herbs the sheep graze on and the sea spray that seasons the pastures. The [Lun olive groves](/europe/croatia/pag_island/lun_olive_groves) at the island's northern tip contain trees over 1,500 years old. And [Novalja and Zrće Beach](/europe/croatia/pag_island/novalja_and_zrce) — one of Europe's best-known open-air clubbing destinations — present an entirely different side of the island.

[Pag Town](/europe/croatia/pag_island/pag_town) is worth a wander: designed in the 15th century by the architect Juraj Dalmatinac, it retains its Renaissance grid-plan layout with a Gothic church and a museum devoted to the local lace-making tradition (*paška čipka*), inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list. For the curious, the [Pag Triangle](/europe/croatia/pag_island/pag_triangle) near Caska is a mysterious landform of unknown origin visible from the air.

The island is accessible year-round via the Pag Bridge from the mainland near Zadar, or by ferry from Prizna to Žigljen. In summer, the bridge road can get busy — arrive early or late to avoid the queue.
