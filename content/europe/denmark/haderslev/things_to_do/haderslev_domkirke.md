---
title: "Haderslev Domkirke"
type: poi
---

Haderslev Cathedral — Domkirken — is the defining building of the town and one of the more unusual Gothic churches in Denmark. Built in red brick from the 13th century onwards, it acquired cathedral status only in 1922 when the newly reunified South Jutland received its own diocese. The building is notable for its twin western towers, which give it a more monumental presence than most Danish parish churches of comparable age, and for the way the Gothic choir and nave were extended and adapted across several centuries without losing coherence.

Inside, the cathedral retains a number of medieval features despite the upheavals of the Reformation. The baptismal font dates from the 12th century and the carved altarpiece is among the finest in the region. The high vaulted nave is bright and austere in the Danish Lutheran tradition. During the years when Haderslev was the Prussian town of Hadersleben (1864–1920), the cathedral served the Danish-speaking congregation and became something of a focal point for cultural identity.

The cathedral stands at the top of the old town, a short climb from the market square, and its towers are visible from many points around the lake. There are regular organ concerts in summer, and the building is free to enter.
