story: >
  In the winter of 1808, Napoleon's Spanish troops garrisoned at Koldinghus lit fires inside the building to keep warm during a bitter Danish cold snap. The fires got out of control and gutted the interior, leaving only the outer walls standing. The burnt-out ruin stood open to the wind and rain for nearly 200 years before the Danish government commissioned the architect Inger and Johannes Exner to restore it in the 1970s — a project that deliberately inserted raw concrete and steel into the medieval shell rather than attempting reconstruction.
---

Koldinghus is the most striking building in Kolding — a Danish royal castle perched on a hill above the town, originally built in 1268 and repeatedly expanded over the following centuries. The castle that stands today is two things at once: a medieval ruin and a modernist interior. After the 1808 fire reduced it to an empty shell, the 20th-century restoration wrapped entirely new spaces inside the old walls, with concrete floors and steel staircases that deliberately contrast with the 700-year-old brick.

The museum inside covers Danish royal history, applied arts, and the history of the castle itself. But the building is the real exhibit — the dialogue between the ruined stone exterior and the functional modern interior is one of the more interesting pieces of architectural thinking in Denmark. The tower offers views over Kolding Fjord on clear days. Entry is paid; combined tickets with Trapholt are available.
