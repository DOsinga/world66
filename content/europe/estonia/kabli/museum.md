  In the second half of the nineteenth century, Kabli was an unlikely shipbuilding centre. Local farmers turned to shipwright work and built large oceangoing schooners on the beach itself, some of which sailed to ports as far as Brazil and the West Indies. The trade collapsed when steam replaced sail, and the village returned to fishing and farming within a generation.

The small Kabli Museum occupies a nineteenth-century wooden house that once belonged to one of the village's many sea captains. The exhibits tell the surprising story of Kabli's mid-1800s shipbuilding boom — when farmers from this tiny coastal hamlet built large oceangoing schooners on the beach and sailed them around the world. Original ship models, navigation instruments, photographs of the captains and their families, and old household items fill the rooms.

The museum is run by volunteers and has limited opening hours — usually summer afternoons, but check ahead in shoulder seasons. Admission is a few euros. The whole visit takes about 45 minutes and gives useful context to the otherwise quiet village.
