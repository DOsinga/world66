The Finnish Lakeland — Järvi-Suomi in Finnish — is the largest lake district in Europe: an enormous patchwork of water and forest covering the southern interior of the country from Tampere in the west to the Russian border in the east. Finland has around 188,000 lakes, and most of them are here. The region is defined less by any single city than by the relationship between land and water, which in places becomes so intertwined that it is hard to say whether you are on an island or a peninsula.

The centre of the Lakeland is Lake Saimaa, the fourth-largest lake in Europe, which sprawls across four provinces and empties west through a canal to the Gulf of Finland. [Savonlinna](/europe/finland/savonlinna), on three islands in the Saimaa system, is the region's flagship city and home to Olavinlinna castle, a medieval fortress rising from a rock in the lake. [Kuopio](/europe/finland/kuopio) in the north is the region's largest city, a working place with a strong food culture. [Lappeenranta](/europe/finland/lappeenranta) at the south end of Saimaa commands the canal connection to Russia and has a well-preserved fortress area on the lakeside.

The summer landscape is extraordinary — glittering water in every direction, forested ridges and eskers breaking the surface as peninsulas and islands, the afternoon light staying long into the evening. The most dramatic of these formations is the [Punkaharju Ridge](/europe/finland/punkaharju), a seven-kilometre esker so narrow in places that you can see both water surfaces from the path running its spine. The rare Saimaa ringed seal, one of the world's most endangered seals, survives only in this lake system — spotting one from a boat or kayak is among the region's defining experiences.

Winter transforms the Lakeland completely: ice roads form across the frozen lakes, cross-country ski tracks run through silent forests, and the saunas — a genuine cultural institution here, not a tourist amenity — acquire particular meaning when the temperature outside is minus twenty.
