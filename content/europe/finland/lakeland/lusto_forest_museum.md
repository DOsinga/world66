  Finland is one of the most forested countries in the world — about 73% of its land area is covered by trees — and the relationship between Finns and their forests is genuinely deep: a combination of economic dependence (timber exports drove Finnish industrialisation), spiritual connection (the forest as refuge, as quiet, as nature), and practical culture (mushroom picking, berry gathering, hunting). Lusto, the Finnish Forest Museum, stands at the southern end of the Punkaharju Ridge near Savonlinna. The building itself is an achievement — designed to complement the forest landscape, with log and timber construction and views into the surrounding trees. The permanent exhibitions cover Finnish forestry from traditional slash-and-burn agriculture through industrial logging, paper production, and the contemporary debates about how much Finland should harvest from its forests versus protect them.

The museum is aimed as much at Finnish visitors as international ones, and several of the exhibitions work as genuine cultural history rather than tourism content. The section on forest workers' culture — the log drives, the remote winter camps, the physical work that was the basis of the Finnish economy for generations — is particularly strong. Combined with a walk on the Punkaharju Ridge immediately outside, it makes a full afternoon.
