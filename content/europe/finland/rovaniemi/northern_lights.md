  The Aurora Borealis occurs when charged particles from the sun collide with gases in the
  upper atmosphere. At Rovaniemi's latitude, the geomagnetic conditions are optimal — the
  city lies within the "auroral oval," the zone of maximum activity. Finnish Lapland claims
  over 200 nights per year with auroral activity, though cloud cover obscures them frequently.
  The Sami people have many names for the aurora; in Finnish folklore the lights were called
  "fox fires," said to be a great fox running across the sky and brushing the hills with its
  tail, sending sparks flying upward.

Rovaniemi lies within the auroral oval, the zone around the magnetic pole where the northern lights appear most frequently and most intensely. From late August through April, on clear nights with sufficient solar activity, the lights can appear overhead in waves of green, occasionally violet or red. The show ranges from a faint greenish smear to curtains of colour that fill the entire sky and move fast enough to track in real time.

The practical approach is simple: stay at least three nights (to improve the odds against cloud cover), get away from the city lights, and watch the space weather forecast at space.fmi.fi or through the Aurora Service app. The forests north of the city and the lakeshores around Rovaniemi offer dark sky viewing within easy reach. Most tour operators in Rovaniemi run northern lights snowmobile or snowshoe trips that take you into dark areas at the most likely hours — typically 9pm to 2am. The experience is deeply dependent on luck and weather, which is part of what makes it memorable.
