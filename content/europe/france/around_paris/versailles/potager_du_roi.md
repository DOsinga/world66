The Potager du Roi lies about 10 minutes' walk south of the palace, behind the Orangerie, and is easily missed by visitors who stick to the main circuit. Created between 1678 and 1683 by Jean-Baptiste de La Quintinie at Louis XIV's direct request, it covers about 9 hectares and was designed to supply the royal kitchens with vegetables, fruit, and herbs year-round.

La Quintinie engineered the entire site from scratch. He drained and excavated a stinking pond, replaced the soil, built warm walls for espalier fruit trees, and created a system of hotbeds and glass frames that allowed out-of-season production. Louis XIV visited regularly and apparently liked to pick peas himself. The garden became one of the most famous in Europe, visited by the English diarist John Evelyn among others.

Today the Potager is still a working kitchen garden, managed by the École nationale supérieure du paysage, the national landscape school. Fruit and vegetables are still grown here and sold on site. Entry requires a separate ticket from the palace. It is a peaceful and slightly hidden corner — the crowds of the main gardens don't reach here.
