The Royal Abbey of Fontevraud is one of the largest surviving monastic complexes in Europe, and one of the most historically charged sites in France. Founded in 1101 by the wandering preacher Robert of Arbrissel, it became the mausoleum of the Plantagenet dynasty — the royal house that ruled England from 1154 and controlled much of France. Henry II of England, his queen Eleanor of Aquitaine, their son Richard the Lionheart, and his wife Isabelle of Angoulême all lie in effigy in the abbey church, their painted stone figures among the finest Plantagenet funerary sculpture to survive anywhere.

The abbey church is Romanesque, cool and austere, with a long nave lit by high windows. The cloisters beyond are elegant 16th-century Gothic, built during the abbey's prosperous late medieval period. The chapter house has notable Renaissance murals. Behind the main buildings, the **Romanesque kitchen** — Tour Évraud — is a remarkable structure: an octagonal tower dating from around 1160, its original purpose forgotten for so long that 19th-century scholars argued it had been a baptistery or chapel. Archaeological research only confirmed in 2020 that it was indeed the abbey kitchen.

After the Revolution the abbey was seized and converted to a prison by Napoleonic decree in 1804. It held prisoners — men, women, and children — from nine surrounding departments for over 150 years, until 1963. The restoration that followed has been exceptional; the buildings now house a cultural centre, an art-house cinema, a well-regarded restaurant, and a hotel within the medieval walls. Visiting the former cells alongside the Plantagenet tombs is a quietly vertiginous experience.
