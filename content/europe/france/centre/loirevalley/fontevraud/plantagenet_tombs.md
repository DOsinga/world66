  Eleanor of Aquitaine's effigy is the only Plantagenet tomb figure that shows the deceased reading a book. She holds it open, as if interrupted mid-chapter. Eleanor died at Fontevraud in 1204, aged about 82, having outlived her husband Henry II, her son Richard the Lionheart, and her favourite son Geoffrey. She had spent 15 years imprisoned by Henry after backing a rebellion by their sons, and spent her last years as a politician and peacemaker. The book she reads has never been satisfactorily identified.

Inside the abbey church of Fontevraud, four painted stone effigies lie in the nave: Henry II of England, Eleanor of Aquitaine, Richard the Lionheart, and his wife Isabelle of Angoulême. They are among the finest Plantagenet royal tombs to survive anywhere, and they have an unusual quality for medieval funerary monuments — they feel individual. Eleanor reads; Richard wears his crown at a slight angle; Henry II's face carries the traces of a man who has seen a great deal.

The tombs are not burial sites in any practical sense. The actual remains of the Plantagenets were scattered or destroyed during the French Revolution, when the abbey was ransacked and the crypt opened. What survive are the effigies themselves, which were removed to safety and eventually restored to the church. They are the most tangible relic of the Angevin empire — the cross-channel realm that stretched from Scotland to the Pyrenees in the late 12th century and produced some of the most dramatic conflicts of medieval history.
