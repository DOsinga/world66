  The function of Tour Évraud was debated for centuries. After the abbey became a prison, nobody could quite remember what the octagonal tower with its cluster of smaller apses had been for. Scholars proposed it was a baptistery, a round chapel, or a funerary monument. One theory held it was a lantern tower used for signalling. Tour Évraud is the most extraordinary building at Fontevraud — a Romanesque kitchen tower dating from around 1160, shaped like nothing else in France. An octagonal central structure is surrounded by five radiating apsidioles, each topped with a conical stone roof, giving the whole thing the appearance of a cluster of towers or a small Gothic cathedral seen from above. Chimneys and smoke outlets pierce the roof at multiple points.

It survived the Revolution and the prison era largely intact because nobody was quite sure what to do with it. The building had become so detached from its original purpose that it was used variously as a prison chapel and a storage facility. Since the restoration began in the 1970s it has been one of the abbey's most-visited structures — both for its architectural strangeness and for the puzzle it posed for so long. It stands in the abbey grounds near the kitchen courtyard, and entrance is included with the main abbey ticket.
