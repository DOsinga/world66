The Château de Langeais is one of the best-preserved medieval castles in France, and one of the most honest: it makes no claims to grandeur it cannot support. Louis XI ordered it built between 1465 and 1469 as part of his defensive line against the Duchy of Brittany, and the speed of construction — barely four years — gives it a striking architectural unity. The exterior looks exactly like what it was: a fortress, with machiolations, round towers, and a drawbridge that still works. The interior tells a different story.

Step inside and the 15th century is intact in a way almost nowhere else in the Loire achieves. Original tapestries hang on the walls. Carved fireplaces stand in their original rooms. Beds, chairs, chests, and candleholders are all period furniture, not reproductions, largely because the castle passed through private hands for centuries without being gutted or "restored." The great hall, where the marriage of Anne of Brittany and Charles VIII took place in 1491, contains a wax recreation of the ceremony that is less kitsch than it sounds — the figures are good, and the event was genuinely consequential.

Behind the castle, in the courtyard, stands the ruins of a 10th-century keep — one of the oldest in France, raised around 994 AD by Foulques Nerra, Count of Anjou, whose fortress-building campaigns reshaped this entire stretch of the Loire. The older ruin and the newer castle side by side tell 500 years of medieval history in a single glance.
