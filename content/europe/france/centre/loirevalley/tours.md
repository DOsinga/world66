Tours sits at the confluence of the Loire and the Cher, the largest city in the valley and a natural base for exploring the chateaux country. The old centre on the south bank of the Loire is compact and largely pedestrian — a tight grid of half-timbered houses, Romanesque and Gothic churches, and lively café terraces that fills with students from the university during term time.

The medieval quarter around [Place Plumereau](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/place_plumereau) is the hub of street life, with bars and restaurants spilling out under the carved timber facades. From there, [Rue Colbert](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/rue_colbert) leads east through a corridor of restaurants and antique shops to the [cathedral](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/cathedral). West of Rue Nationale — the main shopping axis — the streets are quieter but no less handsome, with several good museums tucked among the townhouses, including the [Musée des Beaux-Arts](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/musee_des_beaux_arts) in the former Archbishop's Palace and the [Musée du Compagnonnage](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/musee_du_compagnonnage) in the cellars of [Saint-Julien Abbey](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/saint_julien_abbey).

The [train station](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/tours_station) is a monument in itself, a Belle Époque palace by Victor Laloux — who also designed the [Hôtel de Ville](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/hotel_de_ville) across the square — that signals Tours's confidence as a regional capital. The [Basilica of Saint Martin](/europe/france/centre/loirevalley/tours/basilica_saint_martin) and the two surviving medieval towers nearby give a sense of the great pilgrimage church that once stood here. Villandry, Chenonceau, and Amboise are all within half an hour, making it easy to see two chateaux in a day and still be back in town for dinner on rue Colbert.
