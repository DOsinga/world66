The formula "mort pour la France" — died for France — is not merely a turn of phrase. In French law, it is an official designation granted by the state to soldiers and resistance fighters killed in combat, confirming their sacrifice and entitling their families to certain honours and pensions. To read these words on a plaque is to know that someone filed the papers, pursued the recognition, and fixed a legal category of heroism to a particular name on a particular wall.

Léon Bruny's plaque is in the eastern reaches of Marseille, in a neighbourhood that retains its working-class character. The name Bruny suggests a long-established Provençal family; the plaque records a death but not a date or a unit, which makes placing his sacrifice in a specific war difficult without further archival research. He may have died in the trenches of the Western Front, or in North Africa, or in the maquis of the Resistance — the formula covers all of these and more.

What is certain is that someone in this neighbourhood cared enough to mark the loss. Street plaques like this one were rarely placed by the state; they were paid for by families, by neighbours, by veterans' associations — by people who knew the man behind the name. Léon Bruny, whoever he was, mattered enough that his memory was cast in stone and mounted where those who passed would see it. That is the whole argument of a plaque like this: that he should not be walked past without a moment's recognition.
