The Carasso name carries a long history in the Mediterranean world. Sephardic in origin, the family name is found across the communities that descended from the Jews expelled from Spain in 1492 — communities that settled in North Africa, the Ottoman Empire, and the port cities of southern France. That three brothers from one family died for France speaks to the profound integration of this community into French civic life, and to the particular tragedy that fell on Jewish families during the wars of the twentieth century.

The plaque does not specify which war claimed the Carasso brothers, but Marseille's Jewish community bore a double weight during the Second World War: sons who died fighting the Germans in uniform, and then the horror of the Vichy years, when the city's old Jewish quarter in the Panier was razed by the Nazis in January 1943 and thousands of the city's Jewish residents were deported to the death camps. A memorial to brothers who died for France in that context is also a memorial to a community that gave everything to a republic that did not always protect it in return.

The plaque sits in a residential neighbourhood north of the city centre. It draws almost no visitors, which makes finding it all the more affecting. Three names, one family, one city — the arithmetic of war rendered in stone.
