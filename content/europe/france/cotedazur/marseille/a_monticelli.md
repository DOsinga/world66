Adolphe Monticelli was born in Marseille in 1824 and spent most of his working life there, avoiding Paris whenever he could. He painted with dense impasto, layering pigment until his canvases looked encrusted with jewels, and developed a colour sense that was unlike anything being done in academic French painting of his era. He sold little, drank heavily, and died in poverty in Marseille in 1886 — the same year Van Gogh arrived in Paris and encountered his work for the first time.

Van Gogh owned twelve of Monticelli's paintings and wrote about him with explicit admiration, citing him as a direct predecessor whose technique and palette had shown what was possible. When Van Gogh and Gauguin discussed Marseille, they were partly talking about Monticelli — a painter who had lived and worked in the city, explored its light and colour on his own terms, and whose example pointed toward the Post-Impressionism they were working toward. The line from Monticelli to Van Gogh to twentieth-century painting is real, if not always taught.

The memorial acknowledges what Marseille's own critical tradition was slow to see: that a painter who was considered eccentric and marginal during his lifetime turned out to be at the origin of one of the most consequential stylistic shifts in modern art. He painted the city; the city eventually put up a monument to him.
