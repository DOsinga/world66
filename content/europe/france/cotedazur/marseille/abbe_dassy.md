Here's the puzzle: Abbé Dassy served one of Marseille's roughest, densest quarters near Belsunce, and almost nothing about his life survives in wide circulation. What remains is his name, carved curbside — the quiet commemoration this city saves for people who mattered locally, not nationally.

Parish priests here weren't just clergy. They ran soup kitchens, argued with city hall on behalf of residents, and sometimes organised resistance under occupation. Naming a lane after one was Marseille's way of nodding to that work — even after the 1905 law split church from state and made such gestures politically delicate.

Stand here and you're in a part of town that's been rebuilt, torn down, and repopulated more times than anyone counts. This little inscription is a fossil from an older social landscape — proof that before the bulldozers, Dassy was simply someone the street remembered.
