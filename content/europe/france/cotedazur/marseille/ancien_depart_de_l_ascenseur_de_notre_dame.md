Notre-Dame de la Garde has crowned the highest natural point in Marseille since 1853, when the neo-Byzantine basilica replaced an earlier chapel on the summit at 162 metres above sea level. The climb on foot — roughly 25 minutes from the city centre via the winding streets of Le Panier or the Rue du Fort — is the classic approach, but for much of the late nineteenth and early twentieth century Marseillais and visitors had a mechanical alternative: a funicular railway that connected the lower city to the basilica terrace. The departure point marked here is the lower station of that lost ascent.

The Marseille funicular to Notre-Dame de la Garde operated from 1892, running up the rock face on a track that is still partially visible in the cliff. It was a popular convenience for the devout, the curious, and the many sailors who visited the basilica to give thanks — or to leave votive offerings — before and after voyages. The funicular closed in 1967 as car ownership grew and the road to the summit was improved; the infrastructure was dismantled, leaving only this lower station as a reminder of the earlier arrangement.

Today the options for reaching the top are on foot, by road with private transport, or by the tourist petit train that departs from the Vieux-Port. The walk remains the most rewarding: the ascent through the Belle de Mai and Belsunce neighbourhoods offers changing views over the rooftops and the harbour, and the panorama from the terrace — Frioul islands, Cap Croisette, the Étoile massif to the north — makes the effort worthwhile whatever the season.
