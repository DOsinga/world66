The Roux de Corse were a Corsican trading family who established themselves in Marseille in the 17th century, building wealth in the Mediterranean commerce that flowed through the port. The mansion they commissioned in the Rue Grignan follows the *hôtel particulier* model — a private urban palace for a merchant family positioning itself as close to the aristocracy as its commercial origins permitted. The Rue Grignan was then, as now, one of the more prestigious streets in central Marseille.

The facade is a deliberate statement: rusticated stonework on the ground floor, carved stone balconies on the piano nobile, a composition that mirrors the architectural language of Parisian hôtels particuliers of the same period. In Marseille this language was adapted for the Mediterranean climate — higher ceilings, larger windows, a courtyard arrangement promoting air circulation. The building has been classified as a Monument Historique since the 1930s, preserving it through successive changes of use: merchant residence, hotel, government office, arts venue.

Marseille produced fewer surviving examples of this genre than comparable French commercial cities because the city's 19th-century expansion — more aggressive and more complete than in most of France — demolished large sections of the old commercial quarters. The Rue Grignan area retained more of its 18th-century fabric than most of the city centre, which is why the mansion survives. The facade rewards careful attention for what it communicates about the social ambitions of Marseille's pre-Revolutionary trading elite.
