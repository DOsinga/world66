The Roux de Corse were Corsican traders who got rich off Mediterranean commerce, then built themselves a palace to prove it. Look at the facade: rusticated stone on the ground floor, carved balconies on the piano nobile — the exact architectural language of Parisian hôtels particuliers, transplanted to a merchant's street. It's the finest surviving ancien-régime townhouse in the city.

But this is Marseille, not Paris. The style got adapted for the heat — higher ceilings, bigger windows, a courtyard built to move air. Classified a Monument Historique since the 1930s, it's survived as residence, hotel, government office, and arts venue.

Most of Marseille's 18th-century commercial quarters got demolished in the city's aggressive 19th-century expansion — far more brutal than elsewhere in France. Rue Grignan kept more of its old fabric, which is the only reason this place is still standing. Read the facade closely and you're reading the social ambitions of the city's pre-Revolutionary trading elite.
