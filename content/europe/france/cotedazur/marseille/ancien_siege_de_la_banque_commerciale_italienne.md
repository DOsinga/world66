title: Ancien siège de la Banque commerciale italienne

By the late 1800s, Italians were Marseille's biggest foreign group — sometimes a fifth of the whole city. They poured in from Genoa, Piedmont, Naples, chasing work on the docks, in construction, in the soap factories. Italian dialects fought Provençal and Occitan in the markets. This was, quietly, an Italian city inside France.

The Banque Commerciale Italienne was their money. It wired remittances back to families in Calabria, bankrolled the shops going up along the Rue de Rome, and greased the trade in olive oil, wine, and textiles across the water. Italian banks trailed Italian migrants everywhere — Marseille, Buenos Aires, New York — serving people that French and American banks ignored.

Within two or three generations, that clientele melted into French life. Intermarriage, schools, two world wars — the old Italian identity dissolved into something purely Marseillais. The building survived it all, wearing a new name now, its banking-era past surfacing only in the city's archive photos.
