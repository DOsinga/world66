By the late nineteenth century Italians were the largest foreign-born group in Marseille, at times comprising a fifth or more of the city's population. They came from Genoa and Liguria, from Piedmont, from Naples and the Mezzogiorno, pushed out by rural poverty and pulled by Marseille's insatiable demand for labour on the docks, in construction, in soap factories, and in small trade. The Italian quarter clustered in the streets around the Vieux-Port and spread north toward Belsunce; Italian dialects competed with Provençal and Occitan in the markets. By the early twentieth century Marseille was, in a meaningful sense, an Italian city within France — politically French, culturally creolised, commercially entangled with the peninsula across the water.

The Banque Commerciale Italienne was the financial institution that served this community. It handled remittances sent home to families in Calabria or Liguria, financed the small businesses that Italian entrepreneurs were establishing along the Rue de Rome and in the wholesale quarter, and provided correspondent banking for the Italy-France trade in textiles, olive oil, wine, and manufactured goods. Its presence in Marseille reflected the broader reality that Italian banks followed Italian migration wherever it went — to Marseille, to Buenos Aires, to New York — building financial infrastructure for communities that French or American banks largely ignored. The building itself would have been designed in the classical or Italianate manner favoured for banking houses of the period, projecting solidity and trustworthiness to depositors who had often crossed the Mediterranean with very little.

The bank's particular clientele — working-class and petit-bourgeois Italian immigrants — gradually assimilated into French life across two or three generations. By the mid-twentieth century the distinctly Italian character of Marseille's community was fading, as intermarriage, schooling, and the trauma of two world wars dissolved the old national identities into a Marseillais identity of its own. The building on the Canebière district survived this transformation, its façade now bearing a different name and a different purpose, though the tight script of the banking era still occasionally surfaces in the archival photograph collections of the city.
