The SNCM — Société Nationale Maritime Corse-Méditerranée — operated the lifeline ferry routes that France considered a public service obligation: Marseille to Bastia, Ajaccio, and other Corsican ports, as well as sailings to Algiers, Tunis, and Sardinia. For generations of Corsicans, the overnight crossing from Marseille was the fundamental link to the mainland, as ingrained in the island's identity as the train de nuit had once been for passengers from the provinces. Algerians crossing during the post-independence era, Tunisians visiting family, summer tourists heading south with their cars loaded for the ferry deck — the SNCM carried them all from the Joliette docks just north of the Vieux-Port.

The company was the product of several earlier shipping lines nationalised or merged in the postwar decades, and it carried with it the labour culture of the French state enterprise: powerful unions, rigid work rules, generous conditions, and a workforce that knew how to stop the boats when negotiations broke down. Strikes were frequent and sometimes spectacular; passengers stranded on the quay or on board became a fixture of French summer news cycles. The state poured subsidies into the company to maintain routes deemed essential to territorial continuity — particularly the Corsican connections, which France is legally obligated to service — but the financial model never reconciled the subsidies with the actual cost structure. Low-cost competitors, particularly Corsica Ferries, took market share on the tourist routes that had once covered some of the losses.

The bankruptcy came in 2014, prolonged by legal disputes, union actions, and European Commission rulings about the legitimacy of the state aid the company had received. After the collapse, La Méridionale and Corsica Ferries absorbed most of the routes. The grand headquarters building near the port, where ferry schedules once determined the rhythm of a Mediterranean city, was left without its former occupant. The building stands as a monument to the era when France believed that running ferry lines across the sea was as natural a public duty as running the railways across the land.
