The SNCM ran the boats France called a public duty: Marseille to Bastia, Ajaccio, Algiers, Tunis, Sardinia. Corsicans crossing overnight, Algerians visiting family, tourists with cars stacked on the deck — they all shipped out from the Joliette docks just north of the Vieux-Port. This building set the schedules that set the rhythm of the whole city.

The company inherited the full French state-enterprise culture: powerful unions, generous conditions, and a workforce that knew exactly how to stop the boats. Strikes were a fixture of the summer news, passengers stranded on the quay or stuck on board. The state poured in subsidies to keep the Corsican routes running — legally obligated to — but the math never added up, and low-cost Corsica Ferries kept eating the profitable tourist runs.

Bankruptcy hit in 2014, dragged out by lawsuits, strikes, and Brussels rulings on whether all that state aid was even legal. La Méridionale and Corsica Ferries carved up the routes. The grand headquarters near the port now stands empty — a monument to the day France stopped believing that running ferries was as natural a duty as running trains.
