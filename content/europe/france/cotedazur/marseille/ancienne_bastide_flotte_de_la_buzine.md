The 18th-century Provençal bastide at La Buzine appears in Pagnol's autobiographical tetralogy — La Gloire de mon père, Le Château de ma mère, Le Temps des secrets, Le Temps des amours — as the destination of summer walks that form the emotional centre of his childhood. The "castle of my mother" was not a castle but a large country house visible across a property the Pagnol family had to cross, partly illegally, to reach their own rented bastide nearby. His mother's distress at being caught trespassing became one of the defining episodes of his childhood narrative and the scene from which the second book takes its title.

Pagnol bought the Buzine property in 1941, during the German occupation, intending to establish a film studio on the grounds — his own Provençal equivalent of the Hollywood studios he had visited and admired. The plan was serious: he had already produced and directed films independently and had the resources to attempt it. The project collapsed when the liberation changed the economic conditions and the postwar French film industry reconcentrated in Paris. The bastide was left unused. Pagnol's widow sold it in 1973 and it passed through private hands, falling into dereliction.

The city of Marseille acquired the property in the 1990s and restored it as a cinema and cultural centre dedicated to Pagnol's work. It now functions as the principal institutional memorial to his legacy in the city — screening his films, hosting exhibitions, and maintaining the grounds. The irony is complete: the house Pagnol bought and could not use became, after his death, the most substantial monument to his life in Marseille.
