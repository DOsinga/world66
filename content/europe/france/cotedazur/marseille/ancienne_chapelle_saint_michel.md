Saint Michael — warrior archangel, psychopomp and weigher of souls — was among the most widely invoked saints in medieval Christendom, and Marseille's old parishes planted chapels in his name at places that felt exposed or liminal: hilltop spurs, road junctions, the margins between the settled town and the wild garrigue. The Ancienne Chapelle Saint-Michel stands in the district south of the Vieux-Port, at coordinates that place it in the rolling neighbourhoods of the 10th arrondissement, well clear of the monumental city centre.

The chapel's history follows the arc typical of Marseille's smaller religious buildings. Founded in the medieval or early modern period to serve a local parish or confraternity, it survived the ancien régime intact, then ran into trouble during the Revolution of 1789, when religious properties were nationalised and many chapels were secularised, sold off, or converted to secular uses — stables, warehouses, community halls. Some were demolished; others slipped quietly into private hands and kept their vaulted ceilings as a discreet reminder of their former function.

What survives today as the Ancienne Chapelle Saint-Michel is best read as a piece of Marseille's dense archaeological palimpsest. The façade, if it remains, is likely modest: a plain stone front, perhaps a small oculus or arched doorway in the Provençal Romanesque manner, with no grand bell-tower to announce itself to the street. This is a chapel that repays slow attention — one to notice while walking rather than to visit as a destination.
