The building takes its name from the Présentation nuns who occupied the site before it was converted to penal use. As a prison it operated from the 17th century through the Second World War, its long institutional life punctuated by the fact that it came under the control of the Vichy government and German occupying forces after 1942. Marseille was the main staging point for deportations from France's Mediterranean zone: the city's port and railway connections made it a natural transit hub, and the German and Vichy authorities used several existing detention facilities — including the Présentines and the Baumettes prison — to hold Jews and political prisoners before transferring them to the camps.

The numbers are not small. Between 1942 and 1944, tens of thousands of people passed through the various holding facilities in and around Marseille. The January 1943 German roundup — which destroyed the old Vieux-Port neighbourhood — was accompanied by mass arrests and deportations organised through the city's detention network. The Présentines was part of that machinery. Most of those who entered it under German occupation did not return.

The site stands now as a memorial by default — a structure whose walls contained events that the city has not forgotten and cannot wish away. Marseille has a complicated relationship with its Vichy-era history: the city also sheltered and helped thousands of refugees, and the resistance was active here. The Présentines is a reminder that both things were true simultaneously, in the same city, sometimes in adjacent buildings.
