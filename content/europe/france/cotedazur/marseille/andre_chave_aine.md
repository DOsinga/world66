The suffix "Aîné" — the Elder — signals that there was also a younger André Chave, probably a son, active in Marseille at the same time. The practice of appending Aîné or Jeune to distinguish father and son was common in nineteenth-century French commercial directories and civic records, particularly among merchant dynasties. The coordinates place this commemoration in the Noailles or Cours Julien vicinity, a neighbourhood that in the nineteenth century was home to tradespeople, small manufacturers, and the commercial middle class that drove much of Marseille's port-linked prosperity.

André Chave Aîné was most likely a négociant — a merchant trader — or a manufacturer connected to the industries that funnelled goods between the Mediterranean and the French interior. Marseille's economic boom in the second half of the nineteenth century, fuelled by the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 and the expansion of colonial trade, created fortunes for families who worked the shipping, soap, and textile trades. Men who contributed philanthropically to the city — funding schools, hospitals, or public gardens — were rewarded with a street name or a plaque as a lasting token of municipal gratitude.

The "Aîné" form of commemoration is historically revealing: it means the city, or the neighbourhood, consciously chose to honour the father rather than the son, suggesting the elder's contribution was the more substantial. Today the name survives mainly as a cartographic curiosity, legible to anyone who pauses to read the blue enamel plate that Marseille still affixes to its older streets in the traditional style.
