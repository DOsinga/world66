"Aîné" means "the Elder" — so somewhere in Marseille there was a younger André Chave too, probably his son. Nineteenth-century merchant dynasties tacked Aîné or Jeune onto their names to keep father and son straight in the commercial directories. The blue enamel plate sits in the Noailles–Cours Julien quarter, old turf of tradespeople and small manufacturers.

Chave was almost certainly a négociant, working the shipping, soap, or textile trades that made this port rich. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and money poured in. Merchants who gave back — schools, hospitals, gardens — got their names on the map.

And here's the tell: the city honored the elder, not the son. That usually meant the father did the heavier lifting. Read the plate as you pass — it's the only place the name still lives.
