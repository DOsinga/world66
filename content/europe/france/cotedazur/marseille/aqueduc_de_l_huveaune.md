Before 1849, Marseille was chronically thirsty. Summer droughts dried the street fountains to a trickle, and the city grew faster than its wells and cisterns could handle. This aqueduct, built in the 1600s or 1700s, pulled water from the Huveaune river valley to keep the taps running.

Then came the Canal de Marseille — eighty kilometres of tunnels and siphons hauling alpine water down from the Durance, engineered by Franz Mayor de Montricher over two decades. Overnight, the old aqueducts became useless. Most crumbled and got swallowed into walls and gardens.

What survives here is a fragment worth hunting for: rough masonry, a surviving arch, hydraulic geometry cut without a single power tool. There's no signpost. Find it in the city's northern folds and you've recovered a piece of Marseille's pre-modern thirst.
