Before the Canal de Marseille brought reliable water from the distant Durance river in 1849, the city depended on a patchwork of local sources: wells, cisterns, and short aqueducts that tapped springs and rivers in the surrounding hills. The Huveaune is a small river that rises in the Sainte-Baume massif east of Marseille and flows westward to the sea; an aqueduct drawing from it — or from springs along its valley — was one of the more ambitious elements of this pre-industrial water infrastructure. The structure visible at these coordinates is a remnant of that earlier system, built in the seventeenth or eighteenth century to supplement the city's chronically insufficient supply.

Water scarcity was a persistent grievance in Marseille for much of the early modern period. The city grew faster than its infrastructure could support, and summer droughts regularly reduced street fountains to a trickle. The construction of the Canal de Marseille, engineered by Franz Mayor de Montricher and completed over two decades of work, was a transformative public-works project: eighty kilometres of canal, tunnels, and siphons bringing alpine water to a Mediterranean city. Once the canal opened, the older aqueducts became redundant almost overnight, and most fell into disrepair. The Huveaune aqueduct survived in fragmentary form, absorbed into walls, gardens, and forgotten corners of the urban fabric.

What remains today is the kind of ruin that rewards close attention: rough masonry, a surviving arch or channel section, the geometry of hydraulic engineering carried out without power tools. The coordinates place it in the northern part of the city, where the terrain still shows the undulations that made water distribution difficult and aqueduct construction necessary. It is not a signposted monument; finding it feels like recovering a piece of the city's pre-modern thirst.
