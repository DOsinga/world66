On 5 November 2018, at 9 in the morning, two 19th-century apartment buildings at numbers 63 and 65 Rue d'Aubagne collapsed without warning, killing eight people. The buildings had been flagged as structurally unsafe. Safety inspections had identified the risks. Repairs had not been made and tenants had not been evacuated. The disaster was not a natural event; it was the outcome of years of institutional failure — a city that knew its housing stock was dangerous and did not act.

The political fallout was immediate and severe. Within weeks, the city had been forced to evacuate more than 2,000 people from buildings deemed similarly unsafe, leaving many of them temporarily homeless and in temporary accommodation. The then-mayor Jean-Claude Gaudin was held politically responsible; the disaster contributed to the end of his long tenure. The subsequent years brought legal proceedings, a special plan for deteriorated housing, and renewed attention to the conditions in Noailles and other poor central-city neighbourhoods where neglected tenements disproportionately housed immigrant and low-income families.

The Arbre de la Dignité — the Tree of Dignity — was placed by local residents and neighbourhood associations as a spontaneous memorial in the weeks after the collapse. It has since become a focal point for annual commemorations on 5 November. The name carries its own argument: that the people who died deserved decent housing as a matter of dignity, not charity, and that the city's failure to provide it was a moral and political failure, not just an administrative one.
