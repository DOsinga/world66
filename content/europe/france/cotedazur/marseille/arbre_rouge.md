Arbre Rouge — the Red Tree — stands on Avenue de la Capelette in Marseille's 10th arrondissement, a neighbourhood of quiet residential streets and light industry tucked east of the city centre. The sculpture takes the form of a stylised tree stripped of foliage, its bare branches coated in a flat, arterial red that makes it unmissable against the pale render of the surrounding buildings. It is an example of the chromatic public sculpture tradition in which a single bold colour transforms an everyday silhouette into something charged with meaning: red for vitality, for danger, for the life running beneath bark and beneath skin alike.

Red has long been favoured by artists placing work in urban settings because it refuses to disappear into the background. Where a bronze or grey-steel tree might read as mere ornament, the painted red surface of this piece insists on being read as a statement. The work invites the passer-by to consider what a tree is doing here at all — this is not parkland but a working-class city quarter — and why it has been drained of its natural greens and browns in favour of a colour borrowed from warning signs and beating hearts.

La Capelette is not a neighbourhood that usually draws visitors, which is part of what makes encountering Arbre Rouge satisfying. The sculpture belongs to the people who live here rather than to a tourist circuit, and its maintenance and presence reflect a city policy of distributing public art beyond the Vieux-Port and the grands projets of the waterfront. Walk the avenue on a bright morning and the tree casts a long red shadow across the pavement, a deliberately jarring note of chromatic intensity in an otherwise understated street.
