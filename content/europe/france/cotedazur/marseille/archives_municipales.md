The Archives Municipales de Marseille are among the richest urban archives in France, reflecting a city that maintained an unusual degree of administrative autonomy through much of its history. Marseille's medieval government, organised around a body of consuls from at least the twelfth century, kept careful records of its deliberations, statutes, and commercial dealings — documents that have survived, in part, to the present day and provide historians with a continuous thread of civic life stretching back eight centuries. The archive holds the registers of the consulate, notarial acts recording the commercial transactions of a city that was for centuries one of the Mediterranean's busiest trading ports, early modern accounts of plague epidemics (Marseille suffered catastrophically in 1720, and the documentation of that disaster is extensive), and the full administrative record of a major French city from the Revolution to the present.

Researchers working on Mediterranean history, French urban history, the history of trade, migration, and public health will find the Marseille archives unusually productive. The port registers in particular document the movement of goods and people through a city that served as France's principal gateway to the Levant, North Africa, and the Indian Ocean trade via the Suez Canal after 1869. The archives also hold the records of Marseille's complex relationship with its Algerian community — the city received hundreds of thousands of *pieds-noirs* and Algerian immigrants from the 1950s onward, and the social history of that transformation is documented here.

The reading room is open to the public on production of identity documents, and staff are experienced in assisting researchers who do not read nineteenth-century French administrative handwriting. An online catalogue allows preliminary searching before a visit. The building is located north of the city centre, away from the tourist districts, and is easily reached by metro or bus.
