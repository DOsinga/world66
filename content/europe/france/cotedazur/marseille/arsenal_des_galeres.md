Louis XIV built a Mediterranean navy from almost nothing, and this was its engine room. At full tilt, 15,000 people packed the complex: free workers, military craftsmen, and prisoners sentenced to row the galleys—a punishment France only abolished in 1748. The ships built here fought Ottoman and Barbary corsairs for half a century.

Here's the twist: the galley was already obsolete when production peaked. It only made sense in the enclosed, wind-starved Mediterranean, where human muscle beat unreliable sails. When the galley fleet disbanded in 1748, the arsenal became storage and faded from memory.

The main 17th-century building still stands on the Cours Honoré d'Estienne d'Orves, the wide pedestrian square just southwest of the Vieux-Port. It's a hotel now. That long, plain stone façade gives away nothing about the thousands who once labored behind it.
