The Arsenal des Galères was the industrial spine of French naval power in the Mediterranean during the 17th and early 18th centuries. Louis XIV ordered its expansion in the 1660s as part of Colbert's programme to build a professional French navy from almost nothing. At its peak the complex employed and housed roughly 15,000 people — free workers, military craftsmen, and the condemned men serving as galley slaves, a judicial sentence formally abolished only in 1748. The galleys built and repaired here rowed against Ottoman and Barbary corsair forces across a half-century of Mediterranean campaigns.

The galley was already an obsolescent vessel by the time the arsenal reached maximum production. Its dependence on massed human labour made it viable only where winds were unreliable and precise inshore manoeuvre mattered — the enclosed waters of the Mediterranean, not the Atlantic. When the navy formally disbanded the galley fleet in 1748, the arsenal converted to storage and other military uses, losing its original purpose without losing its main structure.

The principal 17th-century building survives intact on the Cours Honoré d'Estienne d'Orves, the broad pedestrian square southwest of the Vieux-Port — one of the few remaining examples of French royal industrial-military architecture of that period at this scale. It now operates as a hotel. The plain, long stone façade gives no indication of what the building once produced.
