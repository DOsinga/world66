The Basilique du Sacré-Cœur in Marseille is easily overshadowed by the city's far more famous Notre-Dame de la Garde, but it has its own particular history. It belongs to a wave of Sacré-Cœur churches built across France in the years after 1871, when Catholic leaders made a collective vow to construct basilicas dedicated to the Sacred Heart as acts of national expiation — a religious response to the trauma of the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune. The most famous of those churches is the Sacré-Cœur on Montmartre in Paris, but the impulse produced churches in dozens of French towns.

Marseille's version is quieter and more neighbourhood-scaled than the Parisian landmark. It serves the local parish community and is not, in itself, a major pilgrimage destination. That relative obscurity makes it worth a visit for anyone interested in how the same political and religious moment produced very different results depending on the ambition and resources available locally.

The building is generally open for worship at standard hours. The interior reflects the devotional aesthetic of late nineteenth-century French Catholicism — statuary, stained glass, and the imagery of the Sacred Heart that gives the basilica its name.
