Standing at the city's highest point, the Basilique Notre-Dame de la Garde is impossible to lose. The gilded bronze statue of the Virgin and Child crowning the bell tower rises another 11.2 metres above the Romanesque-Byzantine mass below, and on a clear day she can be seen from ships 50 kilometres out at sea. The Marseillais call her "la Bonne Mère" — the Good Mother — and the name is entirely earnest.

The basilica was built between 1853 and 1864 to designs by Henri-Jacques Espérandieu on the site of a 13th-century chapel that had stood here since at least 1214. Espérandieu layered alternating bands of white limestone and green-veined marble across the exterior in the Romano-Byzantine manner fashionable under Napoleon III, and fitted the interior with gilded mosaics whose gold leaf catches the Mediterranean light coming through the upper windows. But what distinguishes the interior from any standard French pilgrimage church is the ex-votos: hundreds of painted panels, model ships in full sail, ceramic plaques, and photographs left by sailors, fishermen, and their families in thanksgiving for surviving storms, shipwrecks, and near-misses at sea. They cover the walls from floor to vault and constitute an accidental archive of Marseillais maritime life over two centuries.

The terrace surrounding the church is itself a reason to climb the hill — by foot up the steep path from the Vieux-Port quarter, or by the tourist road. From here the entire city spreads out in a single view: the Vieux-Port below, the islands of the Frioul archipelago to the west, the calanques cliffs stretching southeast, and on exceptional days the Alps to the northeast. The hill was fortified by the military before Espérandieu built here, and the lower levels of the structure he raised are still visibly defensive in character — which only adds to the building's peculiar atmosphere of sanctuary and command.
