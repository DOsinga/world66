The Batterie de 100 mm TR — TR standing for *Tir Rapide* (rapid fire) — was a coastal artillery installation armed with 100mm guns designed to intercept enemy vessels approaching Marseille from the north and northwest. Positioned on the coast near L'Estaque, the battery commanded wide arcs of fire across the Golfe de Fos and the sea lanes that converge on the harbour entrance. The 100mm calibre was a French inter-war standard for medium coastal defence, capable of engaging fast torpedo boats and light cruisers at ranges up to twelve kilometres.

France constructed the battery as part of its pre-war coastal defence network, but the installation was absorbed into German control after November 1942, when the Wehrmacht occupied the previously unoccupied southern zone and immediately set about hardening Marseille's defences against an Allied seaborne assault. German engineers reinforced several existing French emplacements around the city, adding concrete casemates and integrating them into the broader Atlantic Wall system that stretched, patchily, along the Mediterranean coast. The battery's rapid-fire designation made it particularly useful to the Germans as a defence against landing craft, which were the weapon the Atlantic Wall was specifically designed to defeat.

The site today is largely overgrown, with remnants of concrete works visible among the scrub. It sits within an area that rewards exploration on foot, with views across the sea and back towards the Chaîne de l'Estaque. Access is informal — there is no official heritage site or visitor infrastructure — and the remains are best appreciated as quiet evidence of a coastline that spent two years as a contested military frontier.
