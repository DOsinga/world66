The guns up here pointed south, straight at the open sea — exactly the direction Allied forces came from when they landed in Provence in August 1944. Any fleet aiming for the Vieux-Port had to sail within range first. Strategic logic, mounted on bare limestone at the very southern tip of Marseille.

The French Navy built this into a ring of coastal defences in the early 1900s. German forces took over and expanded it after occupying the southern zone in November 1942, linking it with batteries on Frioul and Château d'If into overlapping fields of fire. By 1944 it barely mattered — Allied air and naval gunfire had knocked out most shore defences before a single soldier hit the beach.

To get here, walk the coastal path from Callelongue, the last road-accessible point on this stretch. You cross garrigue and pale rock, and the ruins blend so well into the limestone you'll almost miss them. The payoff is the view: open Mediterranean on one side, the jagged Calanques coastline on the other.
