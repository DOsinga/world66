The Batterie de Croisette occupies a headland at the southern extremity of Marseille's municipal territory, close to the small fishing hamlet of Callelongue and the beginning of the Calanques national park. The coordinates place it on a rocky promontory that looks out over the Mediterranean approaches from the south — the direction from which Allied forces would eventually land in Provence in August 1944. A coastal battery here had obvious strategic logic: any fleet intending to enter the Vieux-Port or the modern harbour facilities to the north had to pass within range of guns mounted on these southern heights.

The site was part of a ring of coastal defences that the French Navy maintained around Marseille from the early twentieth century, later taken over and expanded by German forces after the occupation of the southern zone in November 1942. The southern batteries complemented those on Cap Croisette and the islands of Frioul and Château d'If, creating overlapping fields of fire that in theory made the outer roadstead extremely dangerous for any hostile naval force. In practice, by 1944 air superiority and naval gunfire from Allied warships had neutralised most fixed shore defences before ground troops went ashore.

Reaching the site requires a walk along the coastal path from Callelongue, the last point accessible by road on the southern Marseille coast. The path passes through garrigue and bare limestone typical of the Calanques, and the battery ruins — where they survive — blend into the pale rock. The reward for the walk is a panorama over the open sea and back along the jagged coastline that gives the Calanques their character.
