The Batterie de l'Escalette sits on the limestone headland south of Marseille where the city's coastline begins to dissolve into the Calanques proper, the terrain too rugged for continuous urban development. The battery was built as part of the defensive cordon protecting the approaches to the Vieux-Port, positioned to engage enemy vessels that might attempt to force their way into the harbour from the south. Its guns commanded a wide arc of sea and would have made a direct naval assault on Marseille extremely costly.

The fortification was modernised and re-armed multiple times over its operational life, most significantly during the German occupation of 1942 to 1944, when Marseille was incorporated into the coastal defence system that the Wehrmacht extended around the entire Mediterranean littoral as an adjunct to the Atlantic Wall. German engineers added reinforced concrete casemates, improved the fire-control positions, and emplaced heavier calibre weapons. The irony was that when liberation came in August 1944, it came overland from the north and west — the coastal guns, never fired in anger at the sea, were simply abandoned.

The site today is accessible to hikers following the coastal paths through the Calanques National Park. The concrete emplacements survive in varying states of decay, some intact enough to enter, others collapsed into the scrub of holm oak and rosemary that has colonised the position. The views from the battery are exceptional — on a clear day the Frioul islands are sharp against the sky, and the shipping lane that the guns once surveilled still carries tankers and ferries on their way in and out of the port.
