The Batterie du Mont-Rose sits on elevated ground south of the city, where the limestone massif of the Calanques begins to break into the sea in a series of dramatic inlets and cliffs. Mont-Rose — the Pink Mountain, named for the warm colour the local stone takes in raking evening light — rises steeply enough above the surrounding coastal terrain to give any artillery battery positioned here a significant advantage in range and observation. From the summit, gunners could see far out to sea and track vessels approaching from both south and southeast, covering approaches that a single lower-lying battery could not.

Coastal batteries on high ground were preferred wherever terrain permitted, because elevation extends the range of a gun beyond its flat-trajectory maximum and allows fire to be directed at the relatively thin deck armour of warships rather than the heavily protected belt armour along the waterline. The French Navy exploited this principle throughout the Mediterranean, and Mont-Rose was a natural choice for a battery position. Under German occupation the site was incorporated into the southern arc of Marseille's coastal defences, alongside the batteries at Croisette and other headlands further east.

The hill today lies within the zone of the Calanques national park, and the scrubby garigue vegetation — kermes oak, rosemary, and Aleppo pine — has partially reclaimed the concrete works left by both French and German engineers. Walkers on the GR98 coastal path and on trails leading south from the Mazargues district pass near the site. The views from the heights are exceptional, taking in the limestone ridges of the Calanques to the east, the open Mediterranean to the south, and on clear days the silhouette of the islands of Riou and Planier offshore.
