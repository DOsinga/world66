In 1985, diving instructor Henri Cosquer entered a submerged tunnel in the cliffs near Cap Morgiou and surfaced inside a dry cave chamber. He kept the discovery quiet for years; it was not until 1991, after three divers drowned attempting to follow him in, that he announced to the world what lay at the end of the 175-metre submerged passage: a cave decorated with some of the oldest known cave paintings in France. The images — hands in negative, horses, ibex, aurochs, seals, and what may be great auks — date to two main periods of activity, around 27,000 and 19,000 years ago. The cave takes its name from its finder and is now a protected historic monument.

The paradox of the Cosquer Cave is geographical. When the paintings were made, sea levels were 100 to 120 metres lower than today; the cave entrance would have been well above the shoreline, and the coast itself was many kilometres further south. As the Ice Age ended and the ice sheets melted, the sea rose and drowned the lower portion of the cave. Today the entrance lies 37 metres below the surface, accessible only to expert divers with the necessary permits — the paintings themselves, most of them higher in the cave above the original water line, survived. For the general public, the cave was essentially inaccessible.

The solution, opened in 2022, is Cosquer Méditerranée: a full-scale replica built inside the Villa Méditerranée, the striking cantilevered building beside MuCEM on the waterfront. The replica recreates 1,800 square metres of the cave interior, with 450 animal representations reproduced in detail using photogrammetry and artisan painting. Tickets for timed entry are sold here; booking ahead is strongly recommended in summer.
