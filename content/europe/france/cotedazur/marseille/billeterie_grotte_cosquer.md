In 1985, diving instructor Henri Cosquer swam through a 175-metre submerged tunnel near Cap Morgiou and surfaced inside a dry, painted cave. He kept it secret for years. Only in 1991, after three divers drowned trying to follow him, did he reveal what waited at the end: horses, ibex, aurochs, seals, negative handprints, and possibly great auks — some of the oldest cave art in France, painted around 27,000 and 19,000 years ago.

Here's the twist. When those paintings were made, the sea sat 100 to 120 metres lower and the coast lay kilometres further south, so the cave mouth stood high and dry. Then the Ice Age ended, the ice melted, and the rising sea drowned the lower half of the cave. The art survived up high — but the entrance now lies 37 metres down, reachable only by expert divers with permits.

So they built a copy. Cosquer Méditerranée opened in 2022 inside the Villa Méditerranée, that dramatic cantilevered box beside MuCEM on the waterfront. It recreates 1,800 square metres of cave and 450 animals using photogrammetry and hand-painting. Buy timed tickets here — and book ahead in summer.
