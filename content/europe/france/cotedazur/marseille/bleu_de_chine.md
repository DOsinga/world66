Bleu de Chine stands on the Quai de la Tourette, where the old city meets the industrial docklands of La Joliette, in the 2nd arrondissement. The title refers to a specific shade of deep cobalt-indigo associated with Chinese blue-and-white porcelain — the bleu de Chine that European merchants and collectors pursued for centuries, and that Marseille traders imported via the Compagnie des Indes routes through Lorient and directly through the city's own commercial networks with the Levant and beyond. This particular blue — cool, saturated, carrying the weight of distance — makes an apt colour for a port-city artwork that faces the open sea.

Marseille's relationship with China and with East Asian trade is longer than most Europeans remember. From the seventeenth century onward, Chinese ceramics, lacquerware and silks passed through the port on their way to the aristocratic houses and bourgeois salons of France. The Joliette docks — now being transformed into the Euroméditerranée cultural and commercial quarter — were for generations the point of arrival for goods from across the globe, and the Quai de la Tourette sits at the seam between that industrial heritage and the new city emerging around the MuCEM and the Cathédrale de la Major. Bleu de Chine occupies that threshold thoughtfully, its title connecting a specific colour to a specific history of exchange.

The work rewards slow looking in changing light. In the flat glare of midday it is simply intense; in the golden hour before the sun drops behind the hills to the west, or in the diffuse grey light of a winter morning, the blue seems to deepen and shift, becoming something more atmospheric and harder to place. It is a colour that has always meant travel, rarity and longing — fitting qualities for an artwork on a quay that once dispatched ships toward horizons that seemed genuinely unreachable.
