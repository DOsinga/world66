The whole boulevard exists to frame water arriving. In 1849 the Canal de Marseille finally brought the Durance River 80 kilometres into a city that had spent decades battling drought and epidemic. The Palais Longchamp at the far end is really a glorified water tower — its central cascade, flanked by allegorical rivers and harvests, marks the aqueduct's functional end point.

The street was laid out in the 1860s as the ceremonial run-up: dead-straight, pale stone, plane trees, uniform apartment blocks. It was designed to reveal Espérandieu's over-the-top Second Empire façade as you walked up from the Canebière. That template went on to shape half the 4th and 5th arrondissements.

The palace holds two museums — the Beaux-Arts in the left wing, Natural History in the right — plus a zoo and garden out back. The rotunda and its still-running cascade are open as part of the grounds. Just know most people arrive from the Cinq-Avenues Metro and approach from behind, missing the grand processional view the boulevard was built for.
