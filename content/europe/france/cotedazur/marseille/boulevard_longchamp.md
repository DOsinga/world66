The Canal de Marseille, completed in 1849, brought water from the Durance River 80 kilometres to the northeast — the engineering project that transformed Marseille from a city perpetually threatened by drought and epidemic into an industrial port capable of sustained growth. The Palais Longchamp at the eastern end of the boulevard was built explicitly as the water tower and celebratory façade of the canal's arrival in the city; the central cascade, flanked by allegorical sculptures of rivers and harvests, represents the Durance entering Marseille. The cascade is not decorative — it was the functional end point of the aqueduct.

Boulevard Longchamp was laid out in the 1860s as the ceremonial approach to the palace: a straight, tree-lined processional route in pale stone, designed to frame Espérandieu's exuberant Second Empire façade from the direction of the Canebière. The plane trees, the uniform apartment buildings, and the axial geometry set the template for the residential streets of the 4th and 5th arrondissements that followed during the city's 19th-century expansion.

The Palais Longchamp at the boulevard's terminus houses two separate museums: the Musée des Beaux-Arts in the left wing and the Natural History Museum in the right, with a zoological garden in the grounds behind. The central rotunda, with its cascade still running, is accessible as part of the garden. Most visitors arrive via the Cinq-Avenues Metro station and approach from the east — missing the intended approach from the city and the processional effect the boulevard was designed to create.
