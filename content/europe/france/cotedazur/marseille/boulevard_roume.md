Ernest Roume ran French West Africa from 1902 to 1908 — a territory stretching from Senegal to Mali, Guinea, and Côte d'Ivoire. He's the one who shifted the colonial capital from Saint-Louis to Dakar, reshaping the region's map for decades. École Polytechnique-trained, he was a technocrat obsessed with railways, groundnuts, and rubber, not glory. A manager of a system whose violence was built right in.

Marseille named more streets after colonial figures than almost any French city, because this port was the empire's front door. Troops, administrators, missionaries, and cargo moved through here to Africa, the Levant, Indochina, and Madagascar. Retired officials settled in; trading houses lined the Canebière; and every naming ceremony was the city bragging about its global reach.

The renaming debate flared here after 2020, but France moves slowly — a 2022 national discussion produced recommendations and no law. Some streets tied to slavery got quietly rechristened; Boulevard Roume didn't. Wide, traffic-heavy, lined with low-rise shops, it's forgettable enough that most people walk past the name without ever reading it.
