This is where the Calanques stop being a day trip. The road in is closed to cars every summer, so you walk — two hours each way on marked trail through scrub and white limestone. What you get at the end isn't just a stunning inlet between towering walls; it's a place that still works on its own terms.

A dozen fishing boats work the tiny harbour here. The cabanons — stone huts with tile roofs, built from the 1800s as weekend retreats — sit inside a national park on long city leases, a quirk of law that lets people actually live in the wild. So you'll find laundry on a line, grilling fish, a transistor radio going, all under the biggest Aleppo pine grove in the Calanques.

One bar-restaurant sits on the quay, and that's the extent of commerce. It runs in season and serves food when it has supplies. Don't show up assuming it's open.
