Morgiou is where the Calanques stop being a day trip and become a destination that asks something of you. The road from the city is closed to cars in summer; the walk from the nearest road-access point takes around two hours each way on marked trail through scrub and limestone. The reward is not just the inlet — though the inlet, set between high white walls and tapering to a narrow harbour mouth — but the atmosphere of a place that still functions on its own terms rather than for visitors.

A dozen fishing boats work the Morgiou harbour, moored alongside the quay of what is technically a hamlet. The cabanons — stone huts with tile roofs, built from the 19th century onward as weekend retreats — are occupied by a community with a peculiar legal status: they exist inside a national park, their owners hold them on long leases from the city, and the park management tolerates a working human presence that would be incompatible with strict conservation doctrine elsewhere. The result is an inhabited wildness: someone's laundry on a line, a smell of grilling fish, a transistor radio, all set against the largest single Aleppo pine grove in the Calanques.

The bar-restaurant at the harbourside is the only commercial establishment; it operates in season and serves food when it has supplies. Do not arrive assuming it will be open.
