Cavallo, San Marco II stands on Place Auguste et François Carli in the 1st arrondissement, a short distance from the Vieux-Port and the old commercial quarter of the city. The title places it in direct conversation with the four gilded bronze horses of the Basilica di San Marco in Venice — among the most well-travelled sculptures in history. Originally made for the Hippodrome of Constantinople, probably in antiquity, they were looted by Venetian crusaders in 1204 during the Fourth Crusade's catastrophic sack of Constantinople, shipped to Venice, and installed above the façade of Saint Mark's Basilica, where they remained for six centuries as a symbol of Venetian maritime dominance and the rewards of conquest. Napoleon had them removed to Paris in 1797; they were returned after his defeat. The horses now inside the basilica are replicas; the originals stand in the museum within. This Marseille work, numbered II, inserts itself into that chain of displacement and reproduction.

The Mediterranean that connects Marseille to Venice is the same sea over which those horses were carried, first from Constantinople to Venice, then from Venice to Paris and back. The competitive relationship between Venice and Genoa — and to a lesser extent Marseille — for control of eastern Mediterranean trade routes is one of the great economic dramas of the medieval world. Genoa and Venice fought ruinously for supremacy; Marseille operated under the protection of the French crown and developed its own eastern connections. The four horses of San Marco were always also horses of empire, their gleaming forms broadcasting to every arriving merchant and ambassador who controlled the sea. A contemporary work that takes them as its subject necessarily inherits that freight.

The placement of Cavallo, San Marco II on a public square in a city that competes with Venice for the title of most theatrically beautiful Mediterranean port gives the work a dry wit as well as a cultural weight. The square itself is modest, neighbourhood-scaled, which only sharpens the contrast with the grandeur of the reference. The work asks what it means to copy something that was itself a copy of conquest — and whether that question has a different answer in Marseille than it does in Venice or Paris.
