The four bronze horses above the door of Venice's San Marco Basilica are among the most-travelled sculptures in history. Made in antiquity, they stood at the Hippodrome of Constantinople until 1204, when Venetian crusaders sacked the city and shipped them back to Venice as trophies. Napoleon took them to Paris in 1797. They were returned after Waterloo. The ones you see now on the basilica are replicas — the originals are inside, in the museum.

This piece on Place Auguste et François Carli is numbered II. A copy of a copy of a looting. That layering is the whole point — the work asks what it means to reproduce something that was itself taken, renamed, and reproduced. Placed in a city that competed with Venice for control of Mediterranean trade routes, the question has a particular edge.

The square itself is modest, neighbourhood-scale. That contrast with the grandeur of the reference isn't accidental.
