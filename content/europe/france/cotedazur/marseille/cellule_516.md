Cellule 516 is located on Rue Georges Moreau in the Grand Saint-Giniez quarter of the 8th arrondissement, a residential neighbourhood in the south of the city. The address places it in the orbit of the former Baumettes prison, which operated for decades as Marseille's main house of detention and whose complex history includes the incarceration of political prisoners, résistants, and collaborators during the Second World War, as well as the long catalogue of ordinary criminal justice that any major city's prison accumulates. Baumettes became a byword for overcrowding, violence and institutional failure, and its name carries a weight in Marseille that extends well beyond those who passed through it.

A cell number is among the most reductive designations a state can assign a human being: not a name but a location within a system, a coordinate in a grid of confinement. Cellule 516 takes that act of reduction as its subject. By extracting a single number from the anonymous mass of the prison record and placing it in public space, the work forces a reckoning with what those numbers meant — who occupied cell 516, under what circumstances, on whose authority, and with what outcome. Memorial art of this kind operates by making the private legible from the outside: the experience of detention is hidden by definition, conducted behind walls that the free city never sees, and the installation proposes that the street need not be an accomplice to that invisibility.

The neighbourhood around Rue Georges Moreau is quiet and largely residential, removed from both the tourist waterfront and the grittier quarters of the north. Encountering Cellule 516 here, in an unexpected domestic setting rather than on the prison's walls themselves, underlines the point that confinement is not a matter only for those inside it. The weight of 516 — as a number, as a stand-in for all the other numbers — belongs to the city as a whole.
