Chambord is the vast Loire Valley hunting lodge Francis I started building in 1519 — double-helix staircase, 440 rooms, a roofline that looks like a small city. Down here in the southern arrondissements near the Corniche, the name attaches to something considerably more modest.

This is a thoroughly French habit: naming a café, hotel, or brasserie after a monument that belongs to the whole nation. A Chambord in Endoume or Périer would have been a neighbourhood fixture — somewhere regulars went without ever asking why it was called that.

The full story behind this particular Chambord is unrecorded. But the gesture of the name — pulling one of the great set-pieces of French architectural history down to a street corner in the south — is itself a quiet assertion that Marseille belongs to the same cultural republic as the Loire Valley.
