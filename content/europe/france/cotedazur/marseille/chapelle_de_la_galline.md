The name alone earns a second look. *Galline* is the Provençal and Old French word for a hen or chicken, and in the landscape of Provence — where churches and chapels were named after local springs, ridges, landowners, and legends — a chapel called *la Galline* sits at the intersection of etymology and myth. The coordinates place this chapel in the far north-western reaches of Marseille, in the arc of hills behind l'Estaque where the city thins into limestone scrub and old tile-works, far from the tourist circuits of the Vieux-Port.

One thread of explanation is purely topographical: Provençal placenames frequently preserve the names of early landowners, and a medieval holding belonging to a family called *Galline* or *Gallina* would naturally lend its name to any chapel built on or near the estate. But the folk tradition is more colourful. Across Provence and Languedoc there survive dozens of legends in which a miraculous chicken or hen — sometimes golden, sometimes glowing — reveals the site of a hidden statue of the Virgin or a sacred spring, prompting the founding of a chapel on that spot. Whether such a story once circulated about this particular site is now impossible to verify, but the pattern is consistent enough that the name alone invites the suspicion.

The chapel sits in a quartier that retains something of the village character that the northern arrondissements of Marseille maintained well into the twentieth century — small, close-grained, slightly apart from the main city. Visitors who make their way up to l'Estaque for the industrial-port light that once drew Cézanne and Braque will find the Chapelle de la Galline a rewarding detour into the quieter, stranger hinterland of the city.
