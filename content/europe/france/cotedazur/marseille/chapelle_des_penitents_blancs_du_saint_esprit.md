In early modern Marseille you could watch a full confraternity procession move through the narrow lanes of the old town: men in hooded robes — white, black, blue, or grey depending on the brotherhood — bearing a painted banner, singing as they went. The Pénitents Blancs du Saint-Esprit were one such brotherhood. Their speciality was the kind of corporate charity that individual Catholics couldn't manage alone: ransoming captives from Barbary pirates, burying the destitute, accompanying condemned men to the scaffold.

Marseille in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries supported an unusual concentration of these confraternities. The Pénitents Blancs, Noirs, Gris, and Bleus each had their own chapel, their own feast day, their own charitable niche. This chapel, between the Panier quarter and the northern slopes of the old city, was the ceremonial centre of the White Penitents — both their meeting place and a neighbourhood oratory for the wider parish.

The Revolution dissolved confraternities as corporate bodies and most of their chapels were repurposed or sold. This one survived as a building. It's an architectural witness to the dense web of lay piety and neighbourhood solidarity that once ran alongside — and sometimes in competition with — the official parish structures of the city.
