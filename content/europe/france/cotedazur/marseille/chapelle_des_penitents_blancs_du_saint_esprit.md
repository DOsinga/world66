Few sights were more striking in the streets of early modern Marseille than a confraternity in full procession: men in hooded robes — white, black, blue, or grey depending on their brotherhood — bearing a painted banner or a heavy wooden crucifix through the narrow lanes of the old town, singing as they went. The Pénitents Blancs du Saint-Esprit were one such confraternity, a lay brotherhood founded to perform acts of corporate piety and charity that individual Catholics could not easily accomplish alone. Their name refers both to the white robes they wore and to the Holy Spirit under whose patronage they placed themselves.

Marseille in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries supported an unusual density of Penitent confraternities. The Pénitents Blancs, Noirs, Gris, and Bleus each maintained their own chapel, their own patron feast, and their own charitable specialities — ransoming Christian captives from Barbary pirates, providing a dignified burial to the destitute, accompanying condemned men to the scaffold. The White Penitents of the Holy Spirit occupied a niche in this ecology, their chapel serving as both the ceremonial centre of the brotherhood and a neighbourhood oratory open to the wider parish. The coordinates place this chapel in the zone between the Panier quarter and the northern slopes of the old city, a neighbourhood where confraternities had deep roots in the social fabric.

The Revolution of 1789 dissolved confraternities as corporate bodies, and many of their chapels were sold, suppressed, or turned to other uses. Some brotherhoods survived in attenuated form into the nineteenth century; a handful persist today as ceremonial societies. What remains of this chapel is a modest but historically charged building — an architectural witness to the web of lay piety and neighbourhood solidarity that once ran alongside, and sometimes in competition with, the official parish structures of the city.
