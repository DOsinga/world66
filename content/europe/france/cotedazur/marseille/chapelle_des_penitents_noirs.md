The penitent brotherhoods were lay confraternities that performed works of mercy from the 13th century onward: running hospitals, ransoming galley slaves, and collecting and burying the unclaimed dead. They were distinguished by the colour of their hooded robes — black, white, grey, or blue — and each brotherhood had its own patron, its own chapel, and its own specialised charitable function. The Pénitents Noirs, dedicated to Notre-Dame de la Miséricorde, took on the most confronting of these roles: accompanying condemned prisoners from prison to the place of execution, ensuring a priest was present, and giving the body a proper burial afterward.

The chapel was built in the 17th century on the slopes of Le Panier and retains its original Baroque interior decoration, including an 18th-century altarpiece — a rarity in Marseille, where the Revolution stripped most churches of their furnishings. The survival of both the building and its decorative programme is partly explained by the brotherhood's own continuity: the Pénitents Noirs were not dissolved and reconstituted after the Revolution but persisted, and the chapel was returned to active use rather than repurposed indefinitely.

The brotherhood still exists and continues to use the chapel. Visiting when it is open gives access to one of the most intact Baroque religious interiors in the city, tucked into a Le Panier side street away from the main tourist circuit.
