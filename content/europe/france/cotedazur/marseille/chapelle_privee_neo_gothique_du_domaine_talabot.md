Paulin Talabot (1799–1879) was one of the defining engineers of the French railway age. A graduate of the École Polytechnique, he surveyed and promoted the route of the Paris–Lyon–Marseille railway — the PLM line — at a time when the question of which corridor would link the capital to the Mediterranean was still fiercely contested. The PLM became the backbone of French rail, and Talabot became a very wealthy man. The Talabot family subsequently established itself among Marseille's industrial patriciate, and the *domaine Talabot* in the hills south of the city was their country seat: part working estate, part retreat from the noise of the port and the railway workshops below.

The private chapel built on the estate reflects a specific moment in French bourgeois Catholicism. The nineteenth century's industrial fortunes were often accompanied by conspicuous religious building: factory owners endowed parish churches; railway magnates commissioned private chapels in the Gothic Revival style then fashionable across France and England. Neo-Gothic — with its pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and stained-glass lancets — carried associations of medieval Catholic piety, aristocratic lineage, and an ordered Christian society that the new industrial families found reassuring. For a family like the Talabots, whose wealth was inseparable from the modernity of steam and steel, a Gothic chapel on the family estate was also a statement of social legitimacy.

The chapel sits on the southern edge of Marseille in a zone of large nineteenth-century properties that once formed a belt of bourgeois retreats between the city and the calanques. Today the neighbourhood is more densely built, and the Talabot estate has been subdivided or adapted, but the chapel itself — its pointed gables and carved stonework slightly incongruous amid the Mediterranean landscape — remains as a privately held monument to the family that helped lay the iron road to Paris.
