The coordinates of this Chapelle Saint-Jean — on the northern jaw of Marseille's ancient harbour, within metres of Fort Saint-Jean — make its identity unmistakable. Fort Saint-Jean has guarded the mouth of the Vieux-Port since the seventeenth century, when Louis XIV ordered its construction after his dispute with the city, but the site is much older: a Hospitaller commandery stood here in the medieval period, and the name *Saint-Jean* belongs first to the Knights of Saint John — the Knights Hospitaller — who took their name from Saint John the Baptist and whose red-crossed banner was once familiar across the medieval Mediterranean.

Saint John the Baptist held a peculiar place in medieval religious culture: forerunner of Christ, voice crying in the wilderness, the last prophet and the first of the new dispensation. His feast on the 24th of June — Midsummer — was one of the great popular celebrations of the Christian year, marked with bonfires across Provence and celebrated with particular intensity in port cities where the summer solstice carried maritime significance. A chapel to Saint John at the harbour entrance was therefore both a statement of Hospitaller authority and a practical oratory for the sailors and pilgrims who moved through the port.

The chapel today sits within the compound of Fort Saint-Jean, which since 2013 has been connected to the MuCEM — the Museum of European and Mediterranean Civilisations — by a dramatic footbridge over the water. Visitors who cross that bridge and explore the fort's ramparts and gardens will find the chapel occupying its ancient position at the threshold between the city and the sea, its dedication to Saint John serving as a quiet reminder that Marseille's relationship with the Mediterranean is older, and stranger, than any modern building can fully contain.
