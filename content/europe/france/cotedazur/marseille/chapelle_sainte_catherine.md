Saint Catherine of Alexandria — martyred around 305 CE, according to tradition, on a spiked breaking-wheel before her eventual beheading — was one of the most widely venerated saints of the medieval West. Patron of students, philosophers, lawyers, wheelwrights, and unmarried women, her feast on 25 November was a popular holiday across Catholic Europe. In France the day carried a particular custom: young women of marriageable age, called *catherinettes*, wore hats of yellow and green — the colours of faith and wisdom — in parades through town. The cult of Saint Catherine ran especially deep in Mediterranean port cities, where merchants and sailors often dedicated chapel chapels to her alongside the Virgin; in Provence alone, dozens of chapels bear her name.

This small chapel sits at the edge of Le Panier, Marseille's oldest quarter, on a coordinate axis almost level with the Fort Saint-Jean and the mouth of the Vieux-Port. The density of medieval devotional buildings in this district reflects Marseille's character as both a working harbour and a city threaded with religious confraternities, monastic houses, and parish chapels that served distinct craft guilds or neighbourhoods. A chapel dedicated to Saint Catherine in this location would have served the scholarly or merchant classes of the district — her patronage of the learned made her a natural choice for any neighbourhood that housed a school, a notarial office, or a community of traders who valued literacy.

The Catherine wheel, the instrument of her failed execution (the wheel broke apart, according to legend, before a sword completed the martyrdom), became one of the most recognisable symbols in European heraldry and church decoration. Look for it in any carved stonework or surviving ironwork around the chapel entrance: even after centuries of Revolution-era damage to Marseille's religious buildings, traces of this iconography sometimes survive in doorway mouldings and window grilles.
