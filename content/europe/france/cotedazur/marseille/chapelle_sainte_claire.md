Clare of Assisi (1194–1253) followed Francis of Assisi's radical embrace of poverty and founded the Order of Poor Ladies — known today as the Poor Clares — in 1212. What makes Clare singular in medieval religious history is that she is the first woman known to have written a monastic rule that received papal approval: the *Rule of Saint Clare*, confirmed just two days before her death in 1253, enshrined the principle of absolute poverty not just for individual nuns but for the community as a whole, refusing the right to hold corporate property. The Poor Clares are an enclosed, contemplative order, meaning the sisters remain within the convent walls; their work is prayer rather than active ministry, and their austere way of life was radical even by 13th-century standards.

The Franciscan network spread rapidly through the Mediterranean world in the decades after Francis's death in 1226, and port cities like Marseille were natural nodes in that expansion. Ships carried Franciscan friars and Clarissine nuns between Spain, southern France, Italy, and the Levant; the order's ethos of poverty sat interestingly alongside the mercantile wealth of the cities that hosted them. Marseille's position as the principal French port on the western Mediterranean made it a relay point in this network, and a Poor Clare presence here — whether a full convent or a chapel attached to one — would have connected the city to houses in Provence, Catalonia, and across the Italian peninsula.

The chapel's coordinates, in the hilly southern arrondissements away from the busy waterfront, suggest a community that sought the relative quiet of the city's upper neighbourhoods — consistent with the enclosed life the Poor Clares followed. Today the building is a quiet landmark in a residential district, its Franciscan origins remembered in the dedication even if the community itself no longer occupies the site.
