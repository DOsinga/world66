Château Borély was built between 1767 and 1778 for Louis Borély, a Marseille merchant from an old trading family, as a pleasure pavilion — a country estate within reach of the city, designed by architect Esprit-Joseph Brun in a restrained neo-classical style. The commission was typical of the prosperous mercantile class that made 18th-century Marseille one of the wealthiest cities in France: a house built for entertaining and display rather than permanent residence, set in formal gardens laid out to complement the main building.

The estate became public in 1863 and the Parc Borély is now one of Marseille's largest and most popular parks, with a boating lake, rose garden, and the circuit de vitesse JP Wimille running along its perimeter. The château houses the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, de la Faïence et de la Mode — commonly called the Musée de la Faïence — with a collection of around 1,200 pieces of European ceramics that makes it the largest such holding in France outside the national collection at Sèvres.

Marseille's own faïence tradition is given particular attention. The city was a major production centre in the 18th century, distinguished by a characteristic palette of turquoise and yellow — colours derived from tin-glazed earthenware techniques introduced from Italy and refined by local workshops. The collection also covers production from Moustiers, Apt, and other Provençal centres alongside Italian, Dutch, and Spanish examples, tracing the spread of tin-glazed ceramics across Europe from the Renaissance onward.
