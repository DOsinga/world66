Bonneveine is one of Marseille's quieter residential quarters, occupying the southern edge of the city between the Prado roundabout and the sea. It is a neighbourhood of apartment buildings, small squares, and the kind of everyday commerce that serves a local population rather than visitors — the Prado beaches, created in the 1970s using rubble excavated during the Vieux-Port urban clearance, lie a short walk to the south-west, drawing summer crowds who mostly pass through Bonneveine without stopping. The cinema here is a neighbourhood institution of the sort that has become increasingly rare in French cities, serving the families and residents of the surrounding streets.

Marseille has a genuine cinema culture, shaped partly by its role as a location for French crime films — the city's distinctive backdrop of waterfront, hills, and dense urban texture made it the natural setting for the polar genre, and the Marseille thriller has its own recognised tradition. The city also hosts the FIDMarseille international documentary and fiction film festival each summer, which has made it a fixture on the French arthouse circuit. A neighbourhood cinema like the one in Bonneveine occupies a different register: it is where the same population that lives and works in a quarter goes to see films on a weekday evening.

The Prado beaches nearby were Marseille's answer to the question of what to do with the rubble from the massive urban renewal programme around the Vieux-Port in the early 1970s. The material was used to extend the seafront southward, creating artificial beaches backed by parkland along the Boulevard du Prado — a transformation that gave the southern city a coastline it had never had and altered the character of Bonneveine's relationship with the sea.
