The Clocher des Accoules is the oldest standing structure in Marseille, a Romanesque bell tower built in the twelfth century as part of the church of Notre-Dame-des-Accoules on the heights of Le Panier. The church itself had been one of the most important in the city for centuries before the Revolution reduced it: the building was requisitioned as a saltpetre depot, then substantially demolished. The tower survived and was built into the side of a later apartment block, where it stands today looking as though the city simply grew around it — which, over eight hundred years, it did.

The name Accoules comes from the Latin Aquolae, meaning springs or small waters. The freshwater sources on this hill were what made the site habitable from the earliest phases of Greek settlement, and the neighbourhood that grew up around them became the oldest quarter of Massalia. A small nineteenth-century oratory was later reconstructed at the base of the tower to restore some sense of the original religious function; it is easy to pass without noticing either the oratory or the tower, since both are absorbed into the dense fabric of Le Panier's streets.

The tower is best approached from the Rue du Panier or the Place des Moulins. Its plain Romanesque stonework — undecorated, functional, built to last — reads differently from the Baroque churches and later monuments that fill most of the city, and that contrast is part of what makes it worth finding. Standing beneath it, you are standing on the oldest inhabited ground in Marseille.
