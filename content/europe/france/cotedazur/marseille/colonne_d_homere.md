The Colonne d'Homère stands on the Allées de Meilhan, the planted promenade above the Canebière, bearing a bust of the poet above a commemorative inscription. Homer has no established connection to Marseille; the city was founded by Greeks from Phocaea in Asia Minor around 600 BC, a century or more after the Homeric poems are conventionally dated, and the Phocaean settlers are not mentioned in either the Iliad or the Odyssey. The column was erected in 1841 nonetheless.

The logic was political rather than historical. Marseille in the 1830s and 1840s was engaged in a sustained effort to assert its cultural identity against the dominance of Paris, which was reshaping French national life through centralisation. The city commissioned several monuments during this period linking itself to the foundations of Western civilisation — a city with Greek origins could claim Homer as a symbolic ancestor, and a symbolic ancestor who predated Rome gave it a depth that no northern French city could match. The column was one monument in a programme, not an isolated eccentricity.

It remains where it was placed, on the Allées de Meilhan between the flower market and the upper Canebière. It is easy to walk past without registering it; the interest lies not in the object itself but in what the act of erecting it reveals about how Marseille understood and argued for its own identity in the 19th century.
