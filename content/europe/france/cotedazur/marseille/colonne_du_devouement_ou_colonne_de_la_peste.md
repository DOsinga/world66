The Colonne du Dévouement — also called the Colonne de la Peste — commemorates the great plague of 1720, in which approximately half of Marseille's population of 90,000 died within two years. The epidemic arrived aboard the merchant vessel Grand-Saint-Antoine in May 1720 and spread through the city despite efforts to contain it; it ultimately killed an estimated 50,000 Marseillais and a further 50,000 in Provence before it burned itself out. The column belongs to the tradition of votive monuments erected after epidemic catastrophe: the community that survives gives thanks in stone, fixing the memory of the ordeal in a permanent public form.

The column's name, "of devotion," nods to the individuals — Belsunce, Roze, the religious orders, the volunteer burial parties — whose courage held the city together when its normal structures collapsed. The 1720 plague was for centuries the event through which Marseille understood itself: the city that survived the unsurvivable, whose port had brought the disease and whose people had endured it. The memorial language around the epidemic is saturated with the vocabulary of devotion and sacrifice rather than of blame.

The coordinates place it in the neighbourhood of Notre-Dame de la Garde and the Fontaine Espérandieu — a cluster of sacred and commemorative monuments on the hill above the old port. This proximity is apt: the plague of 1720 was the defining catastrophe behind much of Marseille's subsequent votive architecture, and the basilica above is itself partly understood as a long-term expression of the gratitude that animated the original column.
