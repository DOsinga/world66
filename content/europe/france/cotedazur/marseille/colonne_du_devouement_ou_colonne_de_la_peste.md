In May 1720 the merchant ship Grand-Saint-Antoine sailed into the Vieux-Port carrying plague. Within two years, roughly 50,000 of Marseille's 90,000 inhabitants were dead — half the city. Another 50,000 died across Provence before it burned out. It was one of the worst epidemics in European history, and Marseille was at the centre of it.

The column's name — "of devotion" — points at the people who held the city together while it was collapsing: the bishop Belsunce, who stayed when most officials fled; Nicolas Roze, who organised burial parties knowing it was likely a death sentence; the religious orders who kept collecting the dead. The memorial language around 1720 was always about sacrifice rather than blame.

The column sits near Notre-Dame de la Garde and the Fontaine Espérandieu — a cluster of votive monuments on the hill above the port. That proximity is no accident. Much of Marseille's sacred architecture on these heights is, in one way or another, an expression of the gratitude that started here.
