Pierre Puget was born in Marseille in 1620, trained in Italy under Pietro da Cortona and later in contact with Bernini, and became the most accomplished sculptor France produced in the 17th century. The works that established his reputation are in the Louvre: Milon de Crotone (1682), which shows the athlete's hand trapped in a split tree as a lion attacks, and Perseus and Andromeda (1684). Both were commissioned for Versailles. He also designed the oval Baroque chapel at the Vieille Charité and produced extensive work for Marseille's churches and the arsenal at Toulon.

The tension with Versailles is central to Puget's story. Colbert commissioned him and championed his work, but Louis XIV repeatedly rejected or set aside his most ambitious projects — the scale and expressiveness of Puget's style sat uneasily alongside the measured classicism that Le Brun was enforcing at court. The remark attributed to Colbert, that Puget's works were "too magnificent for the king," may be apocryphal, but it captures a genuine incompatibility. Puget spent much of his career in Toulon and Genoa rather than Paris, working at a remove from the court machinery that controlled major French patronage.

The column near the Cours Belsunce is a modest civic monument to a sculptor whose work outran it. The Vieille Charité chapel, which Puget also designed, is a more fitting memorial — visible a few hundred metres to the north in the Panier district.
