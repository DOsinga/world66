The Colosses Cosmiques — Cosmic Colossi — occupy space in the northern arc of Marseille, close to the latitude of the Friche la Belle de Mai, the former tobacco factory that since the 1990s has served as the city's principal laboratory for experimental art, music, and urban culture. Whether or not the installation is directly associated with the Friche, it belongs to the same cultural geography: a part of the city that has absorbed wave after wave of creative investment as post-industrial spaces became available and affordable.

The name itself reaches for mythological scale. Colossi, in the ancient world, were statues so large they became landmarks — the Colossus of Rhodes being the paradigm case. To label an artwork cosmic is to claim not merely size but a kind of universal or celestial ambition, figures that belong to the order of planets and constellations rather than streets and squares. Whether the Marseille installation literalises that ambition in humanoid forms, abstract masses, or something more oblique is not recorded in the visible record, but the title alone signals that the work intends to be felt rather than merely noticed.

Marseille in the years around its 2013 European Capital of Culture designation saw a surge of exactly this kind of monumental public commission — works placed in rough, under-finished urban space, asking large questions about belonging, mythology, and the city's relationship to the Mediterranean world it has faced for more than two and a half millennia.
