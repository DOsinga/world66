Few cities in western Europe carry a heavier medical history than Marseille. In 1720 the ship *Grand Saint-Antoine*, arriving from the Levant, brought bubonic plague into the port. The epidemic that followed killed roughly half the city's population and spread through Provence before finally being contained — it was the last great outbreak of plague in France, and the catastrophe permanently shaped European thinking about quarantine, port sanitation, and public health governance. The *lazaret* system, already in use at Marseille before 1720, was tightened and codified in the epidemic's aftermath, making the city an unlikely pioneer in the modern science of infectious disease control.

The Conservatoire du Patrimoine Médical de Marseille preserves and interprets this history. Its collections span surgical instruments, apothecary equipment, hospital records, and documents relating to the city's long involvement in tropical medicine — a field that grew directly from Marseille's colonial role as the gateway to France's North African and sub-Saharan empire. The Assistance Publique – Hôpitaux de Marseille (AP-HM), one of the largest hospital groups in Europe, has its roots in institutions that date back centuries, and the conservatory holds materials tracing that institutional continuity.

What distinguishes this collection from generic medical museums is its insistence on Marseille's specific geography: a city that received ships from plague-ridden ports, soldiers returning from tropical postings, and migrants carrying unfamiliar pathologies. The medical history here is also a history of the Mediterranean as a zone of contact, contagion, and eventual understanding.
