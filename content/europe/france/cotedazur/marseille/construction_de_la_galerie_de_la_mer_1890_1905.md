Between 1890 and 1905 Marseille was one of the busiest construction sites in France. The city's port, already the largest in the country, was being comprehensively rebuilt and extended to handle the surging traffic generated by France's expanding colonial empire in North Africa, West Africa, and Indochina. New docks, warehouses, transit sheds, and maritime infrastructure rose along the Joliette waterfront, reshaping a coastline that had changed little since the Second Empire. It was in this decade and a half of feverish port expansion that the Galerie de la Mer took shape.

The gallery — the name refers to an arcade or covered passage oriented toward the sea — was conceived as a functional and ceremonial structure linking the commercial port apparatus to the city it served. Architecture of this era in Marseille drew freely on classical and Orientalist motifs, reflecting a city that understood itself as the pivot between Europe and the Mediterranean south. The fifteen years of construction coincided with the Third Republic's most confident phase of imperial self-projection, and buildings raised in Marseille during this period were intended to announce that confidence to every ship entering the Vieux-Port or the new docks to the north.

This site marks the footprint and memory of that construction effort. What was built here between 1890 and 1905 belongs to a moment when Marseille genuinely competed with Genoa and Barcelona for dominance over the western Mediterranean trade, and when the city's civic and commercial elites believed, not without reason, that their port was on its way to becoming the greatest in the world.
