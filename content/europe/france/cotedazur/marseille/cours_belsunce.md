In 1720 the plague arrived by ship and moved through Marseille with terrifying speed. Within two years, roughly 50,000 of the city's 90,000 people were dead. The upper classes fled. Most magistrates fled. Most clergy fled. Bishop Henri François Xavier de Belsunce, in charge since 1709, refused.

He walked publicly through infected streets, distributed food, administered last rites outdoors. His decision was theological — a bishop does not abandon his flock — and it made him famous across Catholic Europe. Voltaire put his name in *Candide* as a shorthand for selfless courage. The Chevalier de Roze, who organised the removal of rotting corpses when the city ran out of burial capacity, gets his own memorial nearby. The bishop and the nobleman each took a different side of the same catastrophe.

The boulevard today is the main commercial artery of a quarter that has been predominantly North African since the 1960s — discount clothing, phone-card vendors, covered market. Very little in common with the Baroque bishop whose name it carries. But the name itself is genuine history.
