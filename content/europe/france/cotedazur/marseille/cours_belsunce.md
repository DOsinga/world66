The plague of 1720 was the last major outbreak of bubonic plague in Western Europe. It arrived by ship from the Levant and moved through Marseille with extraordinary speed, killing roughly 50,000 of the city's 90,000 inhabitants within two years. The upper classes, most magistrates, and the majority of the clergy fled. Henri François Xavier de Belsunce, bishop since 1709, refused to go.

Belsunce remained in Marseille throughout the epidemic, walking publicly through infected districts, distributing food, administering last rites in the streets. His decision was theological — a bishop should not abandon his flock — and practical. It made him famous across Catholic Europe. Voltaire used his name in *Candide* as a shorthand for selfless heroism. The Chevalier de Roze, who organised the removal of corpses from the streets when the city ran out of burial capacity, is commemorated separately in Marseille; the bishop and the nobleman each took a different side of the catastrophe.

Today the boulevard is the main commercial artery of the Belsunce quarter, which has been predominantly North African since the 1960s and serves as a major shopping destination for Marseille's immigrant communities. The discount clothing shops, phone-card vendors, and covered market that line it have little in common with the Baroque bishop for whom the street is named, but the name itself carries a genuine history of crisis and refusal.
