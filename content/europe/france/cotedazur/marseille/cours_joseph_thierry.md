This boulevard was laid out in the 1850s and 1860s, part of the same Haussmann-era push that restructured Marseille's street network as the port boomed and the population surged. The formula was straightforward: cut a wide planted cours through the medieval street pattern, name it after a respectable parliamentarian, announce civic order.

It connects Belsunce — the North African commercial district around the Rue d'Aix — to Belle de Mai, the working-class neighbourhood to the northeast. The trees and the generous width are about the only physical evidence of that original ambition. The buildings along it are 19th-century apartment blocks in variable condition, small shops, nothing that's been touched by Marseille's post-2000 cultural investments.

It's not a destination. It's a route. Walk it for a more accurate picture of ordinary Marseille than the Vieux-Port or the regenerated Joliette can offer.
