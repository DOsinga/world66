The Cours Julien was built as a grand 19th-century boulevard. What it became is something else. In the 1980s and 1990s, when rents were low, record shops moved in, then independent bookshops, then cafes with terraces. Street artists started working the walls — first unofficially, then with institutional tolerance. Now entire facades are covered in murals by artists with international reputations, and they change regularly.

The market at the southern end — Place Jean Jaurès, called La Plaine by everyone who lives here — runs Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday mornings. It's been operating in some form since 1789.

The neighbourhood has gentrified without becoming uniform. Long-term residents from North African and Comorian communities share the street with graphic designers who arrived last year. That mixture — Marseille holds it in its inner districts more successfully than most French cities of comparable size. The evening restaurant scene along here and its side streets is among the best in the city for the price.
