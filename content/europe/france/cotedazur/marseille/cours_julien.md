The Cours Julien was laid out as a grand boulevard in the 19th century, part of the same municipal ambition that produced the Canebière and the Préfecture. It runs slightly uphill from the edge of the Noailles market district, broad enough to have planted trees down the centre, lined with the four- and five-storey stone buildings that the Second Empire produced across the south of France.

The bohemian character arrived gradually in the 1980s and 1990s. Record shops moved in when rents were low; independent bookshops and second-hand dealers followed; cafes with terraces opened on the ground floors. Street artists began working the walls — first unofficially, then with increasing institutional tolerance. The murals now cover entire building facades and change regularly; some are by artists with international reputations. The Place Jean Jaurès at the southern end, known locally as La Plaine, holds a market on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday mornings that has operated in some form since 1789.

The neighbourhood has gentrified without becoming uniform. Long-term residents from North African and Comorian communities share the street with graphic designers and musicians who arrived in the last decade. The result is a social mixture that Marseille maintains in its inner districts more successfully than most French cities of comparable size. The evening restaurant scene along the Cours and its side streets is among the best in the city for the price.
