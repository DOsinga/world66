The Cours Saint-Louis is one of Marseille's shorter named thoroughfares — about 150 metres connecting the foot of the Canebière to the Quai de la Fraternité — but it carries the compressed history of the city's medieval commercial importance. The saint in question, Louis IX of France, used Marseille as his embarkation point twice: in 1248 for the Seventh Crusade, which ended in his capture by Egyptian forces, and in 1270 for the Eighth, which ended with his death near Tunis from illness before he reached his objective. The medieval square where his troops assembled was one of the most important in the city.

That square was obliterated in the 19th-century reconstruction that also produced the Bourse shopping centre directly to the north. What remained was a short street lined, every morning, with stalls selling cut flowers and potted plants. The flower market is Marseille's most persistent street commerce — operating in all but the worst weather, drawing both professional buyers from the restaurant and hotel trade and ordinary shoppers making a weekly or daily purchase. The stalls operate roughly from dawn to early afternoon.

The cours sits at exactly the point where the historic city's commercial axis — the Canebière, running from the port inland — meets the water. Everything converges here: the tram line passes the foot of the cours, the ferry to the Château d'If departs from the quai, and the pedestrian traffic of the Vieux-Port spills across it. The flower market is the one element that remains obstinately local in a space that is otherwise heavily touristed.
