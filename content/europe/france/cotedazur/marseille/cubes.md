The Cubes sit in the southern reaches of Marseille, in the latitude band that runs through Mazargues and Bonneveine — the quieter, more residential margin of the city before the terrain rises into the garrigue of the Parc des Calanques. The setting gives geometric abstraction an unusual backdrop: instead of the urban density that typically frames minimalist public sculpture, these forms sit against a horizon of limestone and sea.

The cube as sculptural unit has a durable place in French public art. From the grands ensembles of the 1960s — where cubic apartment blocks were themselves the architecture of social optimism — to the more self-conscious minimalism of the 1980s and 1990s, when public commissions regularly turned to primary geometric forms as a kind of neutral, universally legible vocabulary. Works by artists associated with the CAPC Bordeaux tradition, or with the FNAC public art collection, often deployed cubes and their kin precisely because they carried no narrative freight: they were purely spatial propositions.

Here in Marseille's south, the Cubes function as punctuation marks in a neighbourhood not otherwise known for its public art density. Whether they are stone, steel, or painted concrete, large enough to sit on or imposing enough to walk around, they represent a commitment — modest but genuine — to making abstraction available to people who did not choose to visit a gallery.
