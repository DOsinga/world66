The Église Protestante Unie de France (EPUdF) was created in 2013 through the merger of the Église Réformée de France and the Église Évangélique Luthérienne de France — two strands of French Protestantism that traced their separate histories back to the sixteenth-century Reformation. The union was theologically deliberate: both traditions share a commitment to justification by grace and the authority of scripture, while the merger acknowledged that maintaining parallel denominational structures in a country where Protestants number fewer than three percent of the population made little practical or ecumenical sense. The result is one of the larger Protestant bodies in France, present in cities and towns across the country with a worship style that varies locally between Reformed austerity and Lutheran liturgical warmth.

This building houses one of Marseille's EPUdF congregations. Protestantism has maintained a quiet but persistent presence in Marseille since the Wars of Religion, when the city's commercial connections brought Reformed ideas northward from Geneva and eastward from the Huguenot strongholds of the Languedoc. Marseille's port culture, with its tolerance for minority religious communities born of commercial necessity, made the city more hospitable to Protestant life than many provincial French towns, though it never became a Huguenot stronghold on the scale of Nîmes or La Rochelle.

The neighbourhood around this building reflects the layered social geography of modern Marseille — a city where ethnic, religious, and linguistic communities have long coexisted within walking distance of each other. The congregation here gathers for Sunday worship, pastoral care, and the kind of neighbourhood engagement that French Protestant communities have traditionally seen as an expression of their faith.
