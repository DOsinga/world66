Installed near the northern quays of Marseille, *Désordre* — French for disorder — is a sculptural work that seems to court the very quality its name announces. Bent or scattered forms, depending on the interpretation of the moment, it sits in a city that has always worn its chaos as a badge of honour rather than a source of embarrassment. Marseille's port was for centuries Europe's loudest point of entry: goods, languages, arguments, and ambitions arrived together and sorted themselves out gradually, or not at all.

The piece rewards a slow look. What first reads as accidental — forms tumbling or colliding — reveals an internal order on closer inspection, much as the Panier quarter behind it reveals, beneath its tangled lanes, a grid logic laid down by the Greeks of Massalia 2,600 years ago. Marseille's "disorder" has always been creative disorder: the improvised architectures of communities that arrived with nothing and built something remarkable.

Standing here, with the container cranes visible to the north and the hum of traffic from the Joliette docks, *Désordre* feels less like a provocation and more like a portrait. This is a city that distrusts tidiness on principle, and the artwork is in its element.
