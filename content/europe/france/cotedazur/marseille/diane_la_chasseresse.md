Parc Borély got its share of neoclassical statues in the 19th century, and Diana the Huntress was the period's favourite subject. Parks across France received her — quiver at her back, a hound or stag at her heel, eternally about to chase something. She was popular because she was classically sanctioned: vigorous, nearly naked, and safely ancient.

This is Borély's version. The formal alleys and plane trees are the right setting — this was exactly the kind of sculpture the Belle Époque civic imagination paired with landscaped parkland. Produced in series by foundries serving public commissions, the same Diana appeared in multiple French cities with minor variations in pose. What differs here is the light.

Midi light is harder than Parisian light. It strips the romantic softness these statues were designed to carry. Under it, the goddess looks considerably fiercer than intended. She suits Marseille better for it.
