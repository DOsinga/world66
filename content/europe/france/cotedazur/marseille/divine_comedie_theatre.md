Dante Alighieri completed the *Divina Commedia* in the early fourteenth century, writing in Tuscan vernacular at a moment when Latin was still the prestige language of serious literature. The choice of vernacular was itself a statement: that the spoken languages of the Mediterranean world were adequate to the highest literary ambitions. Marseille, founded by Greek colonists more than two and a half millennia ago, sits within this Mediterranean cultural inheritance — the city that Dante wrote about exists within the same sea, the same trade routes, the same long history.

To name a theatre after the *Commedia* is to claim a place in that tradition. It is a statement of ambition — that what happens here matters, that theatre is continuous with the great project of European literary culture — but also of Mediterranean specificity: the south, the sea, the languages that emerged from Latin and Greek, the culture that the north of Europe sometimes forgets is also European.

Programming at Divine Comédie Théâtre reflects this framing: contemporary work that takes its ambitions seriously, that is not embarrassed by literature or by the past, that sees performance as part of a long conversation rather than a series of isolated events. The scale is intimate — a small venue in the southern arrondissements — but the reach of the name is long.
