Marcel Pagnol was born in Aubagne in 1895, but Marseille made him. When his father Joseph — a primary school teacher in the French *instituteur* tradition — was assigned to posts in the city, the family moved into the school building itself, as was then customary: teachers' lodgings were built into or immediately behind the schoolhouse, tying the educator's domestic life to the institution he served. This arrangement, unremarkable at the time, gave the young Pagnol an unusual intimacy with the rhythms of school life, with the playgrounds and corridors that were simultaneously his neighbourhood and his father's workplace.

Years later, Pagnol distilled these Marseille years into two autobiographical novels that became among the most loved books in the French language. *La Gloire de mon père* (1957) and *Le Château de ma mère* (1958) — together with the posthumously published *Le Temps des secrets* — form a memoir of Provençal childhood that is at once precisely located and universally recognisable. The hills above Aubagne, the summers in the *garrigue*, and the figure of Joseph Pagnol — earnest, bookish, devoted — passed into French cultural memory as emblems of a lost Belle Époque simplicity. The books were adapted into celebrated films in 1990 by Yves Robert, bringing a new generation to the landscape Pagnol had mapped.

This school was one of the Marseille addresses that shaped the writer. Pagnol went on to become a playwright, filmmaker, and the first film director elected to the Académie française (1946). But it was the child who lived here, watching his father teach and spending school holidays in the hills outside the city, who became the source material for everything that followed.
