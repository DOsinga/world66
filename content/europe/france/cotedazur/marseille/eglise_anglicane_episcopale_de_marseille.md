By the mid-19th century Marseille had a substantial British colony: shipping agents, merchants, consular staff, and families attached to P&O liners on the route to India. Wherever the British gathered in sufficient numbers in France, Anglican congregations followed. This church was theirs.

It sits in the quieter residential quarter south of the Vieux-Port, among the consulates and grand bourgeois apartments where Marseille's foreign merchant class once lived. The interior is modest by Continental standards — plain stone, clear glass, wooden pews — but that's Anglican aesthetic, not budget. Scale was never the point; community was.

Today the congregation is small: expatriates, English-speaking visitors, a thread of continuity stretching back nearly two centuries. In a city whose skyline is dominated by Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde and whose street-level religious geography is overwhelmingly Catholic, this quiet Anglican building is something of a curiosity — a reminder that when Marseille was the gateway to the British Empire, the empire brought its own church.
