Marseille was one of the most international ports in the Mediterranean, and by the mid-19th century a substantial British colony had settled here — shipping agents, merchants, consular staff, and the families of officers attached to the Peninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Company (P&O), whose liners called regularly on the route to India and the Far East. Wherever the British gathered in sufficient numbers across France, Anglican congregations followed, and Marseille was no exception. The church was established to give this community its own place of worship, with services conducted in English according to the rites of the Church of England and, later, the Episcopal tradition.

The building sits in the quieter residential and diplomatic quarter south of the Vieux-Port, not far from the consulates and grand bourgeois apartments that once housed Marseille's foreign merchant class. Inside, the scale is modest — this was always a congregation defined by its community rather than by civic ambition — but the interior retains the understated Anglican aesthetic: plain stone, clear glass, and wooden pews. Services today draw a small but loyal congregation of English-speaking expatriates and visitors, maintaining a thread of worship that stretches back nearly two centuries.

It is one of the few functioning Protestant churches in a city whose skyline is dominated by Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde and whose street-level religious geography is overwhelmingly Catholic. For visitors, the church is worth a quiet look from the outside even when closed; the building quietly recalls the era when Marseille's commercial reach extended across the British Empire and the port hummed with the voices of a dozen nations.
