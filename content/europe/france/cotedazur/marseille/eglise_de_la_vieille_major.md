When the colossal new Major Cathedral — the *Cathédrale de la Major* — was consecrated in 1893, the building it replaced was spared demolition, an unusual mercy in a century that tore down medieval structures across France with little sentiment. The *Vieille Major*, the old cathedral, now stands in the shadow of its enormous successor on the Esplanade de la Major, a study in the contrast between Romanesque restraint and 19th-century ecclesiastical ambition. The old building's origins are ancient: a church on this site is documented from the 5th century, making it one of the earliest Christian foundations in Provence. The present Romanesque structure dates largely from the 11th and 12th centuries, with later additions including a 15th-century apse chapel that contains a fine terracotta altarpiece by Francesco Laurana.

The cathedral was dedicated to Saint Mary Major — *Sancta Maria Major* in Latin, which contracted over centuries into *la Major* — aligning it with the Roman basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore and positioning Marseille's mother church within the prestigious lineage of Marian dedications. For centuries this was the bishop's seat and the ceremonial heart of the city. Royal visits, episcopal consecrations, and the funerals of Marseille's most powerful families all passed through its doors. The surrounding district, perched on the high ground at the north-western corner of the Vieux-Port overlooking the sea, gave the cathedral a commanding position visible from ships entering the harbour.

The Vieille Major can be visited today as a smaller companion to its towering neighbour. Its interior has the hushed, worn quality of a building that has outlived its own official importance — the bishop's throne moved next door well over a century ago — but the Romanesque stonework and the Laurana chapel retain real distinction. The contrast between the two buildings, old and new, facing each other on the same esplanade, is one of the more quietly eloquent architectural conversations in the south of France.
