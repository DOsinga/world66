The name "Bon Pasteur" — Good Shepherd — draws on one of the most recognisable images in the Gospels. In John 10:11, Jesus declares "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep," and the figure of a young man carrying a lamb on his shoulders became one of the earliest symbols of Christ in the Roman catacombs. The dedication was not merely devotional: it carried an institutional charge. The Congregation of Our Lady of Charity of the Good Shepherd, founded in Angers in 1835 by Saint Mary Euphrasia Pelletier, established homes across Europe and beyond to shelter and rehabilitate young women who had fallen into poverty, prostitution, or social marginalisation. "Bon Pasteur" churches were often directly affiliated with those institutions, combining worship with a social welfare mission rooted in nineteenth-century Catholic reformism.

In Marseille, a port city that has always drawn migrants, sailors, and the dispossessed, the congregation found fertile ground. The church sits in the northern reaches of central Marseille, in a part of the city that retains a dense, working-class grain. The building itself is modest — a parish church rather than a monument — but its dedication links it to a network of social Catholicism that shaped French urban life from the July Monarchy through the Third Republic. That tradition is still visible in the neighbourhood: the church remains a quiet anchor in a district that has never been the tourist Marseille but has always been the lived-in one.

Visitors who push open the door between services will typically find an intimate interior with little to distract from prayer — no famous relics, no celebrated artwork. What it offers instead is a sense of continuity: the same stone, the same dedication, the same insistence that the shepherd goes looking for the lost sheep. That is not a bad ethos for a church in a port neighbourhood.
