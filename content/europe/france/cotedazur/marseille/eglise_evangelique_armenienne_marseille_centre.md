Most people know that Marseille has a substantial Armenian community, the largest in France, descended from survivors of the 1915 genocide perpetrated by the Ottoman government. What is less widely appreciated is that Armenians brought two separate Christian traditions with them when they fled. The majority belonged to the Armenian Apostolic Church, one of the oldest Christian denominations in the world, which traces its founding to the apostles Thaddaeus and Bartholomew and became the state religion of Armenia in 301 AD, making it the first officially Christian nation. But a significant minority were Armenian Evangelicals — Protestants — whose community had its origins in nineteenth-century American and British missionary activity in the Ottoman Empire. Congregationalist and Presbyterian missionaries established schools and congregations across Anatolia from the 1830s onwards, and a distinct Armenian Protestant identity took shape, with its own liturgy, hymnody, and ecclesiastical structures.

Both communities arrived in Marseille in waves after 1915, when the systematic deportations and massacres that killed an estimated one to one-and-a-half million Armenians drove survivors into exile. The port of Marseille was often the first French soil they reached, and many stayed. The city's Armenian quarter grew up around the northern arrondissements, but the community established institutions — churches, schools, cultural associations — across the city. The Apostolic community built the Cathedral of the Armenian Apostolic Church; the Evangelical community, smaller but tenacious, maintained its own congregation with its own theology and its own sense of Armenian Protestant identity, distinct from both the Apostolic majority and the French Reformed tradition around it.

This church, in the centre of Marseille, is the gathering point for that Evangelical strand. It holds services in Armenian and maintains ties to the Union of Armenian Evangelical Churches in the Near East, the umbrella body for the diaspora congregations. For visitors, it is a quiet monument to the complexity of the Armenian presence in France: a community that survived genocide, crossed the Mediterranean, and kept alive not just a language and a memory but a theological argument about what Armenian Christianity should look like.
