Most people know Marseille has France's largest Armenian community, survivors and descendants of the 1915 genocide. What surprises people is that Armenians arrived with two distinct Christian traditions. The majority were Armenian Apostolic — one of the world's oldest churches, state religion of Armenia since 301 AD. But a significant minority were Armenian Evangelicals, Protestants whose roots traced back to American and British missionary work in Ottoman Anatolia during the 1830s. They had their own liturgy, their own hymnody, their own theological identity — and they kept it.

Both communities washed up through Marseille's port after 1915. The Apostolic majority built their cathedral. This congregation, smaller and tenacious, kept the Evangelical strand alive — holding services in Armenian, maintaining ties to the Union of Armenian Evangelical Churches in the Near East.

It's not a grand building. But the name above the door marks something real: a community that survived genocide, crossed the Mediterranean, and kept arguing about what Armenian Christianity should look like.
