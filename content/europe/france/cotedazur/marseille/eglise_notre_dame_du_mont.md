They started building in 1747 and didn't finish until 1864. Money ran out, work stopped, resumed under different conditions, stopped again. The result is a church that reads as accommodation rather than vision — each phase of construction trying to match what came before. It sits on the slope above Cours Julien, Marseille's street-art-and-record-shops square, which arrived a century after the church finally opened.

The parish is one of the most mixed in the city: students, North African families, artists from the Cours Julien scene, newer arrivals living in cheap housing nearby. The church runs food distribution, legal advice for undocumented residents, pastoral support for families in crisis — alongside Sunday Mass. That social role is increasingly central to what it thinks it is.

From the street it's easy to walk past. Its importance is felt by its neighbourhood, not announced to visitors.
