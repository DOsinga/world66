Work on Notre-Dame du Mont began in 1747 but proceeded in fits and starts — funding interrupted, building suspended, resumed under different conditions — until the church was finally completed in 1864. This drawn-out construction history left its mark on the building, which reads as a work of accommodation rather than a single unified design. It sits on the slope above Cours Julien, the broad square that became Marseille's counterculture hub in the late 20th century, lined with street art, record shops, and music venues.

The parish is one of the most heterogeneous in Marseille: students from the nearby schools and art institutions, long-established North African residents, artists and musicians from the Cours Julien scene, and newer arrivals who move through the neighbourhood's cheap housing and informal economy. The church runs an active programme of social services — food distribution, legal advice for undocumented residents, pastoral support for families in crisis — alongside its religious functions. This dual role is increasingly central to how the church defines itself in its neighbourhood.

From the street, the building is easy to walk past without registering as noteworthy; its interest is less in the architecture than in the combination of location and institutional character. It is the kind of church whose importance is understood by its neighbourhood rather than signalled to visitors.
