France's Protestant story is one of the longest and most turbulent in European religious history. The Huguenots — French Calvinist Protestants — emerged in the mid-sixteenth century and were soon caught in the Wars of Religion that devastated the country for decades. The Edict of Nantes in 1598, issued by Henri IV, granted them substantial freedoms: the right to worship, to hold public office, to garrison certain towns. It was a fragile peace. When Louis XIV revoked the Edict in 1685, an estimated two to three hundred thousand Protestants fled France — to England, the Dutch Republic, Prussia, Switzerland — in the mass emigration known as the Refuge. Those who remained were stripped of legal protection, forced to baptise their children Catholic, and pushed into a clandestine religious life that would persist until the Revolution finally restored civil rights to French Protestants in 1787.

Marseille's Protestant community survived through all of this, a small but tenacious presence in a city that was overwhelmingly Catholic. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought gradual growth and the establishment of permanent congregations. For most of that period, French Protestantism remained divided along the same fault lines as the Reformation itself: Reformed (Calvinist) churches on one side, Lutheran churches on the other. That division ended in 2013, when the two streams merged to form the Église Protestante Unie de France, a single denomination that now encompasses congregations of both heritages.

This congregation on the Grignan side of Marseille's sixth arrondissement — a neighbourhood known for its art galleries and bourgeois streetscape — belongs to that united church. The building sits in a part of the city that feels more Lyonnais than Mediterranean, with its Haussmann-era facades and quiet commerce. The congregation it houses carries a theology formed over five centuries of minority status in a Catholic country: Reformed in its roots, ecumenical in its present, and still shaped by the memory of what it cost French Protestants to hold to a different reading of the Gospels.
