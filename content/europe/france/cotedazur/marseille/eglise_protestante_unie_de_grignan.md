French Protestants have been paying for their theology since the sixteenth century. The Wars of Religion, the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, the mass flight of two to three hundred thousand Huguenots to England, Prussia, and the Dutch Republic — those who stayed behind were stripped of legal protection and pushed underground for over a century. Civil rights only came back in 1787. Marseille's Protestant community held on through all of it: small, tenacious, shaped by what minority faith costs.

For most of that history, French Protestantism remained split between Reformed (Calvinist) and Lutheran traditions. That ended in 2013, when both streams merged into the Église Protestante Unie de France. This congregation, in the sixth arrondissement near the art galleries and quiet Haussmann facades, belongs to that united church.

The building sits in a part of Marseille that feels almost Lyonnais. The theology inside was forged over five centuries of keeping a different reading of the Gospels alive in a country that punished it.
