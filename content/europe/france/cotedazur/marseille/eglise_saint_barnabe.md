Barnabas is one of the more vivid secondary figures of the New Testament. Born a Levite from Cyprus, he appears in the Acts of the Apostles as the man who vouched for the newly converted Saul of Tarsus when the Jerusalem apostles were understandably wary of the man who had been persecuting Christians. That act of trust is what made Paul's ministry possible: without Barnabas as guarantor, Paul might never have been accepted into the inner circle of the early church. The two then set out together on what is counted as Paul's first missionary journey, travelling through Cyprus and Asia Minor, establishing congregations in city after city. They eventually fell out — Acts records a "sharp disagreement" over whether to bring a wavering companion on a second journey — and went their separate ways. Barnabas returned to Cyprus, which has claimed him as its patron saint ever since. His feast day falls on 11 June.

The church of Saint-Barnabé sits in the northeastern quarter of Marseille, in the district that took its name from the church itself — a common pattern in old French cities where a parish church became the organising centre of a neighbourhood's identity. The twelfth arrondissement is a long way in character from the Vieux-Port: quieter, more residential, the Marseille of long avenues and modest apartment blocks rather than tourist terraces and fishing boats. Arriving here, you understand that the city is far larger and more various than its postcard image suggests.

The coincidence of the church's dedication with Marseille's own history as a port is worth a moment's thought. Barnabas crossed the Mediterranean repeatedly in the mid-first century, carrying a new religion from its origins in the eastern Mediterranean westward through the island-hopping trade routes that later brought goods — and ideas, and faiths — to cities like Marseille itself. The church that bears his name stands in a neighbourhood built by exactly that kind of long, slow accumulation of arrivals.
