Without Barnabas, Paul might never have gotten in. When the newly converted Saul of Tarsus showed up in Jerusalem, the apostles were understandably wary of the man who had spent years persecuting Christians. Barnabas vouched for him. That act of trust opened the door, and the two set off together on what became Christianity's first missionary journey — Cyprus, Asia Minor, city after city. They eventually fell out over a personnel disagreement and went separate ways. Barnabas returned to Cyprus, which claimed him as patron saint. His feast falls on 11 June.

The church sits in the northeastern twelfth arrondissement, far from the Vieux-Port in character as well as distance. Long avenues, modest apartment blocks, the Marseille that doesn't make the postcards. The neighbourhood took its name from the church — a common pattern, the parish becoming the organising centre of a quarter's identity before anything else did.

There's a neat coincidence here. Barnabas crossed the Mediterranean repeatedly carrying a new religion westward along trade routes. The city that bears his church's name was built by exactly that kind of slow accumulation of arrivals.
