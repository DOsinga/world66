In 1720 the plague hit Marseille and the official class mostly ran. Bishop Belsunce stayed. He moved through the infected streets, administered last rites, organised what relief was possible while roughly 50,000 of the city's 90,000 residents died in two years. He's buried in this church. The neighbourhood outside still carries his name.

The Dominicans — Prêcheurs, preachers — built the first church on this site in the thirteenth century, a decade after the order's founding. The current building is mostly seventeenth century. What makes it worth stepping into is the paintings by Michel Serre, a Marseille artist who witnessed the epidemic firsthand. His canvases show streets filled with bodies and the work of those trying to clear them — the most detailed visual record the city has of its worst catastrophe.

The neighbourhood outside, busy with commerce, has almost entirely forgotten what happened here. The church preserves it.
