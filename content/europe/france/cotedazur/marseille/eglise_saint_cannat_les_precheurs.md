The Dominicans — known in French as Prêcheurs, preachers — arrived in Marseille in 1225, within a decade of the order's founding by Saint Dominic. They established themselves on what is now the Rue Tapis-Vert and built the first church on this site in the 13th century. The current building is substantially a 17th-century reconstruction, though the foundations of Dominican presence here are eight centuries deep.

The church's most important contents are historical rather than architectural. Bishop Henri de Belsunce — after whom the surrounding neighbourhood is named — is buried here. Belsunce remained in Marseille throughout the plague of 1720, when most of the wealthy and official class fled; he moved through the infected streets, administered last rites, and organised what relief was possible. The epidemic killed roughly 50,000 of Marseille's 90,000 residents in two years. Michel Serre, a Marseille painter who witnessed the plague, documented it in a series of paintings that are now among the church's holdings — the most detailed visual record of that event, showing streets filled with the dead and the work of those trying to clear them.

The paintings make the church effectively a memorial to a catastrophe that shaped the city's modern geography and psychology. The neighbourhood outside, busy with commerce, has almost entirely forgotten what happened here; the church preserves the memory.
