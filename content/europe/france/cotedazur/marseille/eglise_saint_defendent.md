Saint Défendant (Defendens) belongs to the constellation of martyrs from the Theban Legion, one of the most powerful and enduring legends of early Christianity. According to the tradition recorded by Bishop Eucherius of Lyon around 430 CE, the Theban Legion was a Roman military unit recruited in Upper Egypt and composed entirely of Christians. Ordered in 286 CE by Emperor Maximian to help suppress a rebellion in Gaul — and, according to some accounts, to participate in the persecution of Christian civilians — the legion refused. Their commander Maurice and his companions Exuperius, Candidate, Innocent, and many others were massacred at Agaunum (modern Saint-Maurice-en-Valais, Switzerland). Defendens was among the soldiers venerated as one of those martyrs.

The cult of the Theban Legion spread rapidly through the Alpine and Provençal world. Marseille, as the great Roman port of southern Gaul and a city with deep Christian roots, became one of the nodes through which these soldier-martyr dedications filtered westward. The appeal was straightforward: soldiers who chose death over apostasy offered a model of loyalty that transcended military obedience — a figure useful in both military chapels and parish churches serving communities proud of civic courage.

Église Saint-Défendent occupies a working-class neighbourhood in the east-central arrondissements of Marseille, an area that was built out during the city's industrial expansion in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The church itself is modest and solidly built, the sort of parish anchor that knit together a new neighbourhood as it filled with people. The Theban Legion martyrs are not widely remembered today outside specialist circles, but in Provence their names attached themselves to hundreds of chapels, fountains, and feast days — a ghost geography of an older devotional world.
