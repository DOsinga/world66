The legend goes like this: in 286 CE, the emperor Maximian ordered a Roman legion recruited in Egypt — entirely Christian — to suppress a rebellion in Gaul and help persecute Christian civilians. They refused. Their commander Maurice and hundreds of soldiers, including Defendens, were executed on the spot at Agaunum, in what is now Switzerland.

Whether it happened exactly that way, the Theban Legion martyrs became massive in Alpine and Provençal devotion. Marseille, deep-rooted in Roman Christianity, was one of the ports through which that cult spread westward. A soldier who chose death over apostasy was exactly the kind of saint working-class parishes wanted watching over them.

This church serves one of Marseille's east-central neighbourhoods, built out during the industrial expansion of the 19th and early 20th centuries. Modest, solid — the kind of parish anchor that held a new neighbourhood together as it filled with people.
