The most likely patron of this church is Saint Eugene of Carthage (died 505 CE), Bishop of Carthage during one of North Africa's most turbulent centuries. After the Vandal king Huneric expelled all Catholic clergy from their sees in 484 CE, Eugene was sent into exile in the southern Gaul region — the very territory that would eventually become Provence. He returned to Carthage when Huneric's successor Gunthamund reversed the expulsion, only to be exiled again under the next Vandal ruler. He died in exile at Albi, in what is now the Tarn department. His story — a bishop who resisted Arian heresy and political pressure without violence or compromise — made him an enduring model of episcopal steadfastness, and his cult took root in southern France, where he had spent years among the local clergy.

Église Saint-Eugène stands in the western quarters of Marseille, in a neighbourhood of nineteenth and early twentieth-century buildings that climbs toward the hills overlooking the Anse du Pharo and the entrance to the Vieux-Port. This part of the city developed as a prosperous residential district during the Second Empire and early Third Republic, and the church served the bourgeois families who built their villas on these slopes with sea views. Its architecture reflects that era: solid, confident, built to anchor a parish rather than to impress pilgrims.

The western arrondissements of Marseille have their own quieter character — less frenetic than the Noailles market quarter or the Cours Julien arts scene, more given to afternoon strolls and café terraces looking out toward the Frioul islands. Saint-Eugène fits naturally into that rhythm: a neighbourhood church doing the daily work of a parish, its obscure North African patron a small puzzle for anyone curious enough to read the dedication above the door.
