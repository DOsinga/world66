Sitting directly on the Quai de Rive Neuve with its austere Provençal Gothic facade facing the merchant quay, Saint-Ferréol was built by Augustinian friars and completed in 1542 — making it the oldest post-medieval church still standing in the city centre. Its restrained stonework and compact proportions are characteristic of Provençal Gothic: less elaborate than northern French examples, adapted to a Mediterranean building culture that never entirely abandoned classical simplicity. Across the water, the silhouette of the Abbaye Saint-Victor provides an older counterpoint.

The Revolution brought abrupt secularisation. The church was ransacked, stripped of its furnishings, and pressed into service as a depot — a fate shared by religious buildings across France. It was reconsecrated in 1802 under Napoleon’s Concordat with Rome, which restored a regulated form of Catholicism without returning its pre-revolutionary status. The interior was gradually refurnished over the 19th century.

The 1876 organ is the church’s outstanding surviving feature — one of the finest 19th-century instruments in the region, still in use for concerts and liturgy. From the quay outside, the church reads as a quiet aside to the bustle of the Vieux-Port, easy to overlook between the ferry terminals and the fish market stalls.
