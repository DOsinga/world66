Saint Genisius of Arles was no soldier or bishop but a court stenographer — a notary at the Roman court in Arles who, around 303 CE during the Diocletianic persecution, was handed an imperial edict ordering action against Christians. He refused to transcribe it, threw down his writing instruments, and fled the city. Captured at the Rhône, he was tortured and beheaded on the opposite bank. His surprising sainthood — won by a civil servant rather than a preacher or warrior — made him the patron of notaries, stenographers, and actors who perform from scripts.

The church that bears his name sits in the Saint-Giniez neighbourhood of Marseille's 8th arrondissement, a calm residential quarter south of the old city wedged between the Prado axis and the Parc Borély. The neighbourhood takes its name from the saint, evidence of how deeply these early Provençal martyrs sank into local geography. The church itself is a 19th-century parish building serving the streets around it.

Saint-Giniez is not a tourist destination but an honest slice of bourgeois Marseille — tree-lined streets, the hum of school pickup, a Sunday-market feel on Saturday mornings. The church is the neighbourhood's quiet centre of gravity. If you're already near the Parc Borély or the plages du Prado, it's a short detour to pay respects to a saint whose career began not in a monastery but at a writing desk.
