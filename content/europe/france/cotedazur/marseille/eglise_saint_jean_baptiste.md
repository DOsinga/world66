John the Baptist occupies a singular position in Christian theology: the last of the Hebrew prophets, the bridge between old and new testaments, the man who baptised Jesus in the Jordan. His life was austere — he lived in the wilderness, wore camel hair, ate locusts and wild honey — and his death was operatic. Herod Antipas imprisoned him for criticising the tetrarch's marriage to his brother's wife; at a banquet, Herod's stepdaughter Salome danced and was offered any wish she wanted, and at her mother Herodias's urging she asked for John's head on a plate.

The feast of Saint John the Baptist falls on June 24, exactly six months before Christmas — midsummer to midwinter. The date is deliberate. Early Christians planted the feast atop pre-existing solstice celebrations: bonfires, water rituals, and the marking of the sun's turning. In southern France those midsummer fires were still being lit within living memory in rural parishes, half folk custom, half liturgy. No other saint except Jesus himself is honoured with a feast marking his birth rather than his death.

This church in the south of Marseille carries the Baptist's name into a workaday urban neighbourhood. The building is a typical 19th-century Marseillais parish — stone-fronted, capable of absorbing a large congregation for the stations of the liturgical year. It is the kind of church that keeps its doors open on a Tuesday afternoon, where old women light candles and the sacristan knows everyone's grandmother's name.
