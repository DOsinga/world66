John the Baptist baptised Jesus in the Jordan, preached in the wilderness, wore camel hair, ate locusts — and was killed because Herod's stepdaughter danced well at a banquet and her mother wanted a head on a plate. That's the operatic version. The theological version is simpler: he's the last Hebrew prophet, the bridge between testaments, the one who came just before.

His feast falls on June 24, exactly six months before Christmas. That's not coincidence — early Christians planted it squarely on top of the midsummer solstice celebrations: bonfires, water rituals, the marking of the sun's turning. In rural Provence those midsummer fires were still being lit within living memory, half folk custom, half liturgy.

This church in the south of Marseille carries that name into a workaday urban neighbourhood. Stone-fronted, built for a large congregation, doors open on a Tuesday afternoon. The kind of church where the sacristan knows everyone's grandmother's name.
