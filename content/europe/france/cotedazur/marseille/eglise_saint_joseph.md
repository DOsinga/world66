Joseph, husband of Mary and foster father of Jesus, spent centuries as a minor figure in Christian devotion — present in nativity scenes, then largely offstage. It was the Counter-Reformation that began elevating him, and the 19th century that made him ubiquitous. In 1870, Pope Pius IX declared Joseph patron of the universal Church. Workers' movements, industrial upheaval, and political revolution were remaking Europe, and a working-class saint — a carpenter who provided for a holy family — was exactly what the Church wanted to promote.

Churches dedicated to Saint Joseph multiplied across France in the latter half of the 19th century, as parishes were built to serve expanding city neighbourhoods. This one, in the western fringe of Marseille's 7th and 8th arrondissement borderlands, is a product of that wave. The feast of Saint Joseph, March 19, falls in the liturgical season of Lent and is one of the few feast days that can interrupt its quiet with something close to celebration in Catholic households.

The neighbourhood around the church is residential and slightly removed from the tourist circuits that concentrate around the Vieux-Port and Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde. That suits it. This is the Marseille that goes to work, picks up children, and stops at the boulangerie on the way home — with Joseph the worker quietly presiding from the façade.
