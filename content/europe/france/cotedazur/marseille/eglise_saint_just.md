The likeliest patron here is Saint Justus of Lyon, who died around 390 CE. Bishop of Lyon for over a decade, Justus presided over his city's church until an incident at a synod left him racked with guilt: a convicted murderer had been released from prison on his word and immediately killed again. Justus resigned his see, journeyed to Egypt, and spent the rest of his life as a monk in the Theban desert. He never returned. Lyon eventually retrieved his relics, and he was declared a saint not for dying under persecution but for choosing a harder life than the one he was given.

The church sits in the northern reaches of Marseille, in a part of the city that remained semi-rural until the 20th century's expansion. The coordinates place it in the 13th or 14th arrondissement — neighbourhoods built up around older village cores, each with its own parish church that predated the surrounding urbanisation. Saint-Just gives its name to the neighbourhood, and the church is that area's oldest institutional anchor.

The story of a bishop who walked away from power has an appealing quietness to it. There are no beheadings, no dramatic imperial confrontations — just a man who decided he had failed and went to the desert to think about it for the rest of his life. That the parish bearing his name is itself somewhat off the tourist map, in a quietly working neighbourhood, feels almost appropriate.
