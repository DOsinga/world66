Built in the 12th century on the heights of Le Panier, possibly on the site of a Roman temple to Neptune, Saint-Laurent served Marseille's fishing community for centuries before the city grew around it. Its Romanesque walls — round arches, thick stone, small windows designed to hold heat in winter and exclude it in summer — belong to a Mediterranean variant of the style that looks more to Lombardy than to Burgundy. From the promontory beside the church, the entrance to the Vieux-Port is directly below.

In January 1943, German occupying forces gave residents forty-eight hours to evacuate roughly 1,500 buildings in Le Panier, then dynamited the medieval fabric of the neighbourhood, displacing 25,000 people. The official justification was that the warren of alleys sheltered resistance activity. Saint-Laurent survived only because it stood at the outer edge of the demolition zone — a matter of metres rather than policy. The contrast between its intact Romanesque walls and the postwar apartment blocks that replaced the medieval district immediately makes the scale of the destruction legible.

Walking up from the Vieux-Port, the church appears above the stairways that climb the Panier slope, its plain tower visible against the sky. It remains an active parish church and the oldest Romanesque building still standing in Marseille.
