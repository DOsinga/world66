Saint-Laurent went up in the 12th century on the heights of Le Panier, possibly on the site of a Roman temple to Neptune. Its Romanesque walls — round arches, thick stone, small windows — belong to a Mediterranean variant of the style that looks more to Lombardy than to Burgundy. From the promontory beside the church, the entrance to the Vieux-Port is directly below.

In January 1943, German forces gave residents 48 hours to evacuate around 1,500 buildings, then dynamited the medieval fabric of Le Panier, displacing 25,000 people. The official reason was that the warren of alleys sheltered resistance activity. Saint-Laurent survived only because it stood at the outer edge of the demolition zone — metres, not policy. Stand next to its Romanesque walls and look at the postwar apartment blocks that replaced the medieval neighbourhood: the scale of what was destroyed is immediately legible.

It remains the oldest Romanesque building still standing in Marseille. Still an active parish church. Walk up from the Vieux-Port and you'll see its plain tower before you reach it.
