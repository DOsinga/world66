Saint-Lazare stands on the site of a medieval hospital for lepers, founded when the disease was still endemic and the hospital necessarily stood outside the city walls — the sick were isolated from the healthy population by distance and wall alike. By the time the current 18th-century building was constructed, leprosy had long since disappeared from Provence; the patron saint and the name were retained as historical memory, the original social function no longer operative. The location, once peripheral, had been absorbed into the expanding city.

The Cours Belsunce, on which the church faces, became one of the main commercial arteries connecting the old port to the northern neighbourhoods, and from the 20th century onward developed into the centre of Marseille's North African trading economy. The stretch is lined with wholesale shops, money transfer agencies, and restaurants serving North African, sub-Saharan, and Middle Eastern food — a geography of migration that makes the old name and the old saint seem more distant still.

The church continues as an active parish, its congregation predominantly drawn from the surrounding immigrant community. It is not a tourist destination but an ordinary working church embedded in one of Marseille's most commercially alive streets — the kind of place the city presents in abundance once you leave the harbour and the old quarter behind.
