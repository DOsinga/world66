Louis IX of France (1214–1270) is a puzzling candidate for sainthood by modern lights: he persecuted heretics, burned the Talmud, and led two ruinous crusades. But the medieval Church saw a king who wore a hair shirt under his royal robes, washed the feet of lepers, dispensed justice under an oak tree to any subject who asked, and built the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris specifically to house the Crown of Thorns he had acquired at extraordinary expense. He was, in the understanding of his age, a monarch who took seriously the idea that kingship was a form of service. He was canonised in 1297, just 27 years after his death.

The connection to Marseille is direct and geographical. Louis IX embarked on the Seventh Crusade from Aigues-Mortes, the port he built in the Camargue, in 1248 — less than an hour from Marseille. He returned after disaster in Egypt, then left again in 1270 for the Eighth Crusade, once more from Aigues-Mortes, and died of dysentery outside Tunis before his army achieved anything. His body was brought back through the Mediterranean to be entombed at Saint-Denis. Marseille, as the great port of the south, was where his crusading legend was most felt.

The church sits in the northern reaches of Marseille — a part of the city shaped more by the port economy and the industries that grew up around it than by the bourgeois Haussmann-era development of the southern arrondissements. A royal saint in a working neighbourhood: it is a distinctly French kind of irony, and the church carries it without fuss.
