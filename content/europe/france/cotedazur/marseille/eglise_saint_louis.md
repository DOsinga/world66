Louis IX wore a hair shirt under his royal robes, washed the feet of lepers, and built the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house the Crown of Thorns. He also burned the Talmud, persecuted heretics, and led two catastrophic crusades. Medieval standards applied: he was canonised in 1297, 27 years after his death, as a king who understood kingship as service.

The connection to Marseille is direct. Louis embarked on the Seventh Crusade from Aigues-Mortes — less than an hour away — in 1248. He came back after disaster in Egypt. Then in 1270 he left again for the Eighth Crusade, from the same port, and died of dysentery outside Tunis before his army accomplished a thing. His body came back through the Mediterranean to Saint-Denis. Marseille, as the great port of the south, was where his crusading legend registered most.

The church sits in the northern reaches of the city, a neighbourhood shaped by the port economy and working industries rather than bourgeois development. A royal saint in a working neighbourhood: a distinctly French irony, carried without fuss.
