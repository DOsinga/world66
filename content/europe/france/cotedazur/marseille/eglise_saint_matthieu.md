Before becoming one of the twelve apostles, Matthew sat at a tax booth on the road out of Capernaum, extracting duties for Rome from passing fishermen and traders. His call — Jesus stopping, saying simply "Follow me," and Matthew standing up and leaving — is one of the most celebrated moments of rupture in Christian biography. Caravaggio painted it twice for a Roman chapel in the 1590s, and the scene has never stopped attracting artists drawn to its combination of the mundane and the miraculous.

The church's dedication honours a saint who became the patron of those who work with money: accountants, bankers, tax collectors, and customs officers. Matthew's Gospel, the first in the New Testament canon, is the most Jewish of the four, drawing heavily on Hebrew scripture and placing the Sermon on the Mount at its centre. Tradition holds he preached in Judea before travelling east; accounts of his death vary widely, and the location of his relics has been disputed for centuries between sites in Ethiopia, Persia, and Salerno.

This modest church in the northern quartiers of Marseille bears the saint's name in a city built on commerce and the movement of goods. The connection is fitting: Marseille has always been a place where traders, agents, and port officials shaped daily life, and where the line between honest dealing and creative accounting has sometimes blurred just as it did on the road to Capernaum.
