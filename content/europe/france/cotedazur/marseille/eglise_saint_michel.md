Michael appears in the Book of Daniel as the great protector of Israel, in the Letter of Jude wrestling with the devil over the body of Moses, and most vividly in the Book of Revelation as the commander of the heavenly army who casts the dragon out of heaven. He accumulated patronages across the centuries: soldiers, police, the dying, cemeteries, and the sick. His feast day on 29 September — Michaelmas — was one of the four quarter-days of the medieval European calendar, the date on which rents were paid, contracts settled, and the harvest year closed.

The dedication of churches to Michael is so common that it amounts almost to a default option in Christian naming; there are thousands of Saint Michael churches across Europe alone, from the hilltop Mont Saint-Michel in Normandy to the crypt chapels of early Irish monasteries. What distinguishes them is rarely the dedication and almost always the location. Marseille's Église Saint-Michel stands in a residential neighbourhood between the Vieux-Port and the heights of the city, in an area of apartment buildings, small shops, and the everyday texture of urban life that tourists rarely reach.

Coming here is less about the church itself than about the contrast it offers: the archangel of cosmic battle rendered in a neighbourhood scale, a stone façade among the stucco of daily life, reminding the passerby that the great territorial and spiritual dramas of Christian tradition were always also played out at the street level, in local stone, by local people who needed a patron.
