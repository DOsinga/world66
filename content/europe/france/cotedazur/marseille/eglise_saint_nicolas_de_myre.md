Nicholas of Myra was a real person: a bishop on what is now the Turkish coast, born around 270 CE. His reputation for generosity spread fast — stories of gold thrown through windows to provide dowries for poor girls, sick healed, sailors saved. He became patron of sailors, merchants, and children. His feast on 6 December was one of the most celebrated in medieval Europe.

In 1087, merchants from Bari sailed to Myra and stole his remains. Bari built a basilica to house them and became a major pilgrimage destination. Sailors kept his image on board and credited him with calming storms. For a city whose entire economy depended on sea trade and the lives of those who worked the water, the cult of Nicholas was never decorative.

The full dedication — Nicholas-de-Myre rather than just Saint-Nicolas — keeps him anchored in his historical context in Asia Minor. It distinguishes this bishop from the later folk figure who became Father Christmas, and maintains the link between this port neighbourhood and the eastern Mediterranean world it has always traded with.
