Nicholas of Myra was a real person: a bishop in the city now called Demre on the Turkish coast, probably born around 270 CE and dead by the mid-fourth century. The historical record is thin, but his reputation for generosity — the stories of bags of gold thrown through windows to provide dowries for poor girls — spread quickly through the eastern church. He became patron of sailors, merchants, children, and the falsely accused. By the medieval period his feast day on 6 December was one of the most widely celebrated in Europe, and his image, red-robed and often carrying three golden balls, was a fixture of church iconography.

In 1087, merchants from Bari in southern Italy — then competing hard with Venice for eastern trade routes — sailed to Myra and stole the saint's remains. The Basilica di San Nicola in Bari was built to house them, and the city became a major pilgrimage destination. Nicholas's maritime associations made him especially important in port cities: sailors prayed to him before voyages, kept his image aboard ship, and credited him with calming storms. In Marseille, whose entire economy for centuries depended on sea trade and the lives of those who worked the water, the cult of Nicholas was never merely decorative.

This church, a short walk from the area around the Vieux-Port, carries that tradition. The dedication to Nicholas-of-Myra rather than simply Saint-Nicolas preserves the geographical specificity — anchoring the saint in his historical context in Asia Minor, distinguishing him from the later folk figure who became Father Christmas, and maintaining the link between this Marseille neighbourhood and the ancient eastern Mediterranean world that the port has always connected.
