Philip the Apostle is one of the quieter figures in the New Testament. He appears at the feeding of the five thousand and asks Christ to show them the Father — the question that draws the famous answer, "He who has seen me has seen the Father." Modest contributions, but he's been giving his name to parishes across Catholic Europe for two thousand years.

The second dedication is louder. In 1858, a fourteen-year-old girl named Bernadette Soubirous reported eighteen apparitions of the Virgin in a grotto at Lourdes in the Hautes-Pyrénées. The apparition called herself "the Immaculate Conception" — backing the dogma Pius IX had defined just four years earlier. Within decades Lourdes became the largest Marian pilgrimage site in France, drawing millions to the spring that appeared from the grotto floor. The Church approved the apparitions in 1862; Bernadette was canonised in 1933.

Pilgrims heading south to Lourdes by train passed through Marseille. The shrine's imagery became part of everyday French Catholic life, and this parish carries both strands: the ancient apostolic one and the very modern apparition that changed French popular devotion almost overnight.
