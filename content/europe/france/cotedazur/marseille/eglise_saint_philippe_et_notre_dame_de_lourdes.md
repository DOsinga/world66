Saint Philip the Apostle is one of the more quietly present figures in the New Testament, appearing at the feeding of the five thousand and asking Christ to show the disciples the Father — the question that draws the famous answer, "He who has seen me has seen the Father." His feast day, now kept on 3 May in the Latin Church, has been observed since early Christianity, and parishes bearing his name are scattered across Catholic Europe as modest reminders of the apostolic foundation of the faith.

The second dedication, to Our Lady of Lourdes, carries an altogether different kind of weight. In 1858 a fourteen-year-old girl named Bernadette Soubirous reported eighteen apparitions of the Virgin in a grotto at Lourdes, in the Hautes-Pyrénées. The apparition identified herself in Occitan as "the Immaculate Conception," lending support to the dogma Pius IX had defined just four years earlier. Within decades Lourdes had become the largest Marian pilgrimage site in France — and one of the largest in the world — drawing millions of sick and healthy believers to the spring that emerged from the grotto floor. The Church approved the apparitions in 1862; Bernadette was canonised in 1933.

A Marseille parish joining these two dedications speaks to the layered devotional culture of a port city whose Catholic life was always shaped by the sea routes that connected it to Lourdes, Rome, and the wider Mediterranean world. Pilgrims heading south by train to Lourdes passed through Marseille; the shrine's imagery became part of the visual grammar of French popular Catholicism, and this church carries both strands of that inheritance.
