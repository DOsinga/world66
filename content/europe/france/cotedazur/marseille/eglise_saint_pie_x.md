Giuseppe Sarto was born in 1835 in a small village in the Veneto, the son of a postman, and rose through Italian diocesan ministry to become Patriarch of Venice before his election as Pope Pius X in 1903. He is remembered as a reforming pope of a distinctly pastoral cast: he reorganised canon law, promoted frequent communion at a time when many Catholics received it only rarely, and controversially lowered the age of First Communion to the age of reason — roughly seven — arguing that children should not be kept from the Eucharist by excessive caution. His reform of the breviary and his encouragement of Gregorian chant reshaped the liturgical life of parishes across the Catholic world.

Pius X is also associated with the condemnation of Modernism, the theological current he identified in the 1907 decree Pascendi Dominici Gregis, a move that cast a long shadow over Catholic intellectual life in the early twentieth century. He died in 1914, days after the outbreak of the First World War, reportedly broken by the failure of his peace efforts. He was beatified in 1951 and canonised in 1954 by Pius XII — at the time, the most recently canonised pope in the Church's history, a distinction he held until John XXIII and John Paul II were raised to the altar in 2014.

Any church bearing the dedication to Saint Pius X must date from 1954 at the earliest, making it one of the younger Catholic foundations in Marseille. That relative modernity is itself part of the story: the parish belongs to the post-war expansion of French Catholic infrastructure, built to serve a city that grew rapidly in the decades after 1945.
