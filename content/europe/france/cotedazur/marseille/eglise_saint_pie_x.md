Giuseppe Sarto was born in 1835 in a small Veneto village, son of a postman. He worked his way through Italian diocesan ministry to become Patriarch of Venice, then Pope Pius X in 1903. He's remembered as a reformer with a pastoral streak: he lowered the age of First Communion to seven, arguing children shouldn't be kept from the Eucharist by excessive caution. He reorganised canon law and reshaped parish life across the Catholic world by pushing Gregorian chant back into the Sunday liturgy.

He also condemned Modernism in the 1907 decree Pascendi — a move that cast a long shadow over Catholic intellectual life for decades. He died in August 1914, days after the outbreak of the First World War, reportedly broken by the failure of his peace efforts. Beatified in 1951, canonised in 1954 by Pius XII — at the time, the most recently canonised pope in the Church's history.

Any church dedicated to Pius X dates from 1954 at the earliest. This one belongs to the post-war expansion of French Catholic infrastructure, built to serve a Marseille that grew rapidly in the decades after 1945.
