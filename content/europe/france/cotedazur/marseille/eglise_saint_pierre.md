Saint Peter — Simon, son of Jonah, renamed by Christ "the rock on which I will build my church" — is the most widely venerated apostle in Western Christianity and the most commonly chosen patron for parish churches from Ireland to Istanbul. He appears in the gospels as the impulsive, devoted, fallible spokesman of the twelve: the one who walked on water and then sank, who denied Christ three times before the cock crowed, and who received the risen Lord on the shore of Galilee with a catch of fish. Roman tradition holds that Peter came to Rome, founded the see that bears his name, and was crucified upside down under Nero — his own chosen modification, out of humility before his Lord. The keys he holds in every statue and painting are the keys of heaven itself, the symbol of apostolic authority the papacy has claimed ever since.

In Marseille, a city where Christianity arrived early — tradition credits Mary Magdalene and Lazarus with bringing the faith from Palestine in the first century — dedication to Saint Peter carries particular weight. The city's own origins are interwoven with seafaring, and Peter, a fisherman before he was an apostle, is also patron of fishermen. This eastern neighbourhood parish carries his name in that double sense: the keeper of the celestial gate and the patron of those who cast nets into the sea.

The church serves a residential quartier in the eastern arrondissements, away from the tourist circuits of the Vieux-Port and the Canebière. Its role is essentially parochial — baptisms, weddings, Sunday mass — the ordinary rhythm of Catholic neighbourhood life. Saint Peter's particular emblem, the two crossed keys on a gold and silver ground, appears in countless forms in churches of his dedication across Europe, from the grandest basilicas in Rome to modest parish buildings like this one.
