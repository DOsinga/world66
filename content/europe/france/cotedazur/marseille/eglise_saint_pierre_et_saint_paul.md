Peter and Paul are the paired pillars of Western Christianity. One a Galilean fisherman, the other a Pharisee from Tarsus who once hunted Christians before his Damascus road conversion. Both believed to have been martyred in Rome around 64 CE during Nero's persecutions. Their joint feast on 29 June is one of the oldest in the Roman liturgical calendar, observed since at least the third century.

What's worth knowing: they didn't always agree. Paul rebuked Peter publicly at Antioch over table fellowship with gentiles — one of the sharpest recorded arguments between founding figures of any religion. The church holds them together anyway, as equals in martyrdom and equal pillars of the faith. A joint dedication is a statement about reconciliation as much as honour.

This northern Marseille quartier church follows a common French pattern — a working-class parish that needed the combined apostolic weight of both saints under one roof. In a city as heterogeneous as Marseille, the patron pairing still makes sense.
