Roch was born around 1295 in Montpellier, inherited his father's wealth, gave it away, and set out for Rome. Reaching northern Italy during a plague outbreak, he stopped to nurse the dying in hospitals along the way. He contracted plague himself near Piacenza, retreated to a forest to die, and was kept alive by a dog that brought him bread and licked his sores clean. He recovered. He's depicted in almost every painting with the dog and his robe lifted to show the bubo on his thigh.

In Marseille, this is not just a generic plague saint. In 1720 the last major plague outbreak in Western Europe struck the city: roughly 50,000 of Marseille's 90,000 inhabitants died within two years. It started when the cargo ship Grand Saint Antoine docked in the Vieux-Port with infected bales of cloth from the Levant. Devotion to Roch intensified across Provence as the epidemic spread — a man who had touched plague victims and survived was exactly the intercessor these people needed.

Churches of Saint Roch proliferated across France after the Black Death and again after 1720, often built by survivors making vows during the worst of it. Whether this building dates from such a vow is not documented. But the dedication speaks plainly: Marseille knows what it means to invoke a healer who survived against all odds, with a little help from a dog.
