Saint Theodore the Recruit — Theodore Tiro in Greek, from the Latin "tiro" meaning a new soldier — was a Roman legionary martyred around 306 CE during the Diocletianic persecution at Amasea in Pontus, in what is now northern Turkey. His legend holds that he refused to sacrifice to the Roman gods, confessed Christianity, and was burned alive; his body was said to have been recovered intact from the flames. He became one of the earliest and most widely venerated soldier martyrs of the Eastern church, and his cult spread rapidly to Constantinople, Rome, and eventually western Europe. Before the relics of Saint Mark were stolen from Alexandria and brought to Venice in 828, Theodore was the patron saint of the city — his column still stands in the Piazzetta San Marco, facing Saint Mark's lion across the water entrance to the republic.

Theodore occupies an unusual place in hagiography as an entirely military saint who predates the great period of crusader saint-making. He carries no attribute except armour and a lance; his story is purely one of refusing to worship false gods and accepting death for it. Churches dedicated to Theodore tend to be old, with roots in Byzantine or early Carolingian devotional culture, and are scattered across southern Europe wherever the Eastern church's influence travelled along trade routes and pilgrimage networks.

This church in the northwestern arrondissements of Marseille honours that tradition. The city's Greek founding myth — the Phocaean settlers who arrived around 600 BCE — and its centuries of eastern Mediterranean commerce gave Marseille cultural channels that carried soldier saints westward before the crusades made them fashionable everywhere. The dedication to Theodore is a reminder that the church's sanctoral calendar moved along shipping routes as much as overland pilgrim paths, and that this ancient port city's religious life has always had one eye on the eastern sea.
