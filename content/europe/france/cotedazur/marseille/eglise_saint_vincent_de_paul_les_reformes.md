Construction began in 1851 and was suspended repeatedly: funding collapsed, the Franco-Prussian War intervened, political disruptions delayed decisions for years at a time. The building was not completed until 1893. The two spires, each 70 metres tall, became the dominant vertical element at the upper end of the Canebière — Marseille's main boulevard — and are visible from the port and from most of the city's high ground. The long construction history left the building with the slightly assembled quality of a project completed in stages, though the result reads as unified from the exterior.

The choice of neo-Gothic was deliberate and contrarian. Marseille's religious architecture runs predominantly Baroque and neo-classical — from the 17th-century Cathédrale de la Major to Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde's Romano-Byzantine silhouette. Les Réformés stands apart from that tradition, looking more like a church from northern France or Belgium than anything else in the city. The interior is correspondingly cool and vertical: darker than most Marseille churches, and more austere, with stained glass that works better on grey days than in the relentless southern light.

The church marks the point where the Canebière transitions from the commercial lower section near the port to the residential streets climbing toward the 1st and 4th arrondissements. It is a working parish rather than a tourist monument, and the doors are open during services. The view down the Canebière from the steps — looking south toward the Vieux-Port basin — is one of the classic urban vistas of Marseille.
