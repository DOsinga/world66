Émilie de Vialar was born in 1797 near Toulouse into a comfortable bourgeois family. When her grandfather died and left her his fortune, she didn't invest it — she founded a religious congregation. In 1832 she established the Sisters of Saint Joseph of the Apparition and sent them to Bône (now Annaba) in French Algeria, where they set up schools and a dispensary among the Muslim and Jewish populations of the newly colonised territory. Within years her sisters were operating across North Africa, the Levant, and the Ottoman Empire.

Marseille's connection to this story is not incidental. The city was the primary port of embarkation for French Algeria throughout the colonial period. Troop transports, settlers, missionaries, nuns — everyone passed through this harbour. For Vialar's congregation, Marseille was the antechamber to the mission field: the last port before Africa.

She was canonised by Pius XII in 1951. The congregation she founded still works today in education and social services across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The church here is a monument to a woman whose ambitions repeatedly outran institutional resistance — including a protracted fight with the Archbishop of Algiers that forced her sisters out of Algeria entirely, before Rome eventually vindicated her.
