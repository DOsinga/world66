Émilie de Vialar was born in Gaillac, near Toulouse, in 1797 into a prosperous bourgeois family. After years of private charitable work, she used an inheritance from her grandfather to found, in 1832, the Sisters of Saint Joseph of the Apparition — a congregation named for the angel who appeared to Joseph in the Gospel of Matthew. Her initial mission was in Bône (now Annaba) in French Algeria, where the congregation established schools and a dispensary among the Muslim and Jewish populations of the newly colonised territory. Within a few years her sisters had spread across North Africa, the Levant, and the Ottoman Empire, working in hospitals, schools, and orphanages in conditions of considerable difficulty.

The Marseille connection is not incidental. The city was the primary port of embarkation for French Algeria throughout the colonial period: troop transports, settlers, administrators, missionaries, and nuns all passed through its harbour. For a congregation founded to work in Algeria, Marseille was less a metropolitan city than the antechamber to the mission field — the last port before Africa. The church dedicated to Vialar in Marseille thus stands at a historically resonant junction between the French metropole and the colonial world her order served. She was canonised by Pius XII in 1951, fifty years after the formal end of the remarkable expansion she had set in motion.

The congregation she founded continues to work today in education and social services across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The church in Marseille is a quiet monument to a woman whose ambitions for her order outran the institutional resistance she faced throughout her life, including a protracted conflict with the Archbishop of Algiers that forced the congregation out of Algeria entirely before eventually being vindicated by Rome.
