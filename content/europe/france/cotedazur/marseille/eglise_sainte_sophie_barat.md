Madeleine Sophie Barat was born in Joigny, in Burgundy, in 1779, and educated largely by her brother Louis, a priest whose rigorous intellectual formation gave her an unusual grounding in Latin, Greek, history, and theology. In 1800, in the aftermath of the Revolution's assault on French religious life, she co-founded the Society of the Sacred Heart — the Religieuses du Sacré-Cœur de Jésus — with the Jesuit Joseph Varin. The congregation's mission was the education of girls and young women at a moment when the Revolution had destroyed most of the institutional structures that had previously provided it.

The Sacred Heart schools became a significant presence in French Catholic life over the course of the nineteenth century, establishing houses across France and eventually across the world. They were associated above all with the formation of young women from Catholic elite families, though the congregation also ran free schools for the poor alongside its fee-paying institutions. Barat governed the congregation for sixty-three years, dying in 1865 shortly after the First Vatican Council had begun to reconfigure the Church she had served. She was beatified in 1908 and canonised in 1925 by Pius XI, with the American-born Philippine Duchesne — one of her earliest collaborators, who had taken the congregation to Missouri — canonised alongside her in 1988.

A Marseille parish bearing her name places the Society of the Sacred Heart in its Mediterranean context: the city was home to families whose daughters attended Sacred Heart schools and to a Catholic culture in which the congregation's educational work was a recognised institution. The church is a neighbourhood place of worship, but its dedication connects it to one of the more quietly influential chapters in the history of French Catholic women's religious life.
