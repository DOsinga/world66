Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) was a Spanish Carmelite nun who, together with her younger disciple John of the Cross, launched a sweeping reform of the Carmelite order. Distressed by what she saw as a drift toward comfort and laxity, she founded the first Discalced Carmelite convent in 1562 and spent the rest of her life establishing new houses across Spain despite fierce institutional resistance. Her nickname "Discalced" — literally "unshod" — referred to the reformers' practice of going barefoot or wearing sandals as a sign of poverty and humility.

Beyond her reforming energy, Teresa was a writer of extraordinary depth. Her autobiography, the *Book of Her Life*, and the *Interior Castle* — an allegory of the soul's journey toward union with God through seven concentric dwelling places — are milestones of mystical literature and remain in print today. In 1970, Pope Paul VI declared her a Doctor of the Church, making her the first woman to receive that honour alongside Catherine of Siena.

This Marseille church bearing her name carries that tradition into a Mediterranean port city that has always blended Latin spirituality with maritime restlessness. Carmelite foundations spread through France in the seventeenth century partly on the impetus of Teresa's reform, and her feast day on 15 October still draws parishioners to churches like this one across the francophone world.
