Thérèse Martin (1873–1897) entered the Carmelite convent at Lisieux in Normandy at the age of fifteen and died of tuberculosis at twenty-four, never having left the cloister or performed any dramatic public act. Yet the memoir she wrote under obedience, *Story of a Soul*, became one of the most widely read spiritual texts of the twentieth century. She called her approach the "Little Way" — a path of radical trust and childlike confidence in God's mercy, suited to ordinary people rather than heroic ascetics. The appeal was immediate: she was canonised in 1925, just twenty-eight years after her death, at a ceremony in Saint Peter's Square attended by half a million people.

The timing of her canonisation explains why so many French churches of the inter-war years bear her name. Parishes built or rededicated in the 1920s and 1930s often chose Thérèse as patron, her image — roses, a crucifix, the brown Carmelite habit — appearing on the new stained glass of countless suburban and working-class neighbourhoods. Pope John Paul II declared her a Doctor of the Church in 1997, capping a century of popular devotion with theological recognition of her spiritual teaching.

This church in Marseille fits that pattern, serving a residential quarter where her name on the facade signals both a moment of local ecclesiastical history and the enduring pull of a saint who promised that smallness was no obstacle to holiness.  Her feast day falls on 1 October, celebrated each year as the opening of the church's main patronal festivities.
