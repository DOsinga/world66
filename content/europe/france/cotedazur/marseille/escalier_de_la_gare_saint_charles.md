The staircase was not part of the original 1848 station design. The Gare Saint-Charles sits on a plateau roughly 25 metres above the city streets, and for its first seven decades the station turned a dead face toward the city below. The 1925 staircase was commissioned to give Marseille's main terminal — and France's principal Mediterranean gateway — an entrance commensurate with the city's importance. The descent of 104 steps is specifically theatrical: as you walk down, the city unfolds ahead of you in stages, the effect calculated to make arrival feel like a declaration.

Louis Botinelly's four bronze sculptural groups flanking the staircase represent Africa and Asia — the two continents reached through Marseille's colonial port. The figures draw on Orientalist conventions of French public sculpture at their height: powerful, idealised, coded with the visual grammar of empire. They stated, plainly, what the port was for. In the 1920s, more colonial traffic passed through Marseille than through any other French city — soldiers, administrators, merchants, and migrants moving between France and North Africa, West Africa, Madagascar, and Indochina.

Today the staircase is used by commuters and tourists arriving at the station, most of whom pass the sculptures without stopping. The allegorical programme — Africa and Asia as ornamental approaches to a European city — is a document of French colonial attitudes at their most confident. The architecture itself remains effective: the masonry, the width, the way the city expands as you descend are all precisely calculated to make the traveller feel they have arrived somewhere significant. The calculation still works.
