The station was built in 1848 and sat on its plateau for seventy-seven years turning a blank face to the city below. The staircase came in 1925, commissioned specifically to give France's main Mediterranean gateway an entrance that matched the ambition. One hundred and four steps, wide enough for a procession, calculated so the city expands in stages as you descend.

Louis Botinelly's bronze sculptures flanking the descent represent Africa and Asia — the two continents reached through Marseille's colonial port. They're built in the Orientalist grammar of 1920s French public sculpture: idealised, powerful, and entirely frank about what the port was for. More colonial traffic moved through Marseille in the 1920s than through any other French city.

Most commuters walk past them without a glance. The allegorical programme is exactly what it looks like — empire decorating its front door. But the architecture still works: the masonry, the width, the staged reveal of the city below. Arrive by train, take the stairs down, and you'll feel the calculation land.
