Marseille is a city of stairs. The terrain demanded it: the limestone hills that buckle up from the coast forced the planners of every era to negotiate gradient rather than ignore it, and the result is a city laced with stairways that are, in their way, as characteristic as the Canebière or the Vieux-Port. The Escaliers de la Plaine Granger sit in the northern districts, well away from the tourist circuits, in the working-class city that most visitors never see. The coordinates place them in a quarter built up across the twentieth century as the city expanded to house its growing population — densely inhabited, locally rooted, and largely indifferent to its lack of guidebook coverage.

The staircase as an urban form has a particular meaning in Marseille. The most famous examples — the steps of the Gare Saint-Charles, the monumental terracing of the Longchamp Palace — were built as civic theatre, designed to be descended in one's best clothes. The everyday stairs of the residential quartiers are the democratic inverse: they exist to get people and their shopping up and down the hill, and they do so without ceremony. Plaine Granger's steps almost certainly connect a street on one level to a street on another, crossing a slope that a flat city would solve with a ramp or a road.

Walk them in the morning when the light comes from the east and the northern city spreads below. The view from the top of any such staircase is a reminder that Marseille is a genuinely vertical place, and that the best panoramas are often found not at the designated viewpoints but on the way between two unremarkable addresses.
