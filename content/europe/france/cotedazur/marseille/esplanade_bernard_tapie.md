Bernard Tapie (1943–2021) was one of the most improbable figures in modern French public life: a self-made entrepreneur from a working-class Paris suburb who became a pop culture celebrity, a Mitterrand-era government minister, a convicted fraudster, and the man who turned Olympique de Marseille into a European power. He bought the club in 1986 when it was languishing, poured money into it, and over eight years delivered five consecutive French league titles and, in May 1993, the club's only UEFA Champions League. OM defeated AC Milan 1–0 in Munich — Basile Boli's header the decisive blow — becoming the first and still the only French club to win the European Cup. In Marseille, that night remains the emotional summit of living memory.

The fall came quickly. Just weeks before that Munich final, OM fixed a French league match against Valenciennes, paying players to throw the game to protect the squad for Europe. When the affair came to light — the VA-OM scandal — the repercussions were severe: the club was stripped of the 1993 French championship, barred from European competition, and eventually relegated. The Champions League title, however, was not taken away, a distinction Marseillais note carefully. Tapie himself faced criminal proceedings and served time in prison, though he spent subsequent decades in legal battles that eventually secured him substantial financial compensation from Crédit Lyonnais in a parallel dispute.

Naming an esplanade after Tapie, who died of stomach cancer in October 2021, was a statement about Marseille's particular relationship with its own mythology. The city mourned him genuinely, understanding that the scandal was part of the man, not a reason to erase him. Whether this is admirable loyalty or convenient amnesia depends on your vantage point.
