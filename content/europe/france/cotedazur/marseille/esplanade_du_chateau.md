Château-Gombert was once its own village. Marseille absorbed it, but the quarter held its character: low-rise, unhurried, with the feel of a Provençal bourg rather than a city neighbourhood. The esplanade sits on the flat plateau where the city's northern hills ease off — wide sky, slower pace, a place people still know their neighbours by name.

The Musée des Arts et Traditions Populaires du Terroir Marseillais is here — a small museum dedicated to the material life of the Marseille hinterland: tools, costumes, furniture, the physical evidence of how people in these hills actually lived before the industrial era. The esplanade is its natural extension, the gathering point for markets and evening boules.

Getting up here from the centre takes a deliberate bus ride through the northern arrondissements. The reward is a Marseille that feels miles from the Vieux-Port. The air is cleaner, the pace is slower, and the views from any high point remind you just how large and hilly this city actually is.
