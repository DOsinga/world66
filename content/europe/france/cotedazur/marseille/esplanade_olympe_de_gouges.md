In 1791, Marie Gouze — writing as Olympe de Gouges — published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman as a direct reply to the Revolutionary government's Declaration of the Rights of Man. Her argument was legally simple: if the principles applied to men because they were citizens, they must apply equally to women. Article 1: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights." Two years later she was arrested, charged with sedition, and guillotined. The feminist argument was part of the case against her.

This esplanade at the entrance to the Joliette ferry terminal was named in her honour during the 2010s renovation — the result of sustained feminist pressure on Marseille's city government. Across France, fewer than 5 percent of street names commemorate women.

The location carries its own edge. Right here, ferries depart to North Africa and Corsica — one of the largest regular migration routes into France. The esplanade named after a woman executed for arguing that rights are not gendered sits at the threshold where people arrive seeking rights.
