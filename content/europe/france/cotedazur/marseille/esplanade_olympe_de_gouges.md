Marie Gouze, writing under the name Olympe de Gouges, produced her Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen in 1791, immediately following the National Assembly's passage of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789. Her argument was legally simple: if the Revolution's founding principles applied to men because they were citizens, those principles must apply equally to women who were also citizens. Article 1 of her declaration reads: "Woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights." The Revolutionary government found this position intolerable. She was arrested in 1793 for her political writings and guillotined in November of that year. The charge was sedition; her feminist argument was part of the case against her.

The esplanade near the Joliette ferry terminal was named in her honour during the 2010s renovation of the Euroméditerranée district — a conscious choice that came from sustained feminist pressure on Marseille's municipal government to address the historic under-representation of women in French street naming. Marseille has been relatively more willing than most French cities to name streets after women, but the baseline across France is low: surveys consistently show that fewer than 5 percent of French street names commemorate women.

The location has its own significance. The esplanade sits at the entrance to the Joliette ferry terminal, where services depart to North Africa and Corsica — a threshold point for one of the largest regular migration routes into France. Naming it after a woman executed for arguing that rights cannot be gendered is either ironic or apt, depending on how you read the current political moment.
