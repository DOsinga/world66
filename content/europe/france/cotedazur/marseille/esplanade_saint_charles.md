The Gare Saint-Charles was already one of France's first major railway terminals when the Paris line reached it in 1848. But it didn't become a stage until 1925, when engineers added the monumental external staircase: 104 steps descending from the concourse to the city, flanked by allegorical sculptures of Asia and Africa — the destinations Marseille's imperial trade served.

Step out of the train hall and Marseille opens below you. The Boulevard d'Athènes drops toward Belsunce, the rooflines of the northern districts, eventually the sea. It's one of the few railway stations in France where the arrival itself is designed as a spectacle.

The esplanade at the bottom is a taxi rank, a tram stop, a bus interchange — and simultaneously the place where first impressions of the city crystallise. The neighbourhood immediately around it, Belsunce, is one of the most commercially dense and ethnically mixed in Marseille. This is where the general idea of the city meets the specific, loud, particular reality of it.
