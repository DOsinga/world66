The Gare Saint-Charles was one of the first major railway terminals in France, reached by the line from Paris in 1848. The esplanade in front of it was a functional space from the beginning: a turning point for coaches, then cabs, now taxis and trams. The transformation from utilitarian forecourt to civic statement came in 1925, when the monumental external staircase was added, descending 104 steps from the station concourse level to the city street below and flanked by allegorical sculptures representing Asia and Africa — the destinations that Marseille's imperial port trade served.

The staircase makes a theatrical entrance. Arriving by train, you emerge from the concourse and see Marseille laid out below you — the Boulevard d'Athènes descending toward Belsunce, the rooflines of the northern districts, eventually the sea. It is one of the few railway approaches in France where the station itself performs the arrival rather than hiding it. The esplanade at the base is simultaneously a taxi rank, a bus interchange, a tram stop, and a place where people wait, smoke, argue about directions, and take photographs of the steps they just descended.

The quarter around the station — Belsunce and the streets running north — is one of Marseille's most commercially dense and ethnically mixed. The esplanade is the threshold between the arriving city and the city proper: the point at which the generalised expectation of Marseille meets the specific, loud, and particular reality of it.
