Shipbuilding makes vessels. Ship repair keeps them alive. Marseille's northern port has been doing the second job since the nineteenth century, and the drydocks here can handle very large vessels — oil tankers, container ships, cruise liners. Time out of water costs operators money by the hour, so the work is fast and precise: hull surveys, plate replacements, engine overhauls, anti-fouling coatings applied before the ship goes back to sea.

This quayside exhibition makes that world visible. Panels and models explain the drydocking process: how a ship is guided in, how the water is pumped out, what engineers look for before the vessel is cleared to sail. Stand at the dock edge and the scale hits you — the walls rise several storeys; the keel blocks that hold a ship upright out of water distribute hundreds of tonnes across a handful of contact points.

The broader point is that Marseille's northern docks are still working. While other European port cities converted their waterfronts into leisure zones, this one stayed industrial. The global merchant fleet needs constant maintenance, and some of it gets done here.
