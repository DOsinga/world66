Marseille has been one of Europe's principal ship repair ports since the nineteenth century, when the Joliette basin gave the city permanent deep-water access to the Mediterranean. Today the northern port's drydocks can accommodate very large vessels — oil tankers, container ships, and cruise liners — and the shipyards employ skilled trades that range from structural welding and hull-plate replacement to engine overhaul and the application of anti-fouling coatings. Unlike shipbuilding, which creates a vessel from scratch, ship repair is a precision maintenance industry: time in dry dock costs operators money by the hour, so the turnaround must be fast and the diagnosis exact.

This exhibition, sited on the quayside where the work actually takes place, makes that specialist world visible to visitors. Panels and models explain the drydocking process — how a ship is guided into the dock, how water is pumped out to expose the hull, and what surveyors and engineers look for before authorising the vessel to return to sea. The industrial scale is striking: the dock walls rise several storeys, and the keel blocks that support a vessel out of water are engineered to distribute hundreds of tonnes across a handful of contact points.

The broader context is Marseille's continued relevance as a working port at a time when many European cities have converted their waterfronts to leisure uses. The ship repair yards are a reminder that the northern docks remain an active industrial zone, tied to global shipping schedules and the constant need to keep the world's merchant fleet in service.
