The Wallace fountain type was born from catastrophe. After the Prussian siege of 1870–71 and the Commune that followed, Paris had lost much of its potable water infrastructure and the poor were reduced to buying water at ruinous prices. Sir Richard Wallace — Anglo-Irish heir to the Marquess of Hertford's fortune, then living in Paris — responded by commissioning fifty ornate cast-iron drinking fountains and donating them to the city in 1872. The designer was the sculptor Charles-Auguste Lebourg, who devised the now-iconic form: four caryatids (representing Kindness, Simplicity, Charity, and Sobriety) supporting a domed canopy above a continuous-flow basin of cold, clean water, free to anyone.

The design was reproduced and distributed beyond Paris across the following decades. Marseille received examples of the fountain, which arrived as acts of civic hygiene as much as philanthropy — the public health argument for free drinking water was by then impossible to ignore. The Marseille fountain preserves the original Lebourg proportions and green paintwork, the colour adopted as standard for Wallace fountains so they would be recognisable as a public amenity without signage.

Wallace himself is buried in Père Lachaise; he never lived in Marseille, and his connection to the city is entirely via the object. That is part of what makes the Wallace fountain interesting: it is a type, not a unique monument — a piece of nineteenth-century social infrastructure that became so associated with its benefactor that his name outlasted his biography.
