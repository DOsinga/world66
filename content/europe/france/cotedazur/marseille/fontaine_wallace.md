After the Prussian siege of 1870–71 and the Commune that followed, Paris had destroyed water infrastructure and the poor were buying drinking water at ruinous prices. Sir Richard Wallace — Anglo-Irish heir to a vast French fortune — responded by commissioning fifty ornate cast-iron fountains and donating them to the city in 1872. The sculptor Charles-Auguste Lebourg designed the form: four caryatids (Kindness, Simplicity, Charity, Sobriety) holding a domed canopy over a basin of cold, clean, continuously flowing water. Free to anyone.

The design spread beyond Paris across the following decades. Marseille received its example as an act of civic hygiene as much as charity — by then the public health argument for free drinking water was unanswerable. The standard green paint was chosen so the fountains would be immediately recognisable as public amenities, without signage.

Wallace himself is buried in Père Lachaise and never lived in Marseille. His connection to the city is entirely via the object. That's part of what makes it interesting: a piece of Victorian social infrastructure that became so associated with its donor that his name outlasted his biography.
