The Knights of Saint John established a tower at the northern entrance to the Vieux-Port in the 12th century, controlling the harbour approaches and running their logistics for Crusade departures. Marseille was the primary embarkation point for French crusaders crossing to the Holy Land; the Hospitallers held facilities here for centuries before the present fortifications took shape. The current structure dates mainly from the 17th century, rebuilt under Louis XIV as part of his systematic militarisation of the French coastline.

Fort Saint-Jean served subsequently as military barracks, a military prison, and a transit camp for French colonial troops in the 19th and early 20th centuries. After World War II it fell into institutional disuse. Its transformation came with the MuCEM project: when the Musée des Civilisations de l'Europe et de la Méditerranée opened in 2013, Fort Saint-Jean was integrated into the complex and its interior ramparts, towers, and gardens were opened to the public as part of the museum visit.

A narrow footbridge connects the fort to Rudy Ricciotti's MuCEM building — the concrete-latticed cube that sits partly over the water, the most photographed structure in contemporary Marseille. The fort's rooftop terrace gives one of the better unobstructed views of the Vieux-Port entrance, the J4 esplanade, and the Cathédrale de la Major. Access to the fort is included in the MuCEM ticket; on certain days the fort's gardens and ramparts can be entered without purchasing the museum exhibition ticket.
