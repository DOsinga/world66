The Chevalier de Clerville, the military engineer who designed the fort, reportedly received explicit instructions that the artillery should be capable of destroying Marseille's own streets if necessary. The companion installation across the port entrance — Fort Saint-Jean — faces outward toward the sea, covering the conventional defensive purpose. Together they created a controlled choke point at the Vieux-Port entrance, but the primary strategic logic was always about coercion from within rather than defence from without.

The 1659 revolt against the royal governor had been serious enough that Louis XIV entered Marseille the following year under military escort, dismantled the city's traditional liberties, and ordered both forts constructed simultaneously. The message was architectural and deliberate: the most prominent military structure at the entrance to the city faced inward. Marseillais of the period can only have understood the orientation.

Fort Saint-Nicolas now houses the Direction Régionale des Affaires Culturelles (DRAC) and is not routinely open to public visits, though the esplanade and exterior are freely accessible. Its position at the southern tip of the Vieux-Port gives it one of the finest views in Marseille — across the harbour mouth to Fort Saint-Jean, and north toward Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde on its hill above the city.
