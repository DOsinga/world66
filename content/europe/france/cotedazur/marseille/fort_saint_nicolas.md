In 1659 Marseille revolted against royal authority. The following year Louis XIV arrived under military escort, stripped the city of its traditional liberties, and ordered two forts built simultaneously at the Vieux-Port entrance. Fort Saint-Jean faces the sea. Fort Saint-Nicolas faces the city. The engineer, the Chevalier de Clerville, reportedly received explicit instructions: the artillery must be capable of destroying Marseille's own streets if necessary.

That is not a construction error. That is a message. The most prominent military structure at the city's entrance pointed inward, and every Marseillais of the period understood what it meant.

The fort now houses the Direction Régionale des Affaires Culturelles and isn't routinely open to visitors, but the exterior esplanade is free to walk. It has one of the best positions in Marseille — harbour mouth on one side, Fort Saint-Jean across the water, Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde on its hill above.
