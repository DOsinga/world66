Before Portland cement, if you wanted to build something that touched the sea, you needed hydraulic lime. Unlike ordinary quicklime, it sets and hardens even when submerged. Marseille's great port expansion of the 1840s and 1850s consumed enormous quantities of it — dock walls, breakwaters, harbour basins, warehouse foundations built on reclaimed ground.

*Four de l'Aiga* is Provençal for "water oven" — a kiln built to produce the stuff. Workers loaded alternating layers of limestone and fuel, burnt it for days at sustained high temperature, then slaked and screened the clinker. Dusty, hot, continuous work. The hills east of Marseille had both the limestone and the water needed.

The site today reads as heritage rather than industry. But the name preserves what it was: not an oven that consumed water, but one whose product was defined by its relationship to it.
