In Provençal, *aiga* means water, and a *four de l'aiga* — a water oven — was a kiln used to calcine hydraulic lime: a form of limestone that, unlike ordinary quicklime, sets and hardens even when submerged. Before Portland cement became widely available in the late nineteenth century, hydraulic lime was the essential binder for any construction that met the sea — dock walls, breakwaters, harbour basins, and the foundations of warehouses built on reclaimed ground. Marseille's great port expansion of the 1840s and 1850s consumed enormous quantities of it.

The installation at these coordinates sits in the eastern industrial fringe of the city, in a landscape shaped by quarrying, lime burning, and the small-scale manufacturing that once supplied the port trades. Hydraulic lime kilns were typically built near limestone outcrops and water sources, and the hills east of Marseille provided both. Workers loaded the kiln with alternating layers of limestone and fuel, burnt it for several days at sustained high temperature, and then slaked and screened the resulting clinker. The process was dusty, hot, and continuous.

Today the site reads as heritage rather than industry, a surviving trace of the supply chain that underpinned Marseille's nineteenth-century transformation into a major Mediterranean port. The Provençal name preserves the memory of what the kiln produced — not a fire that consumed water, but a fire whose product was defined by its relationship to it.
