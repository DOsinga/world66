Frédéric Mistral (1830–1914) was born in the Bouches-du-Rhône near Maillane, and his presence in Marseille is by monument rather than by residence — but it is a significant monument in a significant city. Mistral was the leader of the Félibrige, the movement he co-founded in 1854 to revive Provençal as a literary language, to document its vocabulary before it disappeared, and to assert the cultural distinctiveness of the Midi against the centralising pressure of Parisian French. His epic poem Mirèio (1859), published in both Provençal and French, brought him European fame and opened a correspondence with Lamartine and later Alphonse Daudet.

In 1904 Mistral was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, sharing it with the Spanish dramatist José Echegaray. He used the prize money to found the Museon Arlaten in Arles, an ethnographic museum of Provençal life. The gesture was characteristic: he put the money back into the culture that had made the work possible. He died in 1914 at eighty-three, having spent sixty years making the case that Provence was not merely a region of France but a civilisation with its own literature, its own cuisine, its own calendar of saints and festivals.

Marseille's monument to Mistral is an act of civic appropriation by the metropolis of a poet who actually preferred smaller towns — Maillane, Arles, Saint-Rémy — but whose fame necessarily radiated into the region's dominant city. The Félibrige's journal was published in Marseille, and the city's role as Provence's commercial capital made it the natural home for the movement's institutional life, even if its heart beat in the villages.
