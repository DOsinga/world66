Marseille has one of the largest communities of Spanish, Portuguese, and Catalan origin of any French city, a legacy of migration waves that peaked in the early and mid twentieth century. Workers from Almería, Valencia, and the Portuguese Alentejo came to fill jobs in the docks, the soap factories, and the construction trades; their descendants remained, and the cultural traces of that migration persist in surnames, food markets, and the occasional shop or gallery name that slips into Iberian languages. *Dos Mares* — of the seas — is precisely that kind of trace: a name that looks south and west across the Mediterranean rather than north toward Paris.

The gallery occupies a position in the dense commercial fabric north of the Vieux-Port, in a neighbourhood where the city's successive waves of immigration have layered on top of one another. Art galleries in this part of Marseille tend to work with local artists and to reflect the city's identity as a port and a crossroads: the sea is never far from the imagery, and the cultural references run from North Africa to the Iberian Peninsula to the Levant. Dos Mares fits that disposition, its name alone positioning it within the Mediterranean rather than within any single national tradition.

Whether the programme at any given moment is painting, photography, or mixed media, the name functions as a statement of orientation — facing the sea that connects cultures rather than the hinterland that divides them. In Marseille, that is less a curatorial gesture than a plain geographic fact.
