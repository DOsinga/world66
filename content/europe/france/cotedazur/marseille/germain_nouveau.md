Germain Nouveau arrived in Paris in the 1870s and fell in with the circle around Verlaine. Then came the friendship that defined him: in 1872 and 1873 he and Arthur Rimbaud lived together in Brussels and London, writing and pushing at what French verse could do. Rimbaud quit poetry altogether afterward. Nouveau kept going.

His life grew stranger as he aged. A spiritual crisis in the 1890s transformed him completely. He renounced his literary identity, took the name Humilis, and became a wandering penitent. He spent years on the steps of Marseille's churches — most notably La Major — begging for alms from a city that had no idea he was a poet. He died in 1920, largely forgotten.

Paul Claudel and others recovered his reputation after his death. He's buried in Marseille. The monument marks both the man who shared Rimbaud's most intense years and the hermit who chose voluntary poverty on these same streets.
