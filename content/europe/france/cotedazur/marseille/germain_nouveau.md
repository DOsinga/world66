Germain Nouveau was born in 1851 at Pourrières, near Aix-en-Provence, and his trajectory through French literary life was one of the strangest of his era. He arrived in Paris in the early 1870s and fell in with the circle around Paul Verlaine, becoming one of the peripheral figures of the symbolist movement. But it was his friendship with Arthur Rimbaud that defined him: in 1872 and 1873 the two lived together in Brussels and London, writing, arguing, and pushing at the edges of what French verse could do. Rimbaud's subsequent abandonment of poetry had no equivalent in Nouveau, who continued to write — increasingly religious verse, saturated with mysticism — for decades afterward.

His life grew stranger as he aged. In the 1890s he underwent a spiritual crisis that transformed him entirely. He renounced his literary identity, took the name Humilis, and began to live as a penitent, wandering and begging. He spent long periods on the steps of Marseille's churches — most notably the steps of the Cathédrale de la Major — asking for alms in a city that did not know he was a poet. He died in Pourrières in 1920, largely forgotten by the literary world.

His reputation recovered slowly after his death, helped by Paul Claudel and others who recognised the quality of his religious poems. He is buried in Marseille, and this monument commemorates both the poet who shared Rimbaud's most intense creative years and the hermit-beggar who spent his last decades in voluntary poverty on these streets.
