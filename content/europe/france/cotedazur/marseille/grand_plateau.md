Marseille is built on a limestone massif cut by narrow valleys and punctuated by flat-topped rises — plateaux — that local toponymy has always distinguished from the hills between them. The Grand Plateau sits in the northern residential arc of the city, above the Vieux-Port basin and between the tight grid of the Cinq-Avenues district and the broader avenues of the Belle-de-Mai quarter. Its elevation puts it above the urban noise without lifting it into the tourist topography of Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde or the Corniche.

The plateau functions primarily as neighbourhood open space — somewhere to walk, to sit, and to look across the city toward the sea. On clear days the view southward takes in the islands of the Frioul archipelago and the Château d'If, and on the clearest days the horizon extends to the limestone ridges of the Calanques. This is the kind of prospect Marseillais take for granted and visitors discover with some surprise: the city's terrain is relentlessly three-dimensional, and wherever you find height you find a different city below you.

The surrounding streets are working-class residential Marseille, with the bakeries, halal butchers, Maghrebi grocery stores, and small cafés that characterise the city's northern arrondissements. The plateau itself is a modest amenity, but in a city where liveable outdoor space can be scarce in the dense inner quarters, a flat open terrace with a view is not nothing.
