Henri Cosquer discovered the cave in 1985 believing it to be empty; returning in 1991 he found walls covered in art. The paintings divide into two periods: around 27,000 BCE — horses, bison, ibex — and a later phase around 19,000 BCE featuring auks, great auks, and seals. That second layer is the most complete prehistoric representation of marine life found anywhere in Europe.

The reason the cave is submerged is straightforward: when the older paintings were made, sea levels were approximately 120 metres lower than today. As glaciers retreated, rising water sealed the entrance around 10,000 BCE, and the cave was completely isolated for roughly 10,000 years. Modern divers reached it through a 175-metre underwater passage; some caused damage after Cosquer's discovery went public. The replica was built in part to remove the incentive to attempt the dive.

The Villa Méditerranée building, designed by Stefano Boeri and opened in 2013, cantilevers over a reflecting pool — a deliberate gesture toward the submerged original. The replica reproduces the cave's geometry and paintings with high precision; standing inside gives a reasonable sense of the actual space, even if not the conditions of descending 37 metres into the sea.
