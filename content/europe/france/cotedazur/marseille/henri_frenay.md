Henri Frenay founded Combat in 1941 — one of the most significant Resistance networks in southern France — while technically still a French army officer. That took nerve. He hadn't decided Vichy was wrong immediately; the break came gradually, then absolutely. He built the network in Lyon and it spread fast through the unoccupied zone. Marseille was a natural hub: refugees, exiles, clandestine operators, a port with a long tradition of not asking too many questions.

Combat ran an underground newspaper, escape lines, and a paramilitary wing. By 1943 it merged with other southern movements to form a unified force, and Frenay sat at the head of the military committee. He was a difficult collaborator — suspicious of Communist influence, resistant to being subordinated to London — but he made the organisation work.

After the liberation he served as the minister handling prisoners, deportees, and refugees. Every day he confronted, by the numbers, what the regime he'd fought had actually done. He later became a committed European federalist. This monument stands near the northern districts his networks moved through.
