Henri Frenay (1905–1988) was a professional soldier who became one of the defining figures of the French Resistance. Disillusioned by the Vichy armistice but initially uncertain about Pétain, Frenay broke decisively with the collaborationist regime early and began building an underground network while still a serving officer. In 1941 he founded Combat in Lyon, which rapidly spread through the Southern Zone. Marseille — then in the nominally unoccupied territory and a city of refugees, exiles, and clandestine networks — became a crucial node for the movement. Its port, its cosmopolitan population, and its tradition of operating outside official channels made it natural Resistance terrain.

Combat published a clandestine newspaper of the same name, ran escape lines, and built a paramilitary wing. By 1943, under pressure from Charles de Gaulle's emissary Jean Moulin, Combat merged with Franc-Tireur and Libération-Sud to form the Mouvements Unis de la Résistance (MUR), with Frenay at the head of the military committee. Frenay was a difficult partner — abrasive, suspicious of Communist influence, and resistant to being subordinated to London — but his organisational energy was central to resistance in the south. After the November 1942 German invasion of the unoccupied zone, Marseille's brief status as a relative sanctuary ended, and the networks were forced deeper underground.

After liberation Frenay served in de Gaulle's provisional government as Minister for Prisoners, Deportees and Refugees — a role that confronted him daily with the scale of what the regime he had fought had done. He later became a committed Europeanist, believing that continental unity was the only lasting answer to the nationalism that had produced two world wars. This monument stands near the northern districts that Resistance networks moved through, a reminder that Marseille's apparent disorder was, in the dark years, a kind of cover.
