The image of a human figure surrounded by or offering a perch to birds is one of the oldest in public sculpture — from Egyptian falconers to Renaissance bronzes and the great figurative works of the 20th century. *Homme aux Oiseaux*, "Man with Birds," belongs to this lineage while reading as distinctly of its time: the posture and treatment of form suggest the mid-century European humanist tradition, in which the relationship between people and the natural world became a recurring preoccupation after the upheavals of two world wars.

Birds carry particular resonance in Marseille, a city whose air is crossed by migrations between Africa, Europe and the Middle East. The hills and calanques around the city are recognised stopover points for species moving between continents, and the old port has always been a place where those who travel by instinct and those who travel by sail arrive at the same shore. A man holding out his arms to birds in this setting reads as a figure of welcome and kinship rather than dominion.

The sculpture sits in its neighbourhood as an unhurried presence. Unlike more theatrical public monuments, it does not demand attention but rewards those who pause beside it — particularly in the early morning, when the light off the Mediterranean is low and the actual birds of Marseille are most audible in the trees around it.
