The Hôpital Caroline was built on the Île de Ratonneau — one of the four Frioul islands visible from the Vieux-Port on any clear day — and opened in 1828, purpose-built to handle the quarantine demands of Marseille's booming port trade. At peak capacity it could hold over a thousand people. Ships arriving from North Africa, the Levant, and other ports deemed epidemiologically risky were required to anchor offshore while their passengers and sometimes their cargo served a compulsory isolation period before being permitted to enter the city. The duration varied — from days to weeks — depending on the disease profile of the port of origin and what the ship's medical officer had reported during the voyage.

The facility treated cholera patients during the 1835 epidemic, one of the worst of the 19th century to reach Mediterranean France. Cholera was the defining public health crisis of the era: it moved along trade routes, it killed quickly, and it forced every major port city to develop quarantine infrastructure as a matter of urban survival. Marseille's position as the main French gateway to the east — and its enormous volume of passenger and cargo traffic — made the Ratonneau facility both necessary and chronically under pressure.

The hospital operated until the early 20th century, when the germ theory of disease and advances in public health made the old quarantine model obsolete. What remains are atmospheric ruins: long vaulted corridors, overgrown courtyards, and sea walls worn by salt. The Frioul islands are accessible by ferry from the Vieux-Port — a short crossing that gives some sense of the isolation once experienced by the thousands who waited here, watching Marseille on the horizon, unable yet to enter.
