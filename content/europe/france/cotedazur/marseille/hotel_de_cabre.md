The mansion at the corner of the Grande Rue and the Rue de la Bonneterie was built in 1535 for Antoine de Cabre, a Marseille merchant, and is the oldest surviving private house in the city. Its late Gothic facade — carved stone decoration around the windows and door, with Provençal Gothic tracery that begins to absorb Italian Renaissance detailing — represents a transitional moment in the city's architecture, when the trade routes that made Marseille wealthy were also bringing new aesthetic ideas north from Genoa and Florence.

When Haussmann-era street improvements required widening the adjacent road in 1954, demolition seemed inevitable. Instead, the city chose a solution that had rarely been attempted in France: the building was lifted onto rollers, moved 13 metres, and rotated 90 degrees to align with the new street layout. The operation required precise engineering and weeks of preparation. That it succeeded — and that the building now stands slightly askew from its original orientation — is visible if you look carefully at its relationship to the surrounding streetscape.

The building survived the 1943 German demolition of the Panier quarter because it stood just outside the cleared zone; the destruction of the quarter's medieval fabric stopped a few streets away. This proximity to the demolition line gives the Hôtel de Cabre an additional resonance: it is the accident of a boundary that preserved it, twice over, once in the 16th century and once in the 20th.
