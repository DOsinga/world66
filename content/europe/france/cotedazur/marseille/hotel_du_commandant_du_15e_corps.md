France's army corps system, codified during the Third Republic, assigned each corps to a specific territorial region and gave its commanding general a commensurate official residence — the *hôtel du commandant* — that doubled as a social and administrative hub for the military establishment. The 15th Army Corps was garrisoned at Marseille from the late nineteenth century and covered the south-eastern Mediterranean zone, drawing on conscripts from Provence, the Languedoc, and the colonies of North Africa. Its soldiers served in the Franco-Prussian War, the colonial campaigns, and the First World War, where the corps was mobilised as part of the mass expansion of the French army.

The building here reflects the confident institutional architecture of the Belle Époque, when the Republic invested in conspicuous public structures for every branch of state authority — prefectures, post offices, barracks, and officers' residences alike. A general's *hôtel* was expected to project seriousness: solid masonry, formal proportions, a walled courtyard that separated military dignity from the civilian street.

By the mid-twentieth century the reorganisation of the French army made the traditional corps structure largely redundant, and many of these residences were converted or transferred to other state uses. The building survives as a marker of Marseille's long identity as France's principal southern military hub, a city where the legions that garrisoned the empire in Africa and the Levant were equipped and commanded.  The neighbourhood around it still carries traces of the barracks culture that once defined this part of the city.
