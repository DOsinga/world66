The 15th Army Corps was garrisoned at Marseille from the late 19th century, covering the whole south-eastern Mediterranean zone. Its soldiers came from Provence, Languedoc, and the colonies of North Africa. This building was the commanding general's official residence — the *hôtel du commandant* — which also functioned as a social hub for the military establishment: dinners, receptions, the business of running an empire's southern hinge.

The architecture is confident Belle Époque institutional: solid masonry, formal proportions, a walled courtyard keeping the military world separate from the civilian street outside. The Third Republic built like this everywhere — prefectures, barracks, post offices, officers' residences — as if permanence in stone could settle the question of legitimacy.

The corps served in the Franco-Prussian War, the colonial campaigns, and the First World War. By mid-century the army reorganised and the traditional corps structure became redundant. Most of these residences ended up in other state hands. The surrounding neighbourhood still carries traces of the barracks culture that once shaped this part of the city.
