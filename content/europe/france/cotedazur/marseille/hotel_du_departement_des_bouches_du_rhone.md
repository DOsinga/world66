William Alsop finished this in 1994 and the reaction was immediate: bewilderment, then outrage. The design — a vast blue ellipse apparently floating above a lower rectangular base with no visible connection to the ground — broke every convention of French civic architecture. The tradition runs to limestone and symmetry; Alsop delivered what critics called a spaceship that had landed on the wrong continent.

The nickname Le Grand Bleu was coined in mockery. It stuck through affection. The colour is the decisive thing: a deep saturated blue that shifts from near-black in the morning to brilliant turquoise at noon. Alsop said he wanted a building that was of the Mediterranean without being picturesque about it. Thirty years on, the consensus has moved: this is now considered one of the more successful pieces of late 20th-century civic architecture in France. The original critics would be appalled to hear it.

Walk under the floating disc and you're in a shaded, windswept plaza — perpetually strange, undeniably memorable. The building sits north of the centre, surrounded by 1960s social housing. A city that spent decades being managed as a problem built itself a government building that refused to apologise.
