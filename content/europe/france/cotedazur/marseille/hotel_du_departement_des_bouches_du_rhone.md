William Alsop's building for the Conseil Général des Bouches-du-Rhône, completed in 1994, caused immediate and sustained controversy. The design — a vast blue ellipse, clad in reflective panels, apparently floating above a lower rectangular base with no visible connection to the ground — violated every convention of French civic architecture. The French tradition of public buildings runs to limestone, symmetry, and historical reference; Alsop gave Marseille what looked, to hostile critics, like a spaceship that had landed by accident on the wrong continent.

The building's nickname, Le Grand Bleu — the Big Blue — was coined in mockery but stuck through affection. The colour was the decisive choice: a deep, saturated blue that reads differently at different times of day, shifting from near-black in the morning to a brilliant turquoise at noon. Alsop wanted the building to evoke the Mediterranean, to be recognisably of its place while refusing any picturesque regionalism. Whether this succeeded was debated for years; the consensus has moved toward the view that it did. The building is now considered one of the more successful examples of late 20th-century institutional architecture in France — a judgment that would have surprised the original critics considerably.

The ellipse disc houses the chamber of elected representatives and principal offices; the lower building handles administrative services. Between them, a public plaza runs beneath the floating form — shaded, windswept, perpetually strange. The Hôtel du Département sits north of the city centre, near the ring of 1960s and 1970s social housing that marked Marseille's postwar expansion, a context that makes its ambition all the more striking. A city that has often been treated as a problem to be managed built itself a government building that refused to apologise for existing.
