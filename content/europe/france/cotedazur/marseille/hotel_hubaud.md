The Hôtel Hubaud was built for one of Marseille's prosperous merchant families during the 18th century, the peak of the city's commercial power. The Hubaud family were active in the trade networks that made Marseille rich: silk and textiles from the Levant, cod from the Grand Banks of Newfoundland, sugar and cotton from France's Caribbean colonies. The building's refined facade — symmetrical, restrained, and faintly Parisian — signals their ambition to be seen as part of the cultivated merchant aristocracy rather than mere traders.

Marseille's hôtels particuliers occupy a curious position in the history of French domestic architecture. Unlike Paris, where the hôtel particulier developed around a courtyard with high walls screening the street, the Marseille variants tend to open more directly onto the city — larger windows, higher ground floors — concessions to a Mediterranean climate that demanded ventilation over privacy. The Hubaud mansion fits this local type: formal enough to impress, practical enough to breathe.

Most of old Marseille was swept away in two waves of destruction: Haussmann-era redevelopment in the 19th century, and the Nazi demolition of the Panier quarter in 1943. Survivors like the Hôtel Hubaud carry the weight of that erasure; they are the evidence of what the city once looked like before the bulldozers arrived.
