The Hubaud family were in the kind of trade that made Marseille wealthy: silk and textiles from the Levant, cod from Newfoundland, sugar and cotton from the Caribbean colonies. They built this mansion in the 18th century at the peak of the city's commercial power, with a facade that signals exactly the right level of ambition — refined enough to announce cultivated merchants, not so flashy as to look like they were trying too hard.

Marseille's hôtels particuliers are slightly different from the Parisian version. In Paris they turn inward, courtyard behind high walls. Here the ground floors are higher and the windows larger — you need cross-ventilation in this climate. The Hubaud mansion is formal enough to impress, practical enough to breathe.

Most of old Marseille is gone. Haussmann's redevelopment took one layer. The Nazi demolition of the Panier quarter in 1943 took another — 1,500 buildings razed in three days. Buildings like this one carry the weight of that erasure. They're the evidence of what the city looked like before.
