The Hôtel Pascal was built during the reign of Louis XV or Louis XVI, when Marseille's merchant class was at its most confident and its most stylish. The Pascal family — probably in Atlantic or Mediterranean trade, as were virtually all the families who built in this manner — hired local architects schooled in the vocabulary of Parisian classicism: the rusticated base, the piano nobile with its tall windows, the subtle ornament above the cornice. The result is a building that could have stood on a Bordeaux quay or a Lyon street, adapted just enough for the Marseille sun.

Pascal was a common Provençal surname; there is no connection to the philosopher Blaise Pascal, who died in 1662, before the great age of Marseille merchant building. The family that commissioned this house was almost certainly enriched by the Levant trade — Marseille held the French monopoly on commerce with the Ottoman Empire throughout the 18th century — or by the cod trade, which brought Newfoundland fish to Mediterranean Catholic tables and returned with wine and salt.

Urban renewal and wartime destruction eliminated the great majority of Marseille's pre-Revolutionary private architecture. The Hôtel Pascal stands as one of the few remaining witnesses to the domestic life of a city that was, in the 18th century, one of the wealthiest ports in Europe — a fact that is easy to forget when looking at the largely 19th- and 20th-century city that replaced it.
