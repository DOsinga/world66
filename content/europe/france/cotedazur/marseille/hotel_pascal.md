The Hôtel Pascal was built during the reign of Louis XV or XVI, when Marseille's merchant class was at its most confident. The Pascal family — almost certainly enriched by the Levant trade, since Marseille held France's monopoly on commerce with the Ottoman Empire throughout the 18th century — hired architects schooled in Parisian classicism: rusticated base, tall windows on the piano nobile, restrained ornament at the cornice. Adapted just enough for the Marseille sun.

The name Pascal is common in Provence and has no connection to the philosopher, who died in 1662 before the great age of Marseille merchant building. These families made their money from silk, spices, and cod — Newfoundland fish shipped to Catholic Mediterranean tables, wine and salt sent back. The building is a record of that wealth.

Urban renewal and the Nazi demolition of 1943 between them eliminated the great majority of Marseille's pre-Revolutionary private architecture. The Hôtel Pascal is one of the few witnesses left to a city that was, in the 18th century, among the wealthiest ports in Europe. Easy to forget when you look at the largely 19th- and 20th-century city that replaced it.
