The Hôtel Paugoy takes its name from the family that commissioned it, almost certainly merchants enriched by the Mediterranean and Atlantic trades that drove Marseille's economy in the 17th and 18th centuries. The Paugoy family would have been typical of the bourgeoisie marchande — not the old nobility, but wealthy enough to build in the style of those who were, acquiring the social respectability that came with a well-dressed urban mansion.

The building is characteristic of Marseille's merchant-class residential architecture: classically proportioned, sufficiently ornate to signal prosperity without crossing into extravagance, designed to impress clients and peers rather than to house a great household. The ground floor would have served commercial functions — an office, a counting room, storage; the upper floors provided domestic quarters arranged around the polite fiction that the family had left trade behind for the life of the leisured elite.

Pre-Revolutionary Marseille has largely vanished, lost to the reshaping of the 19th century and the catastrophic demolitions of January 1943, when the German occupiers levelled the northern Vieux-Port neighbourhood. The Hôtel Paugoy survives as a remnant of that lost city — a building that has outlasted the world that made it, standing in streets now cut through with later arteries and surrounded by architecture of entirely different periods and intentions.
