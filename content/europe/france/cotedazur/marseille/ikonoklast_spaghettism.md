The title alone is worth stopping for. *Ikonoklast Spaghettism* splices two loaded cultural terms: iconoclasm, the ancient and recurring impulse to smash images and overthrow established visual authority, and "spaghettism," a term with roots in early 20th-century Italian Futurism, where it was used mockingly — and then defiantly — to describe art that celebrated the messy, tangled energy of modern life rather than heroic order. To bring them together is to announce a work that does not take its own gravity too seriously.

Marseille's northern districts have been a fertile ground for exactly this kind of irreverent public art since the 1990s. The city's street art scene — concentrated in part around the cours Julien and spreading into the surrounding quartiers — has long attracted artists who treat the wall and the square as open space for experiment rather than commemoration. *Ikonoklast Spaghettism* fits this lineage: a work that wears its theory lightly and lets the strangeness of its name do half the interpretive work.

The best approach is simply to look at it without expecting resolution. The title is a provocation, not a key — the word "iconoclast" means both destroyer and radical renewer, and "spaghettism" refuses to settle into a single meaning. Whatever the piece looks like, it has already done its first job before you arrive: it has made you think about what public art is supposed to be named, and why.
