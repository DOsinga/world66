Stop and say it out loud: *Ikonoklast Spaghettism*. Iconoclasm — the urge to smash images and overthrow visual authority — welded to spaghettism, a term the Italian Futurists threw around to mean tangled, messy, anti-heroic modern energy. Together they announce a work that refuses to take public art seriously while being, itself, a piece of public art.

Marseille's cours Julien neighbourhood has been producing exactly this kind of irreverent work since the 1990s. Street art spread from there into the surrounding quartiers, wall by wall. Artists here treat the city as open space for experiment, not commemoration.

Don't look for a key in the title — iconoclast means both destroyer and radical renewer, and spaghettism won't settle into one meaning. The work has already done its first job before you arrived: it made you think about what public art is supposed to be called, and why.
