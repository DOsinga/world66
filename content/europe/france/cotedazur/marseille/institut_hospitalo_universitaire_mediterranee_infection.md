In March 2020, this building became the centre of the biggest medical controversy of the pandemic. Professor Didier Raoult, the institute's director, announced results from a small trial suggesting hydroxychloroquine and azithromycin could reduce COVID-19 viral load. Politicians in France and the US seized on it. The world was desperate.

Epidemiologists were not convinced. The trial had no proper control group, a tiny sample, patients excluded after the fact, and unaddressed cardiac risks. Larger randomised trials found no benefit. The episode became a textbook case of how preliminary findings travel faster than the evidence that corrects them.

The institute itself does serious work — infectious diseases of the Mediterranean basin and sub-Saharan Africa, antimicrobial resistance, microbial genomics. Raoult retired in 2022 under disciplinary investigation. The building is still a working research facility, not a monument. But if you have any interest in science, media, and how information moves in a crisis, this is a real place to stand and think about it.
