The tango was born in the port neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires and Montevideo in the late 19th century, a dance created by immigrants from Spain, Italy, West Africa and the Argentine interior who found a common rhythm in each other's company. It spread to Paris in the 1900s and became a European obsession by the 1910s, eventually travelling back to the Americas as a refined art form. Its defining characteristic is partnership: the dance is literally impossible alone, demanding negotiation, counterweight and trust between two bodies in constant communication.

*It Takes Two to Tango* arrives in Marseille — a city similarly constituted by the meeting of populations from Spain, Italy, North Africa, sub-Saharan Africa, the Levant and beyond — with the English idiom intact rather than translated. The choice of language is part of the work's point: the phrase crossed the Atlantic and then the Channel and entered universal currency because it described something universally recognisable. In a city where the rue du Panier has been home to Genoese merchants, Corsican fishermen, Armenian refugees and Comorian families across different centuries, the grammar of the tango is not a metaphor but almost a description.

Whether the work takes sculptural, mural or installation form, its title frames it as an argument for interdependence. Whatever you are looking at — two figures, a pair of motifs, a space designed for two people — the tango's implicit instruction applies: this does not work unless both parties show up.
