Émile Duclaux (1840–1904) was a chemist and microbiologist who spent most of his working life alongside Louis Pasteur, first at the École Normale Supérieure and then at the Institut Pasteur in Paris, which he directed after Pasteur's death in 1895. He was among the central figures of the germ theory generation — the researchers who turned microbiology from a curiosity into a practical discipline with consequences for food safety, medicine, and public health. He is also known for intervening publicly in the Dreyfus Affair on the side of Dreyfus, a position that cost him relationships but underscored a scientific community's obligation to evidence over authority.

The garden sits in a quiet residential quarter of the 6th arrondissement, in the arc of streets that climbs from the Vieux-Port towards the hillier neighbourhoods above the corniche. The 6th is one of Marseille's more established bourgeois districts, and small squares and jardins here tend to carry names of republican scientists, writers, and public servants — the commemorative grammar of the Third Republic, which treated science as a civic virtue. Duclaux fits that pattern precisely.

The space itself is a modest urban garden rather than a destination — benches, shade, a pause between buildings. For a city that prides itself on Mediterranean outdoor life, even the smallest green squares perform a real social function in summer, and this one is no exception.
