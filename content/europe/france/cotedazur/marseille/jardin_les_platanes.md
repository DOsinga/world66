The platane — plane tree, Platanus × acerifolia — is the quintessential tree of southern French public space. It lines the cours and allées of Aix-en-Provence, shades the boules players of village squares across Provence, and marks the boundaries between public gardens and surrounding streets in dozens of Provençal towns. Its wide canopy, rapid growth, and tolerance of urban conditions made it the tree of choice for 19th-century municipal planners across the region, and it is now so associated with southern France that a jardin named simply "Les Platanes" immediately signals what to expect on arrival.

The garden sits in the northern arrondissements of Marseille, in a district close to the old port and the city's historic commercial centre. The northern inner arrondissements — the 2nd and 3rd — contain some of Marseille's most historically complex urban fabric: the Panier, the port area, the zone around the Cathédrale de la Major, and the densely populated streets that were partially demolished during the wartime occupation and rebuilt in the postwar decades.

A garden named for its trees rather than a person is refreshingly direct. The platanes themselves are the attraction and the function — shade in summer, filtered light in spring, the particular texture of mottled bark that distinguishes mature plane trees and makes them one of the most visually distinctive elements of Mediterranean urban landscape.
