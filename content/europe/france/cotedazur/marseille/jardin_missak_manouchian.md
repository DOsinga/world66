The garden takes its name from Missak Manouchian, an Armenian survivor of the 1915 genocide who arrived in France as a child and became a poet, a communist, and one of the most effective resistance organisers in occupied Paris. He led the Francs-Tireurs et Partisans — Main d'Œuvre Immigrée, a network of foreign-born fighters who carried out dozens of attacks on the German occupation force. Arrested in November 1943, he was shot at Mont Valérien on 21 February 1944 alongside twenty-one members of his group — most of them immigrants and Jews — after the Nazis plastered Paris with a propaganda poster designed to exploit their foreignness. The "Affiche Rouge" backfired badly and Manouchian became a martyr of the French Resistance.

The Marseille garden was named before his 2024 panthéonisation, reflecting a long-standing grassroots campaign by the French-Armenian community and the French Communist Party. The PCF's role in the process was complicated: party historians had long disputed whether a party informant contributed to the network's exposure in 1943, and the panthéonisation prompted renewed scrutiny of that question. Manouchian's wife Mélinée, herself a resistance figure who outlived him by decades, was interred alongside him.

The garden sits in a neighbourhood shaped by successive waves of Armenian settlement. The first wave came after 1915, when survivors of the genocide disembarked at Marseille — one of the main entry points for refugees from the Ottoman Empire — and many never left. Their descendants are now four or five generations deep. Walking through here, the street names and the community institutions are a quiet record of that history, which the garden extends.
