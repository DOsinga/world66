The mulberry tree — mûrier in French — has a specific historical resonance in Provence. Introduced to support silkworm cultivation, mulberry groves were planted across the region during the periods when silk production was a significant part of the Provençal economy, particularly in the 18th and 19th centuries. Though the silk industry declined before Marseille's modern urban expansion, mulberry trees remained in landscapes that had once been agricultural land, and their names persist in Provençal place names and street geography as traces of that agricultural history.

The garden sits in the northwest of Marseille, in the 15th or 16th arrondissement — the coastal northern districts that border the industrial port zone and the étang de Berre. This is a part of the city shaped by port industry and by large-scale public housing built from the 1950s through the 1980s to accommodate workers and subsequent generations. The grands ensembles here — the housing estates of La Savine, Les Lauriers, and the northern coastal strip — represent one of the more challenging chapters in French urban planning, and the provision of municipal parks in these arrondissements is part of an ongoing effort to improve the quality of public space in areas that were built quickly and densely without adequate green infrastructure.

A "jardin municipal d'arrondissement" signals its administrative character: this is green space provided specifically for the arrondissement's population, part of the formal distribution of civic amenities across the city. The name Les Mûriers connects the urban present to a pre-industrial landscape that has otherwise been entirely built over.
