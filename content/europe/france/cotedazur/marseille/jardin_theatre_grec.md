Marseille was founded around 600 BCE by Greek colonists from Phocaea, a city on the Aegean coast of what is now Turkey. The Phocaeans called their settlement Massalia; it became one of the most prosperous Greek colonies in the western Mediterranean, trading up the Rhône valley and along the coast of Iberia. The city's Greek origins are not merely historical — they are part of its active self-presentation, its claim to a Mediterranean identity that predates Roman conquest and French statehood.

The jardin théâtre grec — a garden theatre using the Greek amphitheatre form — is part of this claim. The Greek theatre design, with tiers of stone seating rising on a hillside around a circular performance space, was developed precisely for outdoor summer performance under Mediterranean skies. To build one in Marseille is to close a loop: a form invented in the Mediterranean world, brought to its westernmost colonial outpost, used for performance in the same kind of climate that produced it.

The venue operates in summer, when Marseille's long dry season makes open-air performance reliable. The garden setting and the stone tiers give it a character distinct from the city's indoor theatres — more communal, more connected to the landscape, more continuous with the tradition of performance as a public civic event rather than a private cultural transaction. The repertoire tends toward work that suits the scale and openness of the space: classical material, large casts, productions that can hold an outdoor audience.
