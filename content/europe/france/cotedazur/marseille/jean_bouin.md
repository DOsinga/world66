Jean Bouin grew up in the working-class neighbourhoods of Marseille and discovered competitive running through local athletics clubs in the years before the First World War. He became one of the most celebrated distance runners in France, setting world records at distances from 3,000 to 10,000 metres and winning cross-country European titles. At the 1912 Stockholm Olympics he ran the 5,000 metres final in the finest race of his career — finishing in 14 minutes 36.7 seconds, a world record that stood for years — only to be beaten by Finland's Hannes Kolehmainen, who crossed the line less than a second ahead. The silver medal and the manner of his defeat made him a French national hero rather than a footnote.

Bouin was mobilised at the outbreak of war in August 1914 and killed in action near Crouy-sur-Ourcq during the opening weeks of fighting on the Western Front, on 29 September 1914. He was 26. His death, coming so early in the conflict, sent a particular shock through French sporting culture — here was a man whose physical gifts had seemed almost superhuman, now gone in the mud of the Marne alongside tens of thousands of others. He became emblematic of a generation of young men who had reached the height of their powers and were then erased by the war.

His name has been given to stadiums in both Marseille and Barcelona, where the Estadi Johan Cruyff — long known as the Estadi Jean-Bouin — still carries his memory on its walls. This monument near the Stade Vélodrome district keeps his name in the city that formed him. The tradition of memorialising athletes who died in the First World War reflects a French understanding of sport as civic preparation — the track and the battlefield as connected by the same virtues of endurance and discipline.
