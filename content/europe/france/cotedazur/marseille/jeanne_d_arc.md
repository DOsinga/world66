Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431) needs little introduction: the peasant girl from Domrémy who claimed divine voices, led French armies to lift the siege of Orléans, saw the Dauphin crowned at Reims, was captured by the Burgundians, sold to the English, tried for heresy and witchcraft by a church court at Rouen, and burned at the stake at the age of approximately nineteen. She was rehabilitated posthumously by a second church trial in 1456, beatified in 1909, and canonised in 1920. She is one of the patron saints of France.

The proliferation of Joan of Arc statues across French cities is a phenomenon of the Third Republic, particularly the decades after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine. Republican politicians, Catholic conservatives, and later nationalists of every stripe all claimed her as their symbol — a girl who had driven the English from France could be made to mean many things depending on who was doing the talking. Emmanuel Frémiet's gilded equestrian statue in Paris (1874) set the dominant visual type: Joan in armour, on horseback, banner raised. Variations on this image multiplied across the country, in town squares, church forecourts, and public gardens.

Marseille's statue of Joan stands in the residential northern part of the city, a reminder that the national mythology she represents was propagated even in cities geographically remote from the Hundred Years' War battlefields of the Loire. The particularity of each local statue lies less in the figure itself — the iconography is largely standardised — than in its placement, its donors, and what it was meant to tell a specific community about itself at the moment it was erected.
