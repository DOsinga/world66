Joan of Arc's story needs no retelling — but her statues do. After France lost the Franco-Prussian War in 1871 and surrendered Alsace-Lorraine, politicians, Catholics, and nationalists all rushed to claim the same symbol: the peasant girl who drove the English out. Municipalities from Calais to Marseille erected her image in town squares and church forecourts. The iconography was nearly identical everywhere — armour, horse, banner raised.

This one stands in the residential north, a long way from the Loire Valley battlefields. That distance is the point. The Third Republic wanted Joan everywhere, in every quarter, reminding every citizen what France meant to be. The local meaning of each statue lives less in the figure than in who paid for it and what they were trying to tell the neighbourhood in the year they put it up.
