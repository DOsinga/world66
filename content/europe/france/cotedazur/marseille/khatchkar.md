A khachkar (Armenian: խաչքար, literally "cross-stone") is a carved stone stele unique to Armenian Christian culture. Each one bears an ornate cross at its centre, surrounded by intricate interlace and vegetal patterns whose complexity is a point of artistic pride — no two khachkars are identical, and the finest examples are considered among the most sophisticated achievements of medieval stone carving anywhere in the world. They have been produced continuously since the ninth century, serving as memorial markers, votive objects, boundary stones, and dedications. In 2010 UNESCO added the art of khachkar craftsmanship to its Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

The khachkar in Marseille speaks directly to the city's Armenian community, which took root here in the years following the 1915 genocide — the systematic killing and deportation of Armenians by the Ottoman government during the First World War, in which somewhere between six hundred thousand and one and a half million people died. Survivors fled in waves to France, with Marseille serving as a primary port of entry. Many settled here permanently, joining an existing small Armenian diaspora and building churches, cultural associations, and schools. Today the Marseille metropolitan area is home to one of the largest Armenian communities in France.

The stone here is both a religious object and a monument of identity. Khachkars installed in diaspora communities carry additional weight: they anchor a people to a cultural tradition that genocide sought to erase, and they inscribe that tradition into the public space of an adopted city. To pass one on a Marseille street is to encounter Armenia — its artistic language, its grief, and its survival — planted in Mediterranean stone.
