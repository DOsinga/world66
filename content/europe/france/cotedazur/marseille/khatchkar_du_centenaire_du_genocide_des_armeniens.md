Khatchkars — from the Armenian words for cross and stone — are the distinctive carved memorial steles of Armenian Christianity. The tradition dates from the 9th century: each stone is unique, cut with interlocking geometric patterns around a central cross, and consecrated as a monument to the dead or as an act of thanksgiving. They are found wherever Armenians have settled, which now means every continent. This one was erected in 2015 for the centenary of the genocide, when commemorations in France were among the most extensive anywhere in the world.

France recognised the Armenian genocide by parliamentary resolution in 2001, early among Western nations and well ahead of several that still have not done so. The recognition reflected both the weight of historical evidence and the political organisation of the French-Armenian diaspora — one of the largest and oldest in the world, concentrated in Marseille, Lyon, and the Paris suburbs. The 2015 centenary events were large and well-attended, combining official ceremony with community mourning for a catastrophe that most survivors' families had never stopped grieving privately.

Marseille's Armenian community traces its roots directly to 1915. Survivors who escaped the deportations and massacres reached the Mediterranean coast by various routes and many passed through Marseille, either staying or continuing north. A century later their descendants number in the tens of thousands, with their own churches, schools, cultural organisations, and political associations. The community's presence is woven into the city's social fabric in ways that this memorial makes visible.
