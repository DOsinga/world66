*L'Arbre de l'Espérance* (The Tree of Hope) stands in a quarter of Marseille where civic life and street culture intersect, not far from the animated pedestrian energy of Cours Julien. The work draws on a deep vein of French symbolism: during the Revolution, municipalities across France planted arbres de la Liberté — liberty trees, usually young oaks or poplars — in public squares as living monuments to democratic renewal. This sculptural tree continues that tradition in metal and stone rather than rootstock, fixing the gesture permanently into the streetscape.

The choice of hope over liberty shifts the register slightly. Where liberty trees were declarative — planting one was a political act — a tree of hope is more searching, more open-ended. Hope implies something still to come, a condition not yet achieved. In a city as economically complex as Marseille, where different quartiers experience very different versions of opportunity, that openness reads as honest rather than naively triumphalist.

The work repays a slow look. The branching structure, whether abstract or naturalistic, is designed to be read from multiple angles, and the quality of Mediterranean light — brilliant in summer, softer in autumn — changes its character across the day. It is the kind of piece that improves on a return visit.
