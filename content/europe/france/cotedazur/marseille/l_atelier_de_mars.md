The name *L'Atelier de Mars* — the workshop or studio of Mars — is a deliberate paradox. Mars is the Roman god of war, and antiquity did not typically grant him a workshop; that role belonged to Vulcan, the divine blacksmith, or to Minerva in her capacity as patron of craft. To give Mars an atelier, a space of creative making rather than destructive force, is to reframe martial energy as something that can be turned toward construction. The work sits in the northern reaches of Marseille, a part of the city with its own complicated history of industrial labour and neighbourhood resilience.

The piece occupies a stretch of the streetscape that is not conventionally scenic, which is precisely the point. Public art in the northern arrondissements has historically been scarce compared with the investment poured into the Vieux-Port and the waterfront. *L'Atelier de Mars* inserts itself into that imbalance — a work of some ambition placed where you might not expect it, asserting that the creative impulse is not the exclusive property of tourist-friendly quarters.

Whether the "atelier" is literal (the street itself as a site of making) or purely metaphorical (the repurposing of martial energy), the piece asks you to think about transformation. A city that has survived centuries of violence, plague, and economic flux has its own kind of forge. What comes out of it is shaped by everything that went in.
