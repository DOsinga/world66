Pliny the Elder recorded the story of a boy at Baiae who befriended a dolphin, which carried him across the bay to school each day. Renaissance sculptors ran with the image — Verrocchio's putto with a dolphin in Florence codified it for centuries. *L'Enfant aux Dauphins* arrives in that tradition, but the setting gives it specific local weight.

Marseille's ancient name, Massalia, was linked to dolphins by the Greeks — the animals appear on Massalian coins going back to the fourth century BCE. Whether or not the folk etymology holds, dolphins are part of how this city understands its relationship to the sea. A child with a dolphin here is not decoration borrowed from elsewhere.

The scale is deliberately intimate. A child and dolphins are not heroic — they're playful, slightly improbable, a moment of trust between species. In a city as hard-edged and proud as Marseille, that tenderness has its own force. The sculpture stands near the southern shore, where the calanques begin and the Mediterranean opens in earnest — exactly the right place for it.
