The child-with-dolphin motif is among the most persistent in Western sculpture. Pliny the Elder recorded the story of a boy at Baiae who befriended a dolphin, which carried him daily across the bay to school — a tale so charming that it circulated for centuries as proof of the animal's affection for humans. Renaissance fountain sculptors revived the image endlessly; Verrocchio's famous putto with a dolphin in Florence codified it for the modern era. *L'Enfant aux Dauphins* arrives in Marseille as the inheritor of all of that, but the setting gives it a particular local resonance.

The sculpture stands near the southern shore of the city, where the calanques begin and the Mediterranean opens in earnest. Marseille's ancient name, Massalia, was associated with dolphins in the folk etymology current among the Greeks: some ancient sources connected the city's identity to those famously sociable creatures of the inland sea. Whether or not the etymology holds, dolphins appear on Massalian coins, and the animals remain part of how the city understands its relationship to the water. A child with a dolphin here is not decoration borrowed from elsewhere — it is an image that belongs.

The scale of the work matters. A child and dolphins are not heroic or monumental; they are playful, intimate, slightly improbable. The sculpture captures a moment of trust between species: the child unafraid, the dolphins willing. In a city as hard-edged and proud as Marseille, that tenderness reads as something quietly radical.
