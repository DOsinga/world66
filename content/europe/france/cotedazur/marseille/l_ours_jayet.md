*Jayet* is an old French word for jet — the lustrous fossilised wood prized since antiquity for its intense, light-absorbing black. Medieval craftsmen worked it into mourning jewellery. *L'Ours Jayet* takes that material's logic into sculpture: a bear with a surface so dark and polished it seems to swallow the light rather than reflect it.

Bears have no natural place in the Provençal landscape. The last Pyrenean bears are far to the west; the Mediterranean scrubland was never their country. That displacement is part of the point. A bear in a southern city is a heraldic animal — a creature of coats of arms and civic fantasy, not ecology — and this one wears that role without apology. It stands on the western side of the city, away from the tourist circuits, in a neighbourhood where public art has to justify itself against the backdrop of ordinary daily life.

The scale matters. A full-grown bear in sculpture creates a small gravitational field — a reason to stop and recalibrate. Locals have made their peace with it, the way Marseillais make their peace with most things: with a shrug, and eventually a proprietary pride.
