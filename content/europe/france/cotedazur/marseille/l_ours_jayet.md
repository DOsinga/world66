Jayet is an old French word for jet — the lustrous fossilised wood prized since antiquity for its intense, light-absorbing black. Jet beads were found in Gallo-Roman graves; medieval craftsmen worked it into mourning jewellery; and the word survived long enough to give this bear its name. L'Ours Jayet is a sculpture in that same spirit: a bear rendered in a surface so dark and so smooth that it seems to pull the light toward itself rather than reflect it.

Bears have no natural place in the Provençal landscape — the last Pyrenean bears are far to the west, and the Mediterranean scrubland has never been their country. That displacement is part of the sculpture's charge. A bear in a southern city is a heraldic animal, a creature of coats of arms and civic fantasy rather than ecology, and L'Ours Jayet wears that role without apology. It stands on the western side of the city, away from the tourist circuits, in a neighbourhood where public art has to justify itself against the backdrop of ordinary daily life.

The scale matters. A full-grown bear, even in sculpture, occupies space in a way that smaller figures do not — it creates a small gravitational field, a reason to slow down and recalibrate. Locals have made their peace with it in the way that Marseillais make their peace with most things: with a shrug and eventually, once enough time has passed, a kind of proprietorial pride.
