Marseille has always lived alongside the sea, and few symbols capture that relationship as viscerally as the whale. La Baleine, a large-scale public sculpture positioned near the southern harbour, plays on the city's deep maritime identity — the creature has haunted sailors' imaginations since antiquity, and Provence's own waters still occasionally yield sightings of fin whales and sperm whales pushing through the Gulf of Lion. The work invites passersby to reckon with the sheer scale of ocean life, the whale's body dwarfing the human figures that inevitably cluster around it.

The sculpture sits in the southern arrondissements, away from the tourist churn of the Vieux-Port. That peripheral position feels deliberate: great whales are creatures of open water, not harbours. Local lore around Marseille has long included stories of dead whales beached on the coast, each one drawing crowds and provoking a kind of reverent unease that no smaller animal quite produces.

Whether La Baleine reads as celebration, elegy, or environmental provocation depends on when you arrive. At low tide, when the stone around it is bare and the smell of brine is strong, the whale seems stranded. At high tide or in evening light reflected off the water, the same form reads as something surfacing — a reminder that the sea has depths the city has never fully fathomed.
