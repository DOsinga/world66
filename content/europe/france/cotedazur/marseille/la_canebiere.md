The name comes from the Provençal *canebe*, hemp. Before the city expanded and absorbed this ground, rope-makers worked here just outside the walls — the industry that supplied the port's rigging and mooring lines. When Marseille grew and the Canebière was laid out as a broad boulevard running down to the Vieux-Port, the name stayed.

At its peak in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the Canebière was lined with department stores, grand hotels, luxury shops, and the social institutions of a prosperous Mediterranean city. The Noailles and the major banks occupied the upper blocks; the cafes where merchants conducted business occupied the lower ones. Sailors from every country knew it; the street appeared in enough travel writing to have its own mythology. Marseillais compared it, without irony, to the Champs-Élysées.

The decline after the 1960s was sharp. Commercial life moved to suburban shopping centres; the demographics of the adjacent Belsunce neighbourhood shifted as new communities arrived; investment went elsewhere. The Canebière today is still a major artery — the tram runs its length, the buildings are mostly intact, the grand scale has not been broken — but the luxury commerce has not come back. It rewards walking from end to end as a study in what happens when a 19th-century city loses confidence in its own centre.
