The northern arrondissements of Marseille contain some of the city's most honest industrial archaeology. La Cartonnerie — literally, the cardboard works — takes its name from its origins as a facility involved in cardboard or packaging manufacture, an industry that serviced the port's vast export trade in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Corrugated cardboard, paper packaging, and cardboard sheets were produced in factories like this one to wrap the soap, oil, and preserved fish that made Marseille's fortune.

The building sits in the Belle de Mai district, the neighbourhood that became famous when the Friche la Belle de Mai — a former tobacco factory — was colonised by artists and cultural organisations in the early 1990s. That conversion set a template for the area: large, cheap industrial space plus proximity to a dense working-class population produced a particular kind of creative energy that more polished cultural institutions struggle to replicate. La Cartonnerie followed a similar logic, its thick walls and generous floor plans making it adaptable to workshops, rehearsal spaces, and exhibition areas that cultural operators were looking for.

The smell of machine oil and pressed paper is long gone, replaced by whatever projects currently occupy the building. But the bones of an industrial past are still legible in the structure — the loading bays, the heavy lintels, the generous ceiling heights designed for equipment rather than people. That history gives the building a credibility that a purpose-built arts centre rarely achieves.
