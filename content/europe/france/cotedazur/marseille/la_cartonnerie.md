The name means what it says: cardboard works. This factory produced the packaging that wrapped Marseille's soap, olive oil, and preserved fish for export — the industrial guts of a port city, tucked into Belle de Mai's dense streets. The port needed paper the way it needed rope and barrels.

Belle de Mai became famous in the early 1990s when artists colonised the Friche, a disused tobacco factory nearby. Large, cheap, close to a working-class neighbourhood — that combination produces a kind of creative energy that purpose-built arts centres can't manufacture. La Cartonnerie followed the same logic.

The machine oil and pressed paper smell is gone. What's left are high ceilings, heavy lintels, loading bays, and floor space designed for equipment rather than people. That inherited scale is the building's real asset. A venue that started as a theatre or gallery would have been built smaller.
