The crypt was built in the 5th century over a 3rd-century Christian necropolis on the south shore of the Vieux-Port, on a site sacred to Marseille's Christian community since the Roman period. The church visible above ground was constructed in the 11th and 14th centuries, so the crypt is the older structure by a considerable margin — descending into it is a move backwards in time by nearly a millennium relative to the nave.

The Black Madonna (Notre-Dame-de-la-Confession) is a 3rd-century Egyptian statue venerated here since late antiquity, predating the Christianisation of the Roman Empire. Each year on 2 February the Chandeleur procession carries her through Marseille's streets — one of the oldest continuous Christian processions in France, uninterrupted except during the Revolution when the statue was hidden to prevent its destruction.

The sarcophagi lining the crypt walls date from the 3rd and 4th centuries, when Marseille was a significant early Christian community on the trade routes between Rome and North Africa. The tomb identified as that of Saint Victor — a Roman soldier martyred in 303 CE — is marked here, though scholars are divided on the attribution. Saint Cassian's tomb, dated to 304 CE, is considered more securely documented.
