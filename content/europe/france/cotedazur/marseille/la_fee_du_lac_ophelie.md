When John Everett Millais exhibited his painting of Ophelia in 1852, he fixed an image the 19th century could not stop returning to: a young woman floating face-up in a stream, flowers drifting around her, the border between life and death dissolved in water. The painting fed a broader cultural fixation on women and water, enchantment and drowning, that persisted well into the Belle Époque. La Fée du Lac Ophélie draws on exactly that tradition, combining the Shakespearean heroine with the *fée du lac* — the lake fairy of French and Celtic folklore, a supernatural female guardian of springs, pools, and still water.

The pairing is not arbitrary. Both figures inhabit thresholds: the fairy stands between the human world and an enchanted realm; Ophelia stands between sanity and madness, life and death. Water is the medium of both transformations. In French Romantic sculpture, these two archetypes often merged into a single idealised female form — serene, remote, beautiful, and ultimately unreachable, qualities that the sculptors and their patrons associated with the southern landscape's own quality of luminous, slightly vertiginous stillness.

The sculpture is positioned in the southern part of Marseille, away from the harbour, in a residential quarter where the pace of the city slows. Finding it requires a short detour from the main thoroughfares, which suits the subject: Ophelia and the lake fairy both reward those who seek them out in quiet corners, rather than announcing themselves from the main road.
