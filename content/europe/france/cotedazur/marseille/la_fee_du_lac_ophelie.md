In 1852, John Everett Millais exhibited his painting of Ophelia: a young woman floating face-up in a stream, flowers drifting around her, the border between life and death dissolved in water. The image stuck. The 19th century couldn't stop returning to it. La Fée du Lac Ophélie merges Shakespeare's heroine with the *fée du lac* — the lake fairy of French and Celtic folklore, the supernatural guardian of springs and still water.

The pairing works because both figures occupy the same threshold. The fairy stands between the human world and an enchanted realm; Ophelia stands between sanity and madness, life and death. Water is the medium of both. In French Romantic sculpture they collapsed into one idealized form — serene, remote, unreachable.

The sculpture sits in a quiet residential quarter south of the harbour, off the main thoroughfares. That suits the subject. Ophelia and the lake fairy both reward the short detour, not the main road.
