Marseille's hills were once dotted with windmills. The mistral — the cold, dry north wind that sweeps down the Rhône valley and out across the Gulf of Lion — was a reliable power source for the mills that ground grain, crushed olives, and processed the raw materials of a growing city. By the 19th century most had fallen into disuse as steam power arrived, but their ruins persisted in the landscape, marking high points with the silhouettes of broken towers and silent vanes. The *Vieux Moulin* — the Old Mill — that gives this site its name is one such remnant, embedded in the eastern edge of the city where the terrain rises toward the Massif des Calanques.

La Fée du Vieux Moulin places a fairy figure in this landscape of industrial ruin, a choice that aligns with the Romantic tradition of inhabiting ruins with supernatural presences. In Provençal folklore, mills were liminal places — active at night when the miller slept, subject to strange sounds and movements that popular imagination attributed to spirits or *fées*. The fairy of the old mill is in some sense the genius loci of a place that technology abandoned but memory preserved.

The eastern coordinates place the sculpture at some distance from the tourist circuit, in a part of Marseille that rewards exploration on foot. The views from this elevated position extend across the city toward the coast, and on clear days the Château d'If is visible offshore — another ruin haunted, at least in fiction, by memorable captives. The mill and its fairy occupy a quieter register, but the impulse to animate old stonework with story is the same.
