La Force — Strength, or Force — stands as a counterpart to neighbouring La Fidélité, the two figures almost certainly conceived as part of a single decorative programme for a civic or institutional building. The pairing was deliberate: the 19th century understood the virtues as a system, each one checking and completing the others. Fidelity without force is fragile; force without fidelity is mere power. Together they state something about how a city intended to govern itself.

The figure follows the classical type: muscular or imposing bearing, perhaps carrying a column shaft or a lion — conventional attributes for Strength across European sculpture from antiquity onward. The execution is the work of the stone-carver's trade at a moment when such figures were produced in considerable numbers for institutional commissions across France; the quality tends to be competent and serious rather than inspired, which gives allegories like this one an appropriate gravity.

Set close to La Fidélité in the same streetscape, the two sculptures form a small open-air programme that rewards a few minutes of comparison. The proximity is not coincidental: they were placed to be read together, two terms in a moral sentence that the building between them was meant to embody.
