The distinction matters: genuine savon de Marseille under the traditional method contains at least 72% vegetable oil (classically olive), is cooked in large copper vats for 72 hours in a four-stage process, and contains no artificial additives, preservatives, or colourants. The resulting soap is a homogeneous block with no layering. Industrial 'Marseille soap' is produced by continuous saponification using different feedstocks and is chemically distinct — a difference that cosmetics and dermatology research has noted in studies on skin irritation.

The factory is one of only a handful in Marseille — and a small number in all of France — still operating by the original *cuisson en chaudron* method. Visitors can observe the production, which is an industrial process rather than an artisanal demonstration: the copper vats, the pasting and finishing stages, the cutting and stamping of bars. A Protected Geographic Indication that would legally restrict the name to traditionally produced soap has been under discussion in French regulatory bodies for decades and has not yet been enacted.

Marseille's soap industry at its height in the early 20th century comprised over 90 factories in and around the city, employing thousands and exporting across Europe and the French colonial world. The industry collapsed after World War II when synthetic detergents captured most of the market. What remains is a small premium sector selling on authenticity and skin-care properties — historically accurate marketing in a case where the historical process actually does produce a chemically different product.
