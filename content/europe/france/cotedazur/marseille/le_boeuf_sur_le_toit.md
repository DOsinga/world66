The name Le Bœuf sur le Toit — "The Ox on the Roof" — entered legend in 1920 when Jean Cocteau attached it to a short theatrical piece with music by Darius Milhaud. Milhaud's score wove Brazilian popular tunes into a riotous accompaniment for a clown act at the Cirque Medrano, and the absurdist title stuck. The following year a bar opened in Paris's 8th arrondissement under that name and quickly became the living room of the Groupe des Six — Milhaud, Poulenc, Honegger, Auric, Tailleferre, and Durey — alongside Cocteau, Picasso, and the whole glittering crowd of interwar Paris. It relocated to a celebrated address near the Champs-Élysées and eventually became one of the grand Parisian brasseries; the name is shorthand for a certain golden bohemian moment in French cultural life.

Marseille's adoption of the name belongs to the long French tradition of provincial establishments borrowing Parisian prestige, though the city hardly needed the loan. Its café culture runs deeper and more raucous than Paris's, fed by centuries of Mediterranean trade. A bar with this name here sits at the intersection of two great French drinking traditions: the formal brasserie of the north and the loose, sociable terrasse of the south.

Set in a residential quarter well away from the Old Port's tourist circuit, the place has the character of a genuine neighbourhood bar — the kind of social gravity that the Parisian original's grand brasserie setting can no longer quite claim. The Groupe des Six, who preferred the informal to the ceremonious, would probably have approved.
