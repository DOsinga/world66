On 21 April 1960, Brazil inaugurated a brand-new capital carved out of the central plateau — Brasília, a planned city designed by urban planner Lúcio Costa and architect Oscar Niemeyer whose sweeping concrete curves became the defining images of postwar modernist optimism. The spectacle captivated Europe. France had particularly deep cultural ties with Brazil: Brazilian writers, musicians, and intellectuals had long moved through Paris, and Niemeyer himself maintained a Paris studio where he designed the Communist Party headquarters in the early 1970s. Across France, bars, hotels, and restaurants named Le Brasilia or Le Brasil appeared through the decade, trading on the excitement of a utopia under construction.

Marseille, a city with its own long Atlantic and colonial connections, was fertile ground for the trend. The southern port had been commercially entangled with Brazil since the nineteenth century through coffee and textile trades, and the Brazilian name would have carried a warm familiarity here as well as cosmopolitan glamour. The timing also coincided with the postwar expansion of Marseille's southern districts, where this establishment sits, giving the name a sense of the new alongside the exotic.

Today Le Brasilia is one of those neighbourhood fixtures whose name outlasted the moment that inspired it. Niemeyer's city is still there on the plateau, UNESCO-listed and still controversial; the brasserie down the street from the Prado beaches carries on in the Mediterranean sun, the Brazil connection now part of the furniture.
