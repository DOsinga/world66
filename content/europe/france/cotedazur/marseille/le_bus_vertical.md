Standing a vehicle on its end is a gesture that never quite loses its power to disorient. A bus is the most democratic object in the urban landscape — unglamorous, shared, taken for granted — and tipping it vertical transforms it into something between a monument and a joke, a piece of found sculpture that forces a second look at an object you have spent your life ignoring. Works in this idiom have appeared in cities across Europe, each time provoking the same double-take: the familiar made impossible, the everyday suddenly architectural.

Marseille's version is planted in the northern districts, a part of the city that rarely appears in tourist itineraries but has been the site of sustained artistic investment. The years around Marseille-Provence 2013 European Capital of Culture brought a particular surge of public art into overlooked neighbourhoods, with commissions and interventions intended to reactivate streets and squares on the city's periphery. A vertical bus is exactly the kind of work suited to that brief — arresting without being precious, legible to a passerby who has never set foot in a gallery.

The piece rewards the effort of coming out this far. There is something genuinely surreal about encountering it on an ordinary street, flanked by apartment blocks and parked cars, the bus's destination blind now pointing at the sky. It is good public art in the simplest sense: it makes the neighbourhood stranger and more interesting than it was before.
