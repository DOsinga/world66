For centuries, itinerant bear trainers were part of European street life. They came from the Alpine valleys of Savoy and Piedmont, from the Balkans — regions where catching and taming bears had become a family trade passed down through generations. A trainer would walk a circuit through towns and villages: the bear danced, wrestled, played dead, begged. Marginal, exhausting work. The bears were taken young and trained by methods that would be illegal everywhere now.

Marseille was a transit city and a port, its streets in the 19th century full of itinerant performers on circuits looping through Provence and into Italy. The figure here — a man in heavy clothing holding the lead — belonged to that world. Bear training disappeared from France by the 1930s, squeezed out by animal welfare legislation and the collapse of the rural markets that sustained it.

What the sculpture preserves is a profession that was ordinary for five hundred years and then simply stopped. That's the kind of thing a city forgets. This one kept a record.
