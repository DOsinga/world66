Mirabeau was born in 1749 at the Château de Mirabeau near Aix-en-Provence, into a family of Provençal nobility whose name he would make synonymous with revolutionary eloquence. His early life was chaotic by any standard: imprisoned multiple times by his own father through royal lettre de cachet, exiled, involved in scandal, perpetually in debt. By the time the Estates-General was convened in 1789, he had lived enough to understand both the aristocratic world he came from and the popular anger that was about to sweep it away.

He represented the Third Estate of Aix at the Estates-General, having been rejected by the nobility of Provence who refused to have him. That rejection turned out to be consequential: speaking for the commons rather than his own class, Mirabeau became the dominant orator of the early Revolution — a man whose physical bulk, scarred face, and ferocious rhetorical power made him impossible to ignore. He argued for a constitutional monarchy, worked secretly as an advisor to Louis XVI, and tried to hold together a moderate course that the logic of events was making impossible. He died in April 1791, before the worst of what followed. The Revolution he had partly unleashed would probably have devoured him.

The name persists in Provence as a marker of a specific kind of political tradition: aristocratic in origin, popular in appeal, theatrical in method, and ultimately ambiguous in its loyalties. A bar or hotel calling itself Le Mirabeau is invoking all of that — the region's most famous political figure, and one of the Revolution's most compelling and contradictory voices.
