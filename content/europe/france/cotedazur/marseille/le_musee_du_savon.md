Soap has been produced in Marseille at commercial scale since at least the 14th century, using the olive oil that was the region's principal agricultural product. By the 17th century the soap had acquired sufficient reputation that Colbert formalised it: Louis XIV's 1688 edict restricted the name savon de Marseille to soap produced in the Marseille region and specified a minimum olive oil content — one of the earliest geographic indications in French commercial history, contemporary with the regulation of Champagne.

Industrial production peaked in the late 19th century. Marseille's factories exported the soap in long bars — cut to weight by the buyer, not pre-packaged — across Europe and to French colonial markets. The 20th century dismantled this industry systematically: manufacturers substituted cheaper palm and copra oils for olive oil; synthetic detergents captured the laundry market after World War II; and the savon de Marseille name, inadequately protected, was applied commercially to soaps made far from Marseille with no olive oil at all. Genuine savon de Marseille — hard soap with at least 72% olive oil content — is now produced by fewer than five traditional manufacturers and carries a Label Rouge.

The museum documents the production process: saponification in large open cauldrons, the taillage where the hardened soap is cut into blocks, the stamping of the traditional 300-gram cube. It also draws the distinction between genuine savon de Marseille and mass-produced imitations — a distinction commercially inconvenient for most of what is sold under that name within a short walk of the museum.
