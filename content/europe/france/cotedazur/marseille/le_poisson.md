Le Poisson — The Fish — stands at the southern edge of Marseille, where the city dissolves into limestone and the Calanques begin. The coordinates place it near the small fishing hamlets of Les Goudes or Callelongue, communities that survived at the very end of the coastal road for centuries by pulling rascasse, rouget and Saint-Pierre from these clear waters. A fish sculpture here is not decorative whimsy: it names the thing that made this shore worth settling.

Fish are inseparable from Marseille's identity at every level. The city's founding myth involves Greek traders from Phocaea choosing this particular calanque as a harbour around 600 BC; the reason was partly the richness of the sea. Bouillabaisse — the saffron-scented fish stew that Marseille guards with territorial ferocity — was born from the practice of simmering the unsellable catch at the end of the market day. The Vieux-Port fish market, held every morning on the quai des Belges, has continued without interruption for generations and remains one of the few places in Europe where you can buy fish that was swimming a few hours before dawn.

Near a sculpture like Le Poisson, the context does much of the work. The clear water of the Calanques, protected since 2012 as a national park, laps at rocks that look much as they did when the first boats worked this coast. Looking out from here, with the city behind and the sea ahead, it is easy to understand why the fish has been this city's totem for more than two millennia.
