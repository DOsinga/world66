Le Polygone Étoilé occupies a repurposed industrial space close to the Joliette docks, in a part of Marseille that long predates the Euroméditerranée regeneration projects a short walk to the south. The venue has spent decades championing cinema and performance that sits outside normal distribution circuits — essay films, artists' video, work-in-progress screenings, and live events that blur the boundary between cinema and theatre. Its programming is rigorous and occasionally demanding, built for people who think of film as an art form rather than a leisure product.

The name, meaning "the star polygon," carries a quiet geometric provocation: a figure that crosses itself, simultaneously regular and unsettling. That describes the spirit of the place. Le Polygone Étoilé has kept alive the legacy of the Marseille filmmaker Marcel Hanoun (1929–2012), one of the most singular voices in French experimental cinema, who made spare, formally inventive films at his own pace across five decades. Hanoun treated cinema as a philosophical act, and the venue has inherited that temperament.

Getting here involves passing through working streetscapes that most visitors to Marseille never see, which gives the experience a slight quality of initiation. Screenings are typically followed by open conversation with filmmakers or curators, and the audience — students, artists, neighbourhood regulars — tends to stay and argue. There is a bar, and the arguments often continue there.
