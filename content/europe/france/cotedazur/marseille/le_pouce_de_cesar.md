Standing near the Place César Baldaccini in the 6th arrondissement, this oversized bronze thumb is one of the most recognisable public sculptures in Marseille. It is a cast of the hand of César Baldaccini (1921–1998), the city's own son, enlarged to a height of roughly two metres. The thumb exists in multiple editions and sizes — the largest stands twelve metres tall — and this Marseille version honours the artist in the city where he was born, in the working-class Belle de Mai neighbourhood, the child of Italian immigrants.

César is best known internationally for his three great series: the Compressions, in which he crushed cars, motorcycles, and consumer objects into dense rectangular blocks; the Expansions, where he poured polyurethane foam and let it spread into organic, lava-like forms; and the Empreintes, direct casts of human body parts enlarged to monumental scale. The Thumb belongs to this last series, begun in 1965. The image of his own thumb, inflated to the size of a pillar, became one of his most reproduced works — and lent its silhouette to the Trophée César, the French film industry's equivalent of the Oscar, which César designed and which has been awarded since 1976.

The sculpture invites you to think about the act of making itself — the thumb being the digit that separates human tool-use from almost every other creature. For César, it was also personal: a self-portrait in the most reductive form, stripped of everything except the most basic fact of the artist's physical presence. Place César Baldaccini, named in his honour, is itself a small monument to a Marseillais who became one of the most significant French sculptors of the twentieth century.
