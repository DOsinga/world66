The Cité de la Verdière is one of the large social housing estates built in the northern arrondissements of Marseille during the postwar decades of rapid urban growth. Like many of the grands ensembles constructed across France in the 1950s and 1960s, it housed tens of thousands of people, many of them immigrants or the children of immigrants, in a landscape of tower blocks and slab blocks whose ambitions for modern living quickly ran up against underinvestment and the economic shocks of the 1970s and 1980s. The estate's name has at times carried the weight of a stigma; the children who grew up here knew it.

Les Enfants de la Verdière — the Children of the Verdière — is a public work that insists on a different kind of naming. It places the residents, and specifically the younger generations who came of age in this place, at the centre of the story rather than the margins. Works like this are responses to the long habit of defining housing estates by their problems rather than their people: the children are not footnotes to urban policy debates but the subject, the point, the ones whose lives constitute what the estate actually is.

The piece stands in a neighbourhood that has seen sustained attempts at regeneration alongside persistent difficulties, and its presence is a small act of counter-narrative. Marseille has a long tradition of celebrating itself through its port and its cuisine and its football club while its northern arrondissements go unrepresented; a work that names the children of a specific cité in a specific neighbourhood is, in that context, a quiet political act.
