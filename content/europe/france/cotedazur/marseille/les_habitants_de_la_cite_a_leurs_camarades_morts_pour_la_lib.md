The plaque reads like a private promise made public: the residents of the cité, to their comrades who died for the liberation of France. Then four names — Colombani Dominique, Lambert Maurice, Mortier Christian, Torresani Louis. The formality of the full title gives way to those four names, which are the whole point. All four are surnames common in working-class Marseille: Corsican, Savoyard, Norman, Italian in origin — a small cross-section of the city's gathered populations who found themselves, in 1944, fighting in the same streets.

The liberation of Marseille took place in August 1944, faster and more violently than anyone had anticipated. Allied forces landed in Provence on 15 August; by the 28th the city was free, but the preceding days had seen intense urban combat as French units of the First Army and local Resistance fighters fought through the streets against a German garrison that had no intention of surrendering without a fight. The port installations had been blown up; the fighting was house by house in several neighbourhoods. The working-class cités of the northern and eastern arrondissements contributed large numbers of Resistance members and combatants, ordinary people who had been living under occupation for four years and chose to act when the moment came.

Plaques like this one are different in character from the official monuments that stand in city squares. This was put up by neighbours, not by the municipality: the residents of the cité themselves decided to name the dead and keep the names visible. Colombani, Lambert, Mortier, and Torresani are not famous. They are just four men from the estate who did not come home.
