Les Trois Passages — the Three Passages — takes its title from a structural motif that carries both physical and metaphorical weight. Three openings, three thresholds, three transitions: the number has long carried symbolic charge in architecture and narrative alike, and the work plays on that resonance without labouring it. The piece stands in a part of Marseille that is residential and largely unvisited by people from other parts of the city, let alone from outside it — the eastern arrondissements where the urban fabric is quieter and more domestic than the port-facing neighbourhoods to the west.

The notion of passage is apt for a city built on movement. Marseille has been a threshold city for most of its 2,600-year history: goods, people, and ideas arriving and departing, passing through rather than settling, or settling after a passage that began somewhere else entirely. The eastern neighbourhoods have their own version of this history — postwar housing built for families moving out of overcrowded central districts, later diversified by immigration from North Africa and the Comoros, communities that have made a life precisely through the act of crossing and settling.

Whether the three passages are literal archways, conceptual gates, or a formal pattern rendered in stone or metal, the work invites the walker to notice transitions that everyday movement makes invisible: the threshold between street and building, between neighbourhood and neighbourhood, between the city one knows and the city one has never been to. In a city of this size and this many populations, that is not a trivial subject.
