Louis Armand (1905–1971) was one of the great modernisers of twentieth-century France. Trained as a mining engineer, he came to prominence through the French Resistance and was appointed director-general of the SNCF in 1946. He inherited a railway network wrecked by wartime bombing and years of neglect and turned it, within a decade, into one of the most advanced in the world. His central achievement was the systematic electrification of the French main lines — a programme that made the SNCF cheaper to run, cleaner, and faster than almost any of its neighbours. Engineers from across Europe and beyond came to study the model he had built.

Armand was not merely a railway man. In 1957 he was appointed the first president of Euratom, the European Atomic Energy Community, carrying the same belief in rational, large-scale technological ambition into the wider European project. He was elected to the Académie française in 1963, a recognition that his influence extended well beyond the technical sphere. He had written and spoken widely on the relationship between technology, planning, and national renewal.

This memorial in Marseille honours the connection between Armand's legacy and the city's role as a major node in the French rail network. The Gare de Marseille-Saint-Charles, one of the great termini of the south, was among the lines modernised under his tenure. The plaque or sculpture here serves as a quiet reminder that the fast, electrified trains arriving daily in Marseille from Paris and Lyon are, in large part, the world Armand made.
