Louis Braille lost his sight at three, in an accident in his father's workshop near Paris. He ended up at the Royal Institution for Blind Youth, where he heard about a military system — "night writing" — developed by Charles Barbier so soldiers could read coded messages in the dark without striking a light. Braille saw the potential immediately. Between 1821 and 1824, aged roughly twelve to fifteen, he refined it into the elegant six-dot cell that carries his name.

France did not officially adopt his system until 1854 — two years after he died. The institution where he had worked and taught resisted it for decades, preferring embossed Roman letters that were far harder to read by touch. Once recognition arrived it spread fast: within a generation, Braille had been adapted for Arabic, Japanese, and dozens of other scripts.

He gave blind people not just a tool but independence — the ability to read privately, to write, to access knowledge without an intermediary. Not bad for a teenager with a stylus.
