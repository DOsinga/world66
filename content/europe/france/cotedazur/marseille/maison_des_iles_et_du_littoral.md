Marseille is inseparable from its sea, and this centre — the Maison des Îles et du Littoral — exists to make that relationship visible and legible. The city faces an arc of islands and inlets that most visitors only glimpse from the Vieux-Port: the Frioul archipelago, made up of Pomègues and Ratonneau, sits about three kilometres offshore; the island of If, with its famous château, is closer still; further south, the spectacular limestone Calanques stretch along the coast towards Cassis, their turquoise coves accessible only on foot, by kayak, or by boat. The centre provides information, maps, and programming about all of it — a single point of orientation for one of the most complex and beautiful coastlines in France.

The islands carry layered histories. Château d'If was built as a royal fortress in the sixteenth century and served for nearly three hundred years as a state prison — its most famous inmate being fictional, the Count of Monte Cristo of Alexandre Dumas's novel, though the cell is pointed out with conviction. The Frioul islands were used as a plague quarantine station during the epidemic of 1720, the same outbreak that devastated Marseille itself and killed nearly half the city's population. Today Pomègues and Ratonneau are linked by a causeway and home to a small residential community, a sailing harbour, and landscapes that feel improbably wild for somewhere so close to a major city.

The Calanques became a national park in 2012, the first in France to combine both land and sea in a single protected area. The Maison des Îles et du Littoral serves as a gateway to understanding that park — its ecology, its geology (the white limestone cliffs formed over millions of years), and the pressures it faces from overuse in summer. Whether you are planning a boat trip to If, a calanque hike, or simply want to understand the geography of the coast you can see from every hilltop in Marseille, this is the place to start.
