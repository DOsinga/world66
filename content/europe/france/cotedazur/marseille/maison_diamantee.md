The Maison Diamantée takes its name from the pointed stone bosses — rustication cut into diamond or pyramidal shapes — that cover its entire street facade. The technique, called bossage en pointe de diamant in French, arrived in France from northern Italy during the Renaissance, where it had been applied to prestigious urban palaces in Ferrara, Milan, and elsewhere as a way of giving a building's surface texture, depth, and a quality of aggressive richness that plain ashlar could not achieve. In Marseille the technique appears on exactly one building, this one, brought here in the 16th century by Italian craftsmen active in the city during a period of close commercial exchange with Genoa and Florence.

The house was built around 1570, probably for a prosperous merchant family in the quarter that is now Le Panier — the oldest continuously inhabited neighbourhood in Marseille, built on the slopes above the Vieux-Port. The facade facing the street reads as a demonstration piece: two storeys of diamond-cut stone above a ground floor, the bosses projecting far enough from the wall surface to cast deep shadows in raking afternoon light, turning the whole elevation into a field of geometric pattern. The effect is more military than domestic in feeling, which was probably the point — rustication of this kind signalled wealth and solidity, a building that would not crumble.

The building now houses the Musée du Vieux Marseille, a small museum devoted to the city's urban history, with collections covering the growth of the city from its Greek founding through the 19th century. The collection includes maps, prints, santons (the painted terracotta figures used in Provençal Christmas crèches), and objects from daily life in the old port city. The museum is worth entering, but even visitors who skip it should stop outside long enough to look closely at the stonework — the precision of each individual boss, and the way the entire surface moves with light.
