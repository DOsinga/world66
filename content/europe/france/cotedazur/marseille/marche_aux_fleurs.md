The Marché aux fleurs occupies a stretch of the Cours Belsunce, the broad pedestrian boulevard that cuts through the city's most commercially dense quarter. Cut flowers arrive before dawn and are arranged by the time the morning commuters pass: roses, gladioli, potted geraniums, seasonal arrangements. The stall-holders have worked this spot, or their families have, for generations — the market's longevity is not picturesque accident but commercial persistence.

The surrounding streets tell a different story from the one Marseille often tells about itself. The Cours Belsunce has been remade repeatedly — Haussmann-era ambitions, postwar reconstruction, recent pedestrianisation — and what fills it now is North African clothing shops, halal butchers, discount household goods, mobile phone unlocking. The flower stalls occupy an older commercial layer that survived all the transformations. Standing among the buckets of carnations while the discount-goods shops open their shutters around you is to see the market as sediment: the city built up and rebuilt around something that simply kept going.

Closed Mondays. The market runs roughly 8am to early afternoon on other days, winding down as stock sells. Come in the morning for the widest selection; come later if you want to negotiate on bunches that won't last another day.
