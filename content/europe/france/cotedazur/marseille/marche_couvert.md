Covered markets — halles — occupy a particular role in French urban life that the supermarket has never quite displaced. At their best they function as a social as well as a commercial institution: a fixed meeting point, a place where the same vendors appear on the same days each week, and where the quality of produce depends on the vendor's personal stake in returning customers rather than a supply chain optimised for shelf life. This marché couvert in the north of Marseille belongs to that tradition, serving a residential neighbourhood with morning stalls of whatever is in season.

The stock follows the rhythms of Provençal supply: early summer brings courgettes with their flowers still attached, melons from the Lubéron, and the first Camargue tomatoes; autumn adds wild mushrooms and new-season olive oil. The fish counter — a fixture in any serious Marseille market — carries rouget, sea bream, and whatever came off the boats at La Joliette or the fish auction at Saumaty, with bouillabaisse offcuts for those who still make the stew at home. Olives, tapenade, and herbes de Provence come from Provençal producers with retail stalls.

Markets like this one are where Marseille's domestic economy still operates in analogue form. The neighbourhood is not a tourist destination, and the market reflects that: prices are local, the language is French or Arabic or Comorian depending on the stall, and nobody is performing Provence for visitors. Come in the morning; most activity is done by noon.
