The Marché d'Intérêt National des Arnavaux operates while the city sleeps. The first trucks arrive around midnight at this vast warehouse complex in northern Marseille; by 2am, when the market formally opens, the loading bays are full, the refrigerated lorries from Spain and the Maghreb are unloading, and the wholesalers' stands are stacked with pallets of peppers, crates of tomatoes, boxes of fish transferred from the overnight auction at the port. By 7am, when the gates close to buyers, the produce has dispersed: into restaurant kitchens, into the city's retail markets, into the supply chains of supermarkets across Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur.

The MIN system — Marché d'Intérêt National, a network of regulated wholesale markets — was created under De Gaulle in 1953 to rationalise food distribution across France. The idea was to concentrate the chaotic postwar trade in a structured setting where prices would be set in open competition and hygiene could be regulated. Arnavaux is one of the original MIN nodes; it serves the entire southern region, making it one of the largest food distribution points between Paris and the Italian border. Hundreds of professional buyers hold accredited access; the scale of the operation, seen from outside the gates at 3am, reads as industrial rather than commercial.

Visitors cannot enter without trade credentials, but the market is part of the infrastructure that explains why Marseille's restaurants source well and why the city's outdoor markets can still sell fish landed that morning. The night hours, the warehouse scale, the regional catchment — it is the city's provisioning system made briefly visible before dispersing into the ordinary morning.
