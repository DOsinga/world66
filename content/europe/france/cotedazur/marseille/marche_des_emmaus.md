The Marché des Emmaüs in Marseille is part of a network of solidarity markets operated by Emmaüs France — a movement with roots that go back to 1949 and one of the most recognisable acts of social entrepreneurship in modern French history. The model is simple and effective: people donate unwanted possessions, "companions" (as Emmaüs calls its resident workers, many of them formerly homeless) sort, repair, and price the goods, and the public buys them at an open market. The proceeds fund the communities themselves and their charitable work. The Marseille sites follow this formula, and the market draws a faithful crowd of bargain hunters, charity-minded regulars, and people on tight budgets for whom it provides genuinely useful goods at genuinely low prices.

Abbé Pierre — born Henri Grouès in Lyon in 1912 — founded the movement almost by accident. He had been a Capuchin friar, then a resistance fighter, then a member of the Assemblée Nationale, and by the early 1950s had turned an old house outside Paris into a shelter for the destitute. When a desperate man who had attempted suicide came to him in 1949, Abbé Pierre persuaded him to stay and help others instead of ending his life. That decision became the founding ethic of Emmaüs: those with nothing to lose help those with even less. The name comes from the biblical village of Emmaus, where two disciples walked with the risen Christ without recognising him — a metaphor, for Abbé Pierre, of the hidden dignity in every marginalised person. By the time of the brutal winter of 1954, Abbé Pierre had become a national figure, launching a radio appeal so charged with urgency that it moved France to flood emergency shelters with donations overnight.

Today Emmaüs is an international federation active in dozens of countries, and its French communities remain anchored in the practical solidarity Abbé Pierre insisted on: not charity handed downward, but work done together. The Marseille market is one expression of that ethic — a place where the city's characteristic mixture of poverty and resourcefulness meets in the most ordinary of commercial forms, the second-hand stall.
