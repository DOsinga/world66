Provence has always understood that its regional identity could be made edible. The Marché Provençal trades in exactly that understanding: stalls stacked with the ingredients and objects that together constitute a shorthand for the good life of the Midi. Herbes de Provence in dried bundles or sealed in small ceramic pots; tapenade in a dozen variations from the classic black olive blend to versions with green olives, anchovies, or sun-dried tomatoes; pastis in miniature bottles alongside the pastis-flavoured biscuits that taste of anise and summer.

Santons — the small fired-clay figurines of Provençal nativity tradition — appear here even outside the Christmas market season. The oldest santon-makers in Marseille date to the post-Revolutionary period, when public crèches were banned and families built their own at home. The figures expanded over the generations to include every character of village life: the knife-grinder, the fishwife, the drunk sleeping under a wall. Today the best santons are considered folk art; the market carries examples across the full range from souvenir to heirloom.

Lavender products occupy an entire register of the market: sachets, pillows, honey from bees worked among lavender fields in the Luberon and Verdon, soaps produced in Marseille itself under the savon de Marseille designation that protects the traditional 72 percent olive oil formula. The market is tourist-facing in a way that some of Marseille's other markets are not, but the quality of the produce is real, and the vendors who have held their spots for years know their suppliers well enough to answer any question you put to them.
