Boulevard de Sébastopol in Marseille's 4th arrondissement sits in a part of the city that tourism maps tend to skip: inland from the Vieux-Port, east of the Cours Julien, a neighbourhood of apartment blocks, small businesses, and the ordinary commercial infrastructure of a residential quarter. The Marché Sébastopol is this neighbourhood's weekly provisioning point — a street market of the straightforward kind, where stalls of produce, poultry, and dry goods set up in the morning and are gone before midday.

The market operates on the French model of fixed weekly days rather than daily trading. Stallholders are typically small producers or wholesale buyers: vegetable growers from the Aix plain, fishmongers taking produce from the early-morning Marseille auctions, butchers, cheese traders. The 4th arrondissement has a mixed population with significant North African and Armenian communities — a reflection of Marseille's successive immigration waves — and the market stock reflects this, with spice vendors and North African pastry stalls alongside the Provençal staples.

What these neighbourhood markets do that supermarkets do not is maintain the social function of a fixed meeting point. Regular customers know their vendors; vendors know their customers' preferences and will keep a piece of something back. This is not sentiment — it is how provisioning worked before the supermarket, and in Marseille, where the open-air market culture is genuinely tenacious, it is how a significant part of the city still shops. The Sébastopol market has no heritage designation and makes no claim to the visitor's attention, which is part of what makes it worth visiting if you happen to be in the quarter on the right morning.
