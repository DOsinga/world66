The Institut Géographique National and its predecessors spent most of the nineteenth century building a national levelling network, which required a fixed reference altitude from which all heights could be calculated. The Mediterranean was chosen over the Atlantic for this zero point because tidal variation here is far smaller — typically under 30 centimetres compared to several metres on the Atlantic coast — making it a more stable baseline. The mean sea level at Endoume, calculated from continuous tide-gauge readings from 1884 onwards, was formally established as France's official altitudinal datum in 1897.

The consequence is that every elevation figure in France traces back to this building. Mont Blanc's 4,808 metres is 4,808 metres above the mean Mediterranean level recorded at the Marégraphe. The same datum was used for the first precision levelling of the Paris Métro, for calculating canal levels across the country, and for setting the reference heights in every French city and department. A brass plate on the building marks the exact reference point.

The station continues to function as a tide gauge and scientific monitoring post, and has acquired additional importance as sea levels have become a subject of active measurement. Because the reference datum has been consistent since 1884, the Marégraphe provides one of the longest continuous sea-level records in the Mediterranean — more than 140 years of data against which current measurements can be compared. The station is not generally open to the public but can be viewed from outside the perimeter at the Endoume promontory.
