Operation Dragoon began on 15 August 1944 when Allied forces — primarily American divisions alongside the French Army B — landed on the beaches of Provence between Toulon and Cannes. The operation was the long-delayed southern complement to the Normandy landings and moved with surprising speed: German forces in the south were stretched thin, resistance networks had prepared the ground, and the advancing troops met less sustained resistance than on the Atlantic coast. Marseille was liberated on 28 August 1944, two days after Paris — the two events so close together that for a brief moment the whole arc of France's liberation seemed to be resolving simultaneously.

American forces played a central role in Dragoon, providing the amphibious lift, much of the air support, and several of the assault divisions that came ashore on the first day. As the campaign pushed north up the Rhône valley, French and American units fought in close coordination, a partnership that had its tensions but produced rapid results. The liberation of Marseille itself was carried out primarily by French troops — General de Monsabert's forces fought street by street through the city while German garrison units attempted to hold out — but American logistical and combat support made the broader Provençal campaign possible.

This memorial registers that American contribution in a city that, by the time liberation came, had lived through four years of occupation, collaboration, deportations, and resistance. Visiting it alongside Marseille's other war memorials allows something like a complete account of what those years meant — from the dark mathematics of the deportation lists to the chaotic joy of a city freed.
