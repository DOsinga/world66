In the spring of 1792, France declared war on Austria and began calling for volunteer battalions. The fédérés of Marseille — a group of revolutionary volunteers — left the city in July, marching north to join the defence of the republic. They carried with them a marching song that had been composed earlier that year in Strasbourg by Claude Joseph Rouget de Lisle, officially titled the "Chant de guerre pour l'armée du Rhin." The Marseille volunteers sang it relentlessly on their march to Paris, and when they arrived and the song spread through the capital, it was immediately identified with them. Within months it was known as La Marseillaise.

The paradox is complete: France's national anthem is named after a city where it was not written, composed by a man who was not from there, and popularised by volunteers who adopted it rather than created it. The song itself had a difficult life in the centuries that followed — banned under Napoleon, restored, banned again under the Restoration, contested during the Paris Commune, and gradually rehabilitated until it became the official anthem of the Third Republic in 1879. By then the legend of the Marseille volunteers had become inseparable from the song's identity.

The memorial marks the departure point in Marseille for those 1792 volunteers — or at least commemorates the historical moment they represent. The connection gives the city a stake in the anthem's story that is sentimental rather than authorial: Marseille did not make the song but it gave the song its name, and that associative claim has been central to local civic identity ever since. The volunteers themselves were marching into a war that would kill many of them; the anthem outlasted all of them by centuries.
