In July 1792, a group of volunteer soldiers left Marseille marching north to defend the republic. They carried a song composed months earlier in Strasbourg by Claude Joseph Rouget de Lisle, written for the Army of the Rhine, with no connection to Marseille whatsoever. The volunteers sang it relentlessly all the way to Paris. When they arrived, the capital heard it — and immediately named it after them.

The paradox is complete. France's national anthem was written in Strasbourg, by an Alsatian, for a different army. It is called La Marseillaise because of 500 men on a long march who liked the tune. The song then had a difficult couple of centuries — banned under Napoleon, restored, banned again under the Restoration, finally made the official anthem of the Third Republic in 1879.

This memorial marks the departure point of those 1792 volunteers — or at least commemorates the moment they represent. Many of them were killed in the war they were marching to fight. The anthem outlasted all of them by centuries, and somewhere along the way, the city stopped needing to explain how it got the name.
