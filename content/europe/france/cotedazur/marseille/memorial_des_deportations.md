In January 1943, SS-Obergruppenführer Carl Oberg ordered a roundup of the old port district. French police carried it out. In 48 hours, approximately 25,000 residents were expelled from their homes. Le Panier — one of the oldest continuously inhabited neighbourhoods in France, its medieval fabric intact for centuries — was then dynamited on Hitler's personal order. Around 2,000 people, primarily Jewish, were deported. Most did not return.

Marseille's position as France's largest southern port made it a major hub in the deportation network. Jews, political prisoners, and resistance members were concentrated here before transport north to Drancy, and from there east to Auschwitz. The proximity to the Italian occupation zone had offered some protection earlier; when Germany took full control in late 1942, the arrests accelerated sharply.

This memorial inscribes the names. It does not let the roundup fold into a generalised account of wartime suffering. It also invites a harder reckoning: French police filled the deportation quotas, prefects signed the orders, institutions that later preferred to forget. The memorial is specific about all of this.
