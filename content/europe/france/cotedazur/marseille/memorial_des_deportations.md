Marseille's location as France's largest southern port made it a critical hub in the Nazi deportation network during the occupation years of 1942 to 1944. Jews, political prisoners, and resistance members were concentrated here before being transported north to the transit camps at Drancy, and from there to Auschwitz and other extermination camps in the east. The city's proximity to the Italian occupation zone in the southeast initially offered some protection; when the Germans took full control in late 1942, the pace of arrests accelerated sharply.

The defining event of Marseille's wartime memory is the Le Panier operation of January 1943. In a 48-hour roundup ordered by SS-Obergruppenführer Carl Oberg and carried out with the cooperation of French police, approximately 25,000 residents of the old port district were expelled. The ancient neighbourhood of Le Panier — one of the oldest continuously inhabited urban quarters in France — was then dynamited on Hitler's personal order, erasing the medieval fabric along with its population. Around 2,000 people, primarily Jewish, were deported; most did not return.

This memorial inscribes that history into the city's landscape, naming those who were taken and ensuring the roundup cannot be folded into a generalised account of wartime suffering. It sits alongside a broader Marseillais reckoning with what collaboration meant at the local level — French police filling deportation quotas, prefects signing orders, institutions that later preferred to forget. Visiting it invites that reckoning.
