"Merveilleuse Cité" lifts a phrase that Marseillais have long used to describe their own city with a mixture of pride and irony: marvellous not in the sense of polished perfection, but in the older, stranger sense of something that astonishes, that defies easy categorisation, that refuses to be anything other than itself. The phrase appears here as a public artwork — a text-based installation or mural in the Belsunce district, placed where it can be read against the layered, chaotic, genuinely remarkable backdrop of central Marseille.

Belsunce, the neighbourhood immediately north of the Vieux-Port, has been a place of arrival for migrants for generations: Armenians, Comorians, Algerians, sub-Saharan Africans, and more recently a wave of artists and young professionals drawn by low rents and central geography. The juxtaposition of "merveilleuse" against this dense, workaday streetscape is deliberate. The work does not prettify or romanticise; it names something that is already there, if you are willing to look past the surface disorder and see the human energy beneath.

The phrase "cité" carries its own charge in French: it means city in the classical sense, but in contemporary usage it most often refers to a housing estate, a cité HLM, a place of concentrated social need. "Merveilleuse Cité" plays on both registers — claiming the nobility of urban life for spaces that official culture has sometimes written off — and in doing so it distils something essential about Marseille's long, proud, argumentative relationship with itself.
