Manouchian survived the Armenian genocide as an orphaned child, came to France as a refugee in 1924, and became a poet, Communist Party member, and factory worker in Paris. During the German occupation he commanded the FTP-MOI — Francs-Tireurs et Partisans, section Main-d'œuvre immigrée — a network assembled almost entirely from immigrants and foreign Jews: Poles, Romanians, Hungarians, Armenians, Italians, Spaniards. Between 1943 and early 1944 the group carried out more than 30 armed actions in Paris, targeting German officers and collaborationist officials.

Arrested in November 1943 through informants and German surveillance, Manouchian and 21 of his group were tried by a German military tribunal and shot at Mont Valérien on 21 February 1944. The Wehrmacht plastered the "Affiche Rouge" — the Red Poster — across Paris: photographs of ten of the fighters, their foreign names, their alleged crimes. The intent was to discredit the resistance as foreign criminality. The effect was the opposite. The poster became one of the most recognisable documents of the occupation; the names became celebrated. Louis Aragon's 1956 poem "L'Affiche Rouge" and Léo Ferré's setting of it made Manouchian permanently famous in France.

In February 2024, Manouchian and his wife Mélinée were entered into the Panthéon — the first stateless person to receive the honour. Marseille had already given his name to a garden and a street near the Vieux-Port. His biography compresses the twentieth century's worst events: genocide, exile, fascism, resistance, propaganda, and posthumous recognition — into a single life that ended at 37.
