When Le Corbusier completed the Unité d'Habitation on the Boulevard Michelet in 1952, he embedded his proportional system directly into the building's concrete skin. The Modulor — a figure derived from the golden ratio and calibrated to the height of a man with one arm raised (notionally 2.26 metres, based on a 1.83-metre standing height) — was stamped into the rough béton brut of the ground-floor pillars as both a design statement and a kind of manufacturer's mark. The imprint is still there: a stylised human silhouette with an outstretched arm, flanked by two scales of measurement that promise architecture tuned to the human body.

Le Corbusier had published the Modulor system in 1948, arguing that modern construction had lost the intuitive proportional sense that governed classical and medieval building. His answer was a mathematical sequence — a kind of golden-section slide rule — that he claimed would make buildings inherently harmonious because they derived from the same ratio system as the human form. The Cité Radieuse was his proving ground: 337 duplex apartments, a rooftop terrace with a running track and paddling pool, internal streets lined with shops, a hotel, and a nursery. It was a vertical city, and the Modulor was its grammar.

The building is now classified as a UNESCO World Heritage Site and is still fully residential, though tours of the rooftop and the internal street are available. The Modulor imprint near the entrance is easy to overlook if you do not know to look for it — which is precisely the point. Le Corbusier did not add it for visitors; he added it because he believed the proportional system was simply true, and that its presence in the concrete, even unread, grounded the building in something larger than fashion.
