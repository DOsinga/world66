Môle I is one of the numbered and lettered jetties that structure Marseille's northern industrial port basin, the Grand Port Maritime de Marseille. Unlike the Vieux-Port, which is the ancient fishing and pleasure harbour in the city centre, this zone is working infrastructure: vast concrete fingers pushing out into the Gulf of Lion to shelter berths for container ships, ro-ro ferries, tankers and bulk carriers. Marseille is France's largest port by tonnage, and the moles are its backbone.

The naming convention — moles identified by letters and numbers — reflects the systematic expansion of port capacity over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Each new arm added sheltered water and more quayside length. Môle I, positioned in the northern port complex, handles the mundane but essential business of a major Mediterranean hub: the loading and unloading of freight bound for North Africa, the Middle East and beyond. The coordinates place it well north of the city centre, in an area largely inaccessible to the public.

For visitors, the moles are most impressive seen from a distance — from the elevated viewpoint of Notre-Dame de la Garde, the cranes, silos and ship superstructures of the port stretch across the northern horizon as a reminder that beneath Marseille's tourist-facing face lies one of Europe's great working harbours.
