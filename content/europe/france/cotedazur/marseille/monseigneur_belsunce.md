In the summer of 1720, a merchant ship called the Grand Saint Antoine docked at Marseille carrying plague bacilli from the eastern Mediterranean. The disease spread with terrible speed through a city of around 90,000 people. Within two years approximately 50,000 Marseillais were dead — half the population. The city's magistrates and much of its bourgeoisie fled to their country estates. Henri de Belsunce, Bishop of Marseille since 1709, did not. He remained in the streets, administering last rites, organising relief, and walking openly among the sick when most of the population was too terrified to leave their houses. His example was reported across Catholic Europe and made him, in the eyes of contemporaries, a figure of almost saintly courage.

Belsunce was born in 1670 into the minor Gascon nobility and trained with the Jesuits before entering the secular clergy. He was a man of conservative, even stern, religious convictions — he spent much of his episcopate opposing Jansenism and remained loyal to orthodox Catholic doctrine throughout his life. But his conduct during the plague transcended theological controversy. He used his own fortune for relief efforts, negotiated with the crown for supplies, and repeatedly placed himself in physical danger. When he finally died, in 1755, he was mourned as the defining moral figure of the worst catastrophe in the city's modern history.

The neighbourhood of Belsunce, now one of the most diverse and bustling quarters of central Marseille, carries his name — a living commemoration more lasting than any plaque. This memorial adds a more formal point of remembrance near the area he served. The 1720 plague shaped Marseille deeply: it led to the construction of the plague wall (mur de la peste) across Provence, reorganised the city's public health institutions, and left a collective memory that lasted for generations. Belsunce is at the centre of how Marseille chose to remember that it survived.
