In summer 1720, the merchant ship Grand Saint Antoine docked at Marseille carrying plague from the eastern Mediterranean. Within two years, around 50,000 people were dead — half the city's population. Officials and wealthy residents fled to their country estates. Bishop Henri de Belsunce stayed.

He walked the streets, administered last rites, organised relief, and spent his own fortune on supplies. He was repeatedly in physical danger. Word spread across Catholic Europe. Belsunce was already a stern, conservative cleric — an anti-Jansenist who brooked little theological argument. The plague made him a different kind of figure entirely.

He died in 1755, having outlived the catastrophe by thirty-five years. The neighbourhood north of the Vieux-Port carries his name — one of the most diverse and bustling quarters of central Marseille today. More durable than any statue. This memorial is a formal addition to what the city has already named after him.
