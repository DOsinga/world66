Marseille's main war memorial stands as the city's most solemn civic monument, carrying the inscribed names of more than two thousand Marseillais killed during the First World War. The city sent soldiers in disproportionate numbers to theatres far beyond the Western Front — the Dardanelles, Salonika, and the broader Eastern Mediterranean — reflecting Marseille's role as France's great southern port and gateway to its colonial empire. Many of the dead came from colonial army units, conscripted from Algeria, Senegal, and Indochina and shipped out through Marseille's own docks.

The memorial is the fixed point of Marseille's 11 November Armistice Day ceremonies, when city officials, veterans' associations, and schoolchildren gather to lay wreaths at its base. The ritual follows the same pattern as in every French commune: a reading of the names, the last post, and a minute's silence. At a memorial of this scale, the recitation of names alone becomes a kind of accounting of grief — families scattered across every arrondissement of the city, each with a name cut into stone.

Standing here, the sheer range of surnames makes the cosmopolitan character of pre-war Marseille legible: Italian, Greek, Spanish, North African, and Provençal names run together in alphabetical order, stripped of rank or origin. The monument is not a place for military pageantry but for reckoning — a city counting what it lost.
