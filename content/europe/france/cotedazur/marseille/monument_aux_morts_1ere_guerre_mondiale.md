No other French city had quite the same relationship with the First World War. Marseille was the embarkation point — hundreds of thousands of colonial troops crossed its docks on the way to a European front. Tirailleurs sénégalais, Malagasy infantrymen, Annamite labourers — they all passed through here. Many never came back this way.

At the same time, the city's own men were drafted north to Verdun, the Somme, and the Chemin des Dames. The southern arrondissements, stretching toward the calanques and the old village cores in the hills, had nothing to shield them from the draft. The names on this stone are a cross-section of a neighbourhood's generation.

The memorial does one specific thing: it plants names in a place. In a city as port-oriented and transient as Marseille, that act of anchoring — insisting these particular men belonged to this particular street — matters more than it might anywhere else.
