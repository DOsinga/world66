Marseille's relationship with the First World War was unlike that of any other French city. As the principal port linking metropolitan France with North Africa, West Africa, Madagascar, and Indochina, it was the point of embarkation and arrival for hundreds of thousands of colonial troops — tirailleurs sénégalais, Malagasy infantrymen, Annamite workers — who crossed its quays on the way to a European front most of them could barely have imagined. At the same time, the city's own sons were conscripted into the French army and sent north to Verdun, the Somme, and the Chemin des Dames. The war touched Marseille from every direction.

This memorial, set in one of the city's southern neighbourhoods, commemorates the local dead of the Great War. The southern arrondissements stretch from the old village cores around the hills toward the calanques and the sea; they were less industrialised than the northern port districts but no less exposed to the draft. The 1.4 million French soldiers killed between 1914 and 1918 came from every social stratum and every corner of the country, and the names carved here are a cross-section of a neighbourhood's generation. After the Armistice, a law passed in 1919 made it obligatory for every French commune to erect a memorial to its fallen; Marseille's sheer size meant that obligation was met at multiple scales simultaneously.

The monument is a quiet fixture of its neighbourhood, unremarkable in form but precise in its function: to hold names in place so they are not lost when the last people who knew them personally are also gone. In a city as port-oriented and transient as Marseille, that act of anchoring — of insisting that these particular men belonged to this particular place — carries extra significance.
