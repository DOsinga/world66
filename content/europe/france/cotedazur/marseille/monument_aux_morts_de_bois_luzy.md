Bois-Luzy sits in the eastern reaches of Marseille, in the 12th arrondissement, where the city loosens its grip and the land rises toward the limestone ridges behind the urban core. The neighbourhood takes its name from a grove of holm oaks — luzi in Provençal — that once shaded the grounds of the Château de Bois-Luzy, an eighteenth-century country house that now functions as a school. The area retains a slightly secluded character: wide plots, older villas, a pace that feels slower than the port districts to the west. It is the kind of place where local landmarks are few and familiar, and where a neighbourhood war memorial anchors community memory with particular force.

The monument was raised after 1919 under the national legislation that required every French commune to commemorate its dead from the 1914–18 war. For a neighbourhood like Bois-Luzy, with its relatively stable residential population, the list of names inscribed on the stone would have represented a perceptible fraction of the men of military age — brothers, fathers, sons recognised by everyone on the street. France lost approximately 1.4 million soldiers in that war, and the grief was not distributed evenly across an abstract national surface but concentrated in exactly these small communities, each of which bore a specific, countable set of losses.

The memorial stands as the neighbourhood's permanent roll-call of the absent. Coming across it amid the calm streets near the château grounds is a reminder that even the quietest corners of a large city were not spared.
