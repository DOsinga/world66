More than 600,000 Allied troops fought in Salonika, across northern Greece, Serbia, and Bulgaria. Almost no one in Western Europe remembers it. This monument, unveiled in 1927 on the Corniche Kennedy, is one of the very few prominent French memorials to that campaign — and it goes further, honouring soldiers who died in "terres lointaines": Madagascar, Morocco, the Levant, every colonial theatre of what was, in fact, a war fought on four continents.

Marseille's connection was direct. Throughout the war, ships left the Vieux-Port and the Joliette docks bound for Salonika, Egypt, and Palestine. The city also received the sick returning from Macedonia, where malaria killed far more troops than the fighting did.

Antoine Sartorio's sculpture faces the sea. That's deliberate — the memorial has a physical axis toward the theatres it commemorates. Stand here and the Mediterranean opens up in front of you, toward Macedonia and the Levant. The Salonika front is still largely absent from French school curricula. This is one of the few places in France that insists it happened.
