The Salonika campaign of 1915–1918 involved more than 600,000 Allied troops fighting across northern Greece, Serbia, and Bulgaria, yet it occupies almost no place in Western European First World War memory. The monument on the Corniche Kennedy, unveiled in 1927, is one of the few prominent French memorials to this theatre. Its inscription honours not only the Armée d'Orient but soldiers who died in "terres lointaines" — Madagascar, Morocco, the Levant, and other colonial theatres of a war that was, in fact, fought on four continents.

Marseille's connection to these campaigns was as the embarkation point. Throughout the war, soldiers bound for Salonika, Egypt, Palestine, and the Dardanelles left from the Vieux-Port and the Joliette docks. The city also received the sick returning from Salonika, where malaria killed and incapacitated far more troops than combat did. The monument was placed on the Corniche facing the sea, oriented toward the theatres it commemorates.

Antoine Sartorio's sculpture shows a standing soldier flanked by allegorical figures. The Corniche location commands a long view of the Mediterranean — an intentional choice that gave the memorial a physical axis toward the campaigns it marked. The Salonika front remains largely absent from French school curricula and official ceremonies; this monument is one of the few reminders in France that the war had a Mediterranean dimension.
