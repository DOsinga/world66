Most French war memorials are dated 1914–1918 or 1939–1945, each war given its own stone. This memorial takes a different approach, spanning 1914 to 1948 in a single inscription that folds both world wars and their immediate aftermath into one continuous wound. The choice of 1948 as the closing date is striking: it extends beyond the Liberation of 1944 and even beyond VE Day in 1945, perhaps to encompass the dead of the Indochina War that France was already fighting from 1946, or to acknowledge the slow deaths — from wounds, from captivity, from displacement — that continued long after the formal armistices.

The consolidated date range reflects a truth that French society sometimes found difficult to articulate separately: that for a generation born around 1895–1910, the wars were not two discrete events but a single long catastrophe with a twenty-year intermission. A man who was wounded at Verdun in 1916, survived the interwar years, and then lost a son at Dunkirk in 1940 did not experience two wars; he experienced one unbroken disaster. This monument names that experience.

The memorial stands in a northern neighbourhood of Marseille, far from the tourist circuits of the Vieux-Port. Its unusual inscription makes it worth the detour for anyone interested in how French communities chose to articulate their losses. There is something quietly radical about refusing to separate the two wars — an insistence that the dead belong together, whatever the history books say.
