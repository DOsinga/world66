Most French war memorials pick one war: 1914–1918 or 1939–1945. This one refuses to separate them. Its inscription spans 1914 to 1948 — covering both world wars in a single continuous wound, then extending three years past VE Day. That closing date of 1948 probably reaches forward into the Indochina War, already underway from 1946, or acknowledges the slow deaths from wounds and captivity that outlasted the armistices.

The consolidated dates name something French society struggled to say cleanly: for a generation born around 1895–1910, the two wars were not separate events. They were one long catastrophe with a twenty-year intermission. A man who survived Verdun and then lost a son at Dunkirk didn't live through two wars. He lived through one unbroken disaster.

This memorial sits in a northern neighbourhood of Marseille, away from the tourist circuits. Its unusual inscription is worth the detour. There is something quietly radical about refusing to separate what happened — insisting that the dead belong together, whatever the history books say.
