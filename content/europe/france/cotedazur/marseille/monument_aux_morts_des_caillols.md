Les Caillols takes its name from the Provençal word for the limestone cobbles that litter these hillsides at the eastern edge of Marseille. It's a fitting name for a neighbourhood that sits at the city's rocky margin, where the 12th arrondissement meets the garrigue and the land turns pale and hard underfoot. Until the twentieth century this was farmland. The memorial here dates from the early 1920s, planted among streets that were themselves still being built.

France's 1919 law mandated memorials, but in many places the communities built them before any official requirement. Local subscriptions paid for stone that would carry the names of men who had walked the same lanes as their surviving neighbours — men who knew this specific pale ridgeline, these specific stony paths.

The monument is low-key by design, in keeping with the neighbourhood's character. Standing here connects you to the very local version of grief that was France's condition in the 1920s: not the national abstract, but these particular names, in this particular place.
