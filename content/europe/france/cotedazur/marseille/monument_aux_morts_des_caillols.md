The name Les Caillols means roughly "the pebbles" or "the stones," from the Provençal word for the limestone cobbles that litter the hillsides here at the eastern edge of Marseille. It is a fitting name for a neighbourhood that sits at the city's rocky margin, where the 12th arrondissement meets the garrigue and the land turns pale and hard underfoot. The area was until the twentieth century largely agricultural, its small farms and orchards gradually replaced by residential streets as Marseille expanded inland. It remains a neighbourhood of modest houses and low-rise apartment buildings, further from the tourist circuits of the Vieux-Port than it is from the open countryside.

The war memorial at Les Caillols was built in the early 1920s, part of the enormous national effort to commemorate the dead of 1914–18. France had lost some 1.4 million soldiers in four years of industrial warfare — a catastrophe that left virtually no commune without its list of the fallen. The law of 1919 mandated memorials, but in many places the impulse came from the communities themselves, who raised money by subscription to build something permanent and local rather than wait for an official monument in a distant square. A neighbourhood memorial like this one, planted among ordinary streets, made the abstraction of national sacrifice concrete and immediate.

The monument at Les Caillols is low-key by design — a stone tablet or stele in keeping with the neighbourhood's unpretentious character. The names it carries belong to men who walked the same stony lanes as their surviving neighbours, who looked up at the same pale ridgeline. Standing here connects you to that very specific, very local version of grief that was France's condition in the years after the war.
