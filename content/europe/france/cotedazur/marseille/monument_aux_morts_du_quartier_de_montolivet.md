Montolivet occupies the eastern slopes of Marseille's 10th arrondissement, a relatively calm residential district of tree-lined streets and mid-century apartment blocks that rises above the autoroute corridor separating central Marseille from its eastern neighbourhoods. It is not a district that draws visitors, but it has the settled, self-sufficient character of a genuine Marseillais quarter: local markets, school runs, and a strong sense of neighbourhood identity that survived the rapid urban growth of the post-war decades.

Like many Marseille neighbourhoods, Montolivet erected its own war memorial to complement the city's grand official monument on the Corniche. Neighbourhood memorials of this kind — common across France's cities as well as its villages — were typically funded by local subscription and carried the names of men from the immediate streets who died in the First and Second World Wars. They served a social function beyond commemoration: they placed grief in a familiar, walkable geography, allowing families to mourn within sight of the houses where the dead had grown up.

The Montolivet memorial stands as an understated example of this tradition. Its stone and inscriptions follow the conventions established across France after 1918 — *Morts pour la France* and the roll of local names — without the grandeur of civic monuments intended for the whole city. For those exploring the quieter arrondissements of eastern Marseille, it is a small but telling marker of how the city's outer neighbourhoods carried their share of twentieth-century loss.
