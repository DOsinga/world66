Montolivet is not the kind of neighbourhood that makes it into guidebooks. It sits on the eastern slopes of Marseille's 10th arrondissement, above the autoroute corridor — tree-lined streets, mid-century apartment blocks, local markets, school runs. The kind of place that has a strong neighbourhood identity precisely because it was never dramatic enough to be famous.

That identity is part of why it has its own war memorial rather than just pointing people toward the grand monument on the Corniche. Neighbourhood memorials like this one were typically funded by local subscription and carried the names of men from the immediate streets — making grief walkable, placing loss within sight of the houses where the dead had grown up.

The stone follows the convention established across France after 1918: *Morts pour la France*, then the local names. What it doesn't have is pretension. For anyone exploring the quieter eastern arrondissements, it's a small but telling marker of how far the city's losses reached — into every ordinary quartier, every unremarkable street.
