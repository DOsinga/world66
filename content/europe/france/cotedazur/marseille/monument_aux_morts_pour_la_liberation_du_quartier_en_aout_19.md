The liberation of Marseille unfolded between 21 and 28 August 1944, just days after the Allied landings of Operation Dragoon on the Provençal coast. French forces under General de Lattre de Tassigny moved rapidly inland and on the city itself, but German resistance was fierce: the Wehrmacht held the Vieux-Port, Notre-Dame de la Garde and key coastal fortifications, and the fighting that drove them out was block-by-block, costly, and intimate. Hundreds of soldiers and civilians were killed in a week of combat that ended with a formal German surrender on 28 August.

This monument commemorates those from the immediate neighbourhood — residents, resistants, bystanders — who died in that specific fighting. Such hyper-local memorials are a distinctive feature of French wartime commemoration. Rather than gathering all losses into a single grand monument, many neighbourhoods across France erected their own plaques and stones, insisting that the deaths of ordinary people in ordinary streets deserved to be named and remembered where they fell.

The location, in the southern part of the city (the coordinates place it in the 9th or 13th arrondissement area), reflects that the liberation was not only a military event in the port and city centre but a city-wide upheaval. Finding this monument is itself a small act of remembrance — pausing in a residential street to read names that local history might otherwise have swallowed.
