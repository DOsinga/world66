The liberation of Marseille took eight days, 21–28 August 1944. French forces under de Lattre de Tassigny moved fast after the Operation Dragoon landings on the Provençal coast, but the Germans held the Vieux-Port, Notre-Dame de la Garde, and the coastal fortifications. The fighting to push them out was block by block. Hundreds of soldiers and civilians were killed before the formal German surrender on 28 August.

This monument names the people from this immediate neighbourhood — residents, resistants, bystanders — who died in that specific fighting. It's a distinctly French form of commemoration: rather than gathering all losses into one grand monument, communities across the country erected hyper-local plaques insisting that deaths on ordinary streets deserved to be named where they fell.

Finding it is itself a small act of remembrance. You're pausing in a residential street to read names that local history might otherwise have swallowed completely. The liberation wasn't only a military event at the port — it was a city-wide upheaval, and the dead were spread across the whole city.
