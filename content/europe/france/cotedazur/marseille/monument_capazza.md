Louis Capazza was born in Marseille in 1863 and died in Paris in 1933, but the feat that made him famous belongs to the sea between. On the night of 9–10 October 1897 he lifted off from Nice in a hydrogen balloon and flew south-east across the Mediterranean, navigating without instruments in darkness, and landed near Bastia on the northern tip of Corsica after thirteen hours in the air. It was the longest open-sea balloon crossing achieved at the time — a record that stood for years — and it made him briefly one of the most celebrated aeronauts in France.

The flight came at a moment when ballooning was still the leading edge of aviation and the French were its most serious practitioners. The aeronautical clubs, the long-distance competitions, the attempts on altitude and endurance records — this was the world Capazza moved in, years before powered flight made balloons seem quaint. His Corsica crossing required dead reckoning over water with no landmarks, in conditions where a slight error in heading would have left him adrift over open sea indefinitely. That he succeeded was a combination of skill, favourable winds, and what contemporaries called audacity.

The monument is positioned near the Pharo, with its view over the sea that Capazza crossed. It is one of several memorials in Marseille to figures connected not with the land but with movement — the port city's instinct to commemorate departure as much as arrival.
