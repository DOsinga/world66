In 1897 Louis Capazza lifted off from Nice in a hydrogen balloon and flew south across open water in the dark. No instruments. No landmarks. Thirteen hours later he landed near Bastia on the northern tip of Corsica — the longest open-sea balloon crossing anyone had made.

Dead reckoning over the Mediterranean, in the dark, with a slight error in heading meaning you drift indefinitely over open sea. He called it audacity. His contemporaries called him one of France's great aeronauts. This was years before powered flight made balloons look quaint.

The monument stands near the Pharo, looking out over the same water he crossed. A port city commemorating departure — that tracks.
