On 9 October 1934, seconds after King Alexander I of Yugoslavia stepped off a ship at Marseille's Joliette dock, a Macedonian gunman affiliated with the Ustasha movement broke through the crowd and shot him dead. French Foreign Minister Louis Barthou, who had come to receive the king, was also hit and died within hours. A Pathé newsreel camera happened to be filming the arrival — the resulting footage, showing the gunman lunging at the open car and the chaos that followed, was broadcast worldwide and is still recognisable today as one of the first pieces of political assassination caught on film.

The killing sent shockwaves through European diplomacy. Alexander had been attempting to hold together a fragile Kingdom of Yugoslavia against rising nationalist tensions; his death accelerated the drift toward the authoritarian regime that would follow. Barthou's death removed one of France's more determined advocates for a collective security agreement against Nazi Germany. The assassins had transited through Hungary and Italy with at least tacit official support — their operation was part of a broader Ustasha campaign backed by Mussolini's government — and the affair became an early test of the League of Nations' capacity to discipline member states, a test it largely failed.

The memorial on the Joliette quay marks one of those rare places where a single day's events can be traced directly to catastrophes that unfolded years later. The footage makes it viscerally present in a way that most historical markers cannot match; standing at the site with that film in mind compresses the distance between 1934 and the wars that followed.
