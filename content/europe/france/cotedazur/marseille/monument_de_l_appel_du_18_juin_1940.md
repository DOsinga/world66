On 18 June 1940, as France's military collapse became irreversible and the government of Marshal Pétain moved toward armistice, an obscure French general named Charles de Gaulle broadcast an appeal on BBC radio from London. Most people in France did not hear it that evening. But the text circulated, the idea spread, and what de Gaulle said that night — that France had lost a battle but had not lost the war, that the flame of resistance must not go out — became the founding declaration of the Free French movement. The date is now a national day of commemoration.

Marseille's claim on this memorial is partly symbolic and partly strategic. The city was, in 1940, one of the nerve centres of what remained of unoccupied France: a port through which refugees, Jewish families, resistance organisers, and Allied agents moved in both directions. The non-occupied zone gave Marseille a different wartime character from Paris — a city of transit, escape networks, and underground activity, alongside the collaboration and surveillance that marked Vichy France everywhere. Commemorating the Appel here anchors Marseille in the national resistance narrative and acknowledges the role southern France played in the long years before liberation.

The memorial is a place to think about the gap between the moment and its meaning — how a broadcast almost nobody heard in real time became the definitive act of French resistance, and how cities and individuals later oriented their wartime stories around it. Whether that retrospective framing simplifies a more complicated history is a question the memorial itself does not answer, but raises by existing.
