On 18 June 1940, as France's army collapsed and Pétain's government moved toward armistice, an obscure general named de Gaulle read an appeal on BBC radio from London. Almost nobody in France heard it that evening. The text circulated later. The idea — that France had lost a battle, not the war, that resistance must not go out — became the founding declaration of the Free French.

Marseille's claim on this memorial is real. In 1940 the city was a nerve centre of what remained of unoccupied France: a port where refugees, Jewish families, resistance organisers, and Allied agents moved in both directions. The underground networks — escape routes to Spain, forged documents, intelligence couriers — ran through here. Vichy surveillance ran through here too. The city held both at once.

The gap between the broadcast and its meaning is the thing to sit with. A message almost nobody heard in real time became the definitive act of French resistance. How cities and individuals later oriented their wartime stories around it is a complicated question this monument quietly raises.
