Marseille is home to the largest Armenian community in France — estimated at well over 100,000 people, descendants mostly of survivors who arrived in the years following the 1915 genocide perpetrated by the Ottoman Empire against the Armenian people. The first wave of refugees reached Marseille through the port in the early 1920s, and successive generations have remained, establishing churches, schools, cultural associations, and a visible presence across the city's northern and eastern arrondissements. Armenian identity is woven into Marseille's civic life to a degree unusual even by the standards of a famously diverse port city.

This monument commemorates that genocide — the systematic mass murder and deportation of the Armenian population of Anatolia, recognised by the French state and by the Republic as a crime against humanity. Marseille's Armenian community has long led French commemorations of April 24th, the annual day of remembrance, and the city was among the first French municipalities to take official positions on recognition. The monument, positioned in the central arrondissements of the city, is both a site of community grief and a public statement of historical recognition.

A note on the name: the phrase "génocide de Marseille" is occasionally used in a different context — referring to the January 1943 destruction of the neighbourhood north of the Vieux-Port, when German and French police forcibly evacuated around 20,000 residents and demolished some 1,500 buildings in what historians call one of the largest mass demolitions in Western Europe during the war. That event, however, is commemorated separately. This monument, at its coordinates in the central city, belongs to the Armenian memorial tradition that Marseille has maintained since the community first put down roots here more than a century ago.
