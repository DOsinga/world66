By the 1930s Marseille had one of the largest Italian immigrant populations in France. After 1922, a different type of Italian arrived: political refugees — anti-fascist organisers, Socialists, Communists, Jews fleeing Mussolini's 1938 racial laws. These people were already networked and politically experienced when Germany occupied the southern zone in November 1942. Their contribution to the resistance was substantial and disproportionate to their numbers.

Marseille's port made it a key node: forged documents, intelligence couriers, escape routes to Spain, fugitives moving in both directions. Italian-origin resistance members show up in the records of most major Marseille networks. French official commemoration rarely foregrounded this — the Gaullist narrative of a France that liberated itself had no room for immigrants, foreign Jews, and Communists.

The Italian community kept its own memory alive through newspapers, associations, and oral tradition. The joint Italian-French dedication of this monument is both historical and diplomatic — one of the few public sites in Marseille where this dimension of the wartime city is directly named.
