By the 1930s, Marseille had one of the largest Italian immigrant populations in France, built by decades of economic migration. After 1922, a different type of Italian arrived: political refugees — anti-fascist organisers, Socialists, Communists, and eventually Jews fleeing Mussolini's 1938 racial laws. These communities were already politically experienced and networked when Germany occupied the southern zone in November 1942. Their contribution to the resistance was substantial and disproportionate to their numbers: they fed into both the Communist FTP and the anarchist networks, and maintained connections to anti-fascist organisations in exile in London and New York.

Marseille's position as a port made it a significant node in the underground networks — a transit point for intelligence, couriers, forged documents, and fugitives moving between France, Spain, and Britain. Italian-origin resistance members appear in the records of most major Marseille networks. French official commemoration, which emphasised the native French and Gaullist character of the resistance, rarely foregrounded their presence. The Gaullist narrative of a France that liberated itself was politically incompatible with emphasising the role of immigrants, Communists, and foreign Jews.

The Italian community of Marseille preserved its own memory of this history through Italian-language newspapers, community associations, and oral tradition — sustaining what official commemoration omitted. The joint Italian-French dedication of the monument is both a historical and a diplomatic gesture, acknowledging the shared experience of fascism and resistance across the two countries. It is one of the few public sites in Marseille where this dimension of the city's wartime history is directly named.
