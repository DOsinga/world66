The cedar of Lebanon has carried symbolic weight for millennia — Solomon's Temple was built with it, Phoenician ships were planked with it, it appears in Gilgamesh. Today it's on the Lebanese flag. In Marseille's northern arrondissements, a mosque bearing the name *Les Cèdres* signals a Levantine community: Lebanese, Syrian, and other eastern Mediterranean Muslims who arrived through different routes than the larger Maghrebi population but settled in the same working-class northern districts.

Marseille absorbed waves of Mediterranean migrants across the 20th century. The northern arrondissements — the 13th, 14th, 15th — became home to families from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, sub-Saharan Africa, Lebanon, Turkey, and the Comoros. Two or three generations in, the mosque isn't just a place of worship — it runs Quranic classes, hosts weddings and funerals, keeps cultural ties alive.

The Mosquée des Cèdres sits in a low-rise residential district where Friday prayers draw people from the surrounding streets. On ordinary days it operates quietly. The name — Lebanon's mountain forests, carried forward in a northern Marseille suburb — is a small act of memory in stone and mortar.
