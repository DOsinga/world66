The cedar of Lebanon — *Cedrus libani* — is one of the ancient world's most freighted symbols. Mentioned in the Epic of Gilgamesh, in the Hebrew Bible, and in Greek and Roman sources, the cedar was the prized timber of the ancient Middle East: Solomon's Temple was built with it, Phoenician ships were planked with it, and Egyptian pharaohs imported it from the mountain forests of what is now Lebanon. Today the cedar appears on the Lebanese flag, and in the diaspora the name *Les Cèdres* signals an unmistakably Levantine affiliation. A mosque bearing this name in Marseille speaks to the presence of Lebanese, Syrian, and other eastern Mediterranean Muslim communities in the city — communities that arrived through different migration routes than the larger Maghrebi population but share the same urban northern arrondissements.

Marseille has been a point of entry for migrants from across the Mediterranean and beyond since at least the 19th century. The northern arrondissements — the 13th, 14th, 15th, and surrounding districts — developed rapidly in the 20th century as housing estates absorbed workers from Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, and sub-Saharan Africa, as well as smaller waves from Lebanon, Turkey, and the Comoros. Religious institutions in these neighbourhoods often serve communities that have been in Marseille for two or three generations: the mosque is not simply a place of worship but a community anchor, running Quranic classes, providing space for weddings and funerals, and maintaining cultural ties to countries of origin.

The Mosquée des Cèdres sits in a residential district to the north-east of the city centre, where the urban fabric opens into lower-rise housing and the neighbourhood character is distinctly working-class. Friday prayers draw worshippers from the surrounding streets; on ordinary days the building operates quietly as a community resource. The name, with its evocation of Lebanon's mountain forests, is a small act of memory — a reminder of where the founding community came from, carried forward in stone and mortar in a port city that has always absorbed people from the sea.
