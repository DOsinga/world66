The collection runs to over 1,000 objects: sarcophagi, mummies, Canopic jars, shabtis, amulets, and funerary material spanning three millennia of Egyptian history. Marseille assembled much of it during the 19th century through its port connections with Egypt and North Africa — the same trading routes that gave the city its commercial character gave its collectors direct access to material that would have reached Paris only through more institutional, more mediated channels.

The building is the Vieille Charité, a 17th-century charitable hospice designed by Pierre Puget — Marseille's most celebrated Baroque sculptor. The central chapel, Puget's masterwork, rises from a galleried courtyard; the collections occupy the arcaded galleries on three levels around it. The combination of building and contents makes this one of the architecturally finest museum settings in France, and one of the least visited relative to its quality.

The same complex houses the Musée des Arts Africains, Océaniens, Amérindiens (MAAOA), one of France's major ethnographic collections, with pre-Columbian, Pacific, and African material across several thousand objects. A single admission covers both; allow at least half a day if taking both seriously.
