France was one of the earliest countries to develop a commercial motorcycle industry, and the Musée de la Moto pays tribute to that heritage through a collection of machines spanning the late nineteenth century to the mid-twentieth. Visitors can trace the evolution from the earliest motorised bicycles to the rugged single-cylinder machines that served dispatch riders in both world wars. The collection features marques that once defined French motoring — Peugeot, whose motorcycles predate its cars, Terrot from Dijon, Gnome et Rhône (originally an aero-engine maker that turned to motorcycles after 1918), and René Gillet, whose heavy side-valve machines were standard issue to the French army and police. Together these machines tell a story of industrial ingenuity and the gradual democratisation of personal transport.

Beyond the machines themselves, the museum sets them in context: photographs, trade catalogues, period posters, and tools from former workshops illustrate the artisanal world in which these motorcycles were built and maintained. The contrast with modern mass production is striking — many of the machines on display were assembled by hand in small factories where craftsmen knew every component by name.

The museum sits in the eastern suburbs of Marseille and is easiest to reach by car; parking is available nearby. It is a niche destination suited to motorcycle enthusiasts and industrial-history buffs rather than the casual visitor, but its intimacy and the quality of its machines make it well worth the detour.
