The current Romano-Byzantine basilica was built between 1853 and 1864 to designs by the architect Henri Espérandieu, replacing an earlier fortified chapel that had occupied the summit of the 162-metre limestone outcrop since the thirteenth century. The museum traces this architectural transformation and the devotional life that has surrounded the site for seven hundred years. Documents, engravings, architectural drawings, and models explain how Espérandieu's lavish design — striped stone courses, a gilded copper statue of the Virgin crowning the bell tower — was financed and executed.

The most extraordinary element of the collection is its assemblage of ex-votos: offerings left by worshippers in thanks for miraculous deliverance. The tradition is ancient across Catholic Europe, but Notre-Dame de la Garde has one of the largest and most varied accumulations anywhere in France. Painted panels depict storms at sea, shipwrecks, mining accidents, and railway disasters from which the devout believed the Virgin had saved them. Hanging alongside are model ships — brigs, barques, steam packets, and naval vessels — presented by sailors and their families, some of extraordinary craftsmanship. Plaques in marble and painted tin, crutches, and military memorabilia from both world wars fill the galleries and the lower church walls.

The museum is integrated into the basilica visit, so entry to the church gives access to the displays. The site itself, invariably called "la Bonne Mère" by Marseillais, commands panoramic views of the city, the Calanques coastline, and the islands of the Frioul archipelago — reason enough to make the climb even without the museum.
