The Musée des Arts Africains, Océaniens, Amérindiens occupies the north wing of the Vieille Charité, the 17th-century charitable hospice designed by Pierre Puget and now one of the finest Baroque buildings in France. The collection runs to more than 4,000 objects: African sculpture and ceremonial objects, material from Polynesia and Melanesia, and pre-Columbian pieces from Mesoamerica and the Andes. The range reflects Marseille's position as the gateway to French Africa and Indochina — a port that had colonial connections others lacked, and that assembled collections as a byproduct.

The African holdings are the strongest, with masks, figurative sculpture, and ritual objects from across sub-Saharan Africa. The Oceanic section covers both Polynesia and Melanesia with objects acquired through missionary and commercial networks during the colonial period. The pre-Columbian material is smaller but includes pieces from several distinct civilisations, which makes the museum unusually broad in scope for a city of Marseille's size.

The Vieille Charité building is worth time in itself. Puget designed the central chapel as a Baroque oval, and it now hosts temporary exhibitions — the same space that originally sheltered beggars cleared from Marseille's streets. The museum shares the building with the Musée d'Archéologie Méditerranéenne, so a single visit can move from Greek ceramics to Pacific canoe prows without changing buildings.
