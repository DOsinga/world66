The Musée des Arts Africains, Océaniens, Amérindiens occupies the north wing of the Vieille Charité — the 17th-century hospice designed by Pierre Puget, now one of the finest Baroque buildings in France. The collection runs to more than 4,000 objects: African masks and ceremonial sculpture, material from Polynesia and Melanesia, pre-Columbian pieces from Mesoamerica and the Andes. The range reflects Marseille's position as the gateway to French Africa and Indochina — a port that had colonial connections others lacked, and that assembled collections as a byproduct.

The African holdings are the strongest. The Oceanic section covers Polynesia and Melanesia with objects acquired through missionary and commercial networks during the colonial period. The pre-Columbian material is smaller but draws from several distinct civilisations. Unusually broad for a city of this size.

Puget's central chapel — a Baroque oval that originally sheltered beggars cleared from Marseille's streets — now hosts temporary exhibitions. The building shares an entrance with the Musée d'Archéologie Méditerranéenne; one admission covers both, and you can move from Greek ceramics to Pacific canoe prows without going outside.
