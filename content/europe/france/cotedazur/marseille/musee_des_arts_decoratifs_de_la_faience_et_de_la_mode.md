In the 17th and 18th centuries Marseille was one of the great centres of European faience production. The workshops — above all those founded by Joseph Clérissy and later run by the Veuve Perrin — supplied the Mediterranean world with tin-glazed earthenware distinguished by vivid polychrome painting: fish, flowers, insects, harbour scenes, rendered with a lightness that set Marseillaise pieces apart from heavier Delft or Rouen wares. At its peak in the mid-18th century the industry employed thousands of workers across dozens of ateliers. The museum holds a major collection, tracing local styles from bold early manganese-outlined forms to the delicate *petit feu* enamel work that became the city's signature.

The setting matters. The Château Borély was built in the 1760s for a wealthy merchant family — classical facades, formal gardens, among the finest Provençal 18th-century architecture in the region. Restored for museum use, the interiors are a good match for the decorative arts collections, which extend beyond faience to furniture, silverware, and objets d'art. The fashion galleries, with historical costume and contemporary designer exhibitions, occupy separate rooms.

The château sits in the Parc Borély near the Prado beaches in the 8th arrondissement — rose gardens, a boating lake, and a café. Worth lingering.
