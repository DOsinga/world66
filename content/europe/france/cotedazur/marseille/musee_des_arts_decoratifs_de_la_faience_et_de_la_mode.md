Marseille was one of the great centres of European faience production during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The city's workshops — above all those founded by Joseph Clérissy and later run by the Veuve Perrin — supplied the Mediterranean world with tin-glazed earthenware distinguished by its vivid polychrome painting: fish, flowers, insects, and harbour scenes rendered with a lightness that set Marseillaise faience apart from heavier Delft or Rouen wares. At its peak in the mid-eighteenth century, the industry employed thousands of workers across dozens of ateliers. The museum holds a major collection of these pieces, allowing visitors to follow the development of local styles from the bold manganese-outlined forms of the early period to the delicate petit feu enamel decoration that became the city's signature.

The setting enhances the experience considerably. The Château Borély was built in the 1760s for a wealthy merchant family and later acquired by the city; its classical facades and formal gardens are among the finest examples of Provençal eighteenth-century architecture. Restored and converted for museum use, the château's interiors provide a sympathetic backdrop for the decorative arts collections, which extend beyond faience to include furniture, silverware, and objets d'art. The fashion collection, displayed in dedicated galleries, presents historical costume and accessories and hosts temporary exhibitions from contemporary designers.

The museum sits in the Parc Borély near the Prado beaches in the eighth arrondissement, reachable by tram or bus from the city centre. The park itself is worth lingering in: rose gardens, a boating lake, and a café make it one of Marseille's most pleasant green spaces.
