After the Revolution closed the churches, Marseille families had nowhere to set up a nativity scene. A merchant named Jean-Louis Lagnel solved it in 1797: small painted terracotta figures, cheap enough for anyone. He didn't stop at the Holy Family. He added the whole Provençal village — the baker, the miller, the fishwife, the village drunk. The santon was born. Marseille has been making them ever since.

This museum in Château-Gombert holds one of the best collections, from the earliest examples to contemporary work. But the santons are just the centrepiece — the rooms also cover Provençal rural life more broadly: regional costumes, woven textiles, carved furniture, copper kitchen equipment, tools. It's a portrait of a world that has otherwise mostly vanished.

Château-Gombert itself is worth the trip. It sits in the 13th arrondissement, semi-rural and quiet, stone streets and a Romanesque church — nothing like the urban quarters below. You take the bus up and arrive somewhere that feels entirely separate from the rest of the city.
