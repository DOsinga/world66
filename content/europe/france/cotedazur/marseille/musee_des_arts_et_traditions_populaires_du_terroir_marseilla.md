The santon — a diminutive of the Provençal word for "little saint" — was invented in Marseille in the years immediately following the French Revolution, when the closure of churches left families without the customary nativity scene. Around 1797, Jean-Louis Lagnel began producing small, affordable painted terracotta figurines that could be arranged into a crèche at home. The innovation was immediate and lasting: Lagnel's figures went beyond the Holy Family to include all the characters of Provençal village life — the baker, the miller, the fishwife, the knife-grinder, the blind man, the village drunk — effectively turning the nativity into a tableau of regional society. Marseille's santon-makers, called santonniers, have kept the craft alive ever since, and the city hosts one of France's great Christmas fairs dedicated to these figures. The museum holds an important collection spanning the earliest examples to contemporary work, documenting both the iconography and the craft techniques.

Santons are the centrepiece, but the museum's scope is broad. Collections of regional costumes, woven textiles, agricultural tools, domestic furniture, and craft implements reconstruct the texture of Provençal rural life from the seventeenth century to the early twentieth. Carved wooden furniture from the Arles and Aubagne traditions, faience from local kilns, and copper kitchen equipment fill the rooms of a handsome mas-style building.

Château-Gombert itself repays the visit. This semi-rural enclave in the thirteenth arrondissement retains stone streets, a Romanesque church, and a character entirely its own — quieter and more village-like than the urban quarters below. Buses connect it to the city centre, but the neighbourhood is most easily explored on foot once you arrive.
