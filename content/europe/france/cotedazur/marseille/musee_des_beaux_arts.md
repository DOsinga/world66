The museum occupies the south wing of the Palais Longchamp, a monumentally extravagant building completed in 1869. The palace was not conceived as a museum: it was built as the ceremonial terminus of the Canal de Marseille, the aqueduct that brought freshwater from the Durance River to a city that had no reliable supply of its own. The central cascade is a water feature disguised as Baroque architecture, and the engineering ambition behind it rivals anything in the building's art collection. The north wing houses the Natural History Museum; the two collections bookend a fountain complex that ranks among the most theatrical pieces of civic engineering in France.

The permanent collection is strong on Flemish masters — Rubens, Jordaens — and 17th-century French painting, with a notable group of works by Pierre Puget. Puget worked principally at Versailles and Toulon and spent long periods in Genoa, but Marseille was his birthplace, and the museum holds drawings, models, and works that document a sculptor who was simultaneously too grand and too provincial for the Paris establishment to fully absorb. Colbert's reported remark — that Puget's submissions were too magnificent for the king — captures something real about the relationship between the man and the court.

Honoré Daumier was born in Marseille in 1808 and later became the most important political caricaturist in French history. His lithographs targeting the July Monarchy and Napoleon III made him famous, briefly imprisoned, and eventually blind. Marseille barely acknowledged him in his lifetime; the museum's dedicated Daumier room is a belated correction.
