Soap-making in Marseille dates to at least the fourteenth century, when the city's access to Provençal olive oil and North African soda ash gave local workshops a natural advantage. By the seventeenth century the industry had grown substantial enough to attract royal attention: in 1688, Louis XIV issued an edict strictly regulating the production of "Savon de Marseille," requiring that it be made exclusively from plant-based oils (primarily olive), forbidding the use of animal fats, and specifying that production could occur only in the summer months when the heat was judged ideal for saponification. The 72% minimum oil content mandated by that tradition remains the benchmark by which authentic Marseille soap is still judged. At its industrial peak in the nineteenth century, the Marseille region operated more than ninety soap factories, and the distinctive green or white 300-gram cube was exported across Europe, the Americas, and the Levant.

The museum sets this history within its commercial, social, and scientific context. Antique tools — the great wooden paddles used to stir the cooking kettles, the wire frames for cutting cakes of soap, the stamps and presses for marking the finished bars — sit alongside trade documents, advertising posters, and examples of packaging from different eras. The chemistry of cold-process versus hot-process saponification is explained accessibly, as is the economic decline triggered by cheaper synthetic detergents in the mid-twentieth century and the subsequent revival of artisanal production.

The museum also addresses the fraught question of authenticity. "Savon de Marseille" remains an unprotected designation, meaning the label can legally appear on products made anywhere with any oils. The museum documents the ongoing campaign by remaining producers — there are now only a handful of traditional soap factories left — to secure protected geographical indication status for the authentic article.
