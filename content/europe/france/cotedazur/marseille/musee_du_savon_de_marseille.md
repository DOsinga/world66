In 1688, Louis XIV issued an edict: Savon de Marseille must be made from plant oils only — primarily olive — no animal fat, summer production only, minimum 72% oil content. Marseille had been making soap since the 14th century, trading on its access to Provençal olive oil and North African soda ash. The king's rules just formalised what was already a major industry. At its peak in the 19th century, the region ran over ninety soap factories.

The museum tells that story through antique tools — the wooden paddles for stirring the kettles, the wire frames for cutting the blocks, the stamps and presses — alongside trade documents and advertising posters from different eras. The chemistry is explained clearly: why hot-process, why the oil content matters, how synthetic detergents gutted the industry after the 1950s.

The uncomfortable part: "Savon de Marseille" is still an unprotected name. Any factory anywhere can put it on the packaging. The handful of traditional producers who still make it the old way have been fighting for protected geographical indication status for years. The museum documents that fight. It's ongoing.
