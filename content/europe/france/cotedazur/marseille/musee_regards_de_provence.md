The Gare Maritime was Pouillon's design for the Joliette passenger terminal, built in the 1950s to handle the ferry traffic between Marseille and French North Africa. At its peak during the Algerian War and through the independence transitions, the building processed people moving in both directions: emigrants going one way, soldiers and pieds-noirs the other. The museum, opened in 2013 as part of the European Capital of Culture programme, repurposes the structure without erasing its history.

The collection focuses on how Provence and the Mediterranean were seen by outside artists — principally the Orientalist painters who used Marseille as a gateway to North Africa in the 19th century, and the artists who followed Cézanne to the Provençal landscape. The Orientalist material is the more complex part of the collection: paintings made by French artists depicting North Africa in the colonial period, displayed in a building that was itself an instrument of that colonialism, in a city where the political charge of this history remains active.

The curators acknowledge the building's history periodically, if not systematically — the connection between the paintings on the walls and the function of the room those walls once defined is left for visitors to make themselves. Pouillon's architecture is worth attention in its own right: the ground-floor hall retains much of its 1950s character, and the sea-facing terraces offer views across the harbour mouth to the islands.
