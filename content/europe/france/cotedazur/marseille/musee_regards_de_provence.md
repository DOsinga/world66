Fernand Pouillon designed this building in the 1950s as Marseille's main passenger ferry terminal — the Joliette Gare Maritime. At its peak during the Algerian War and through the independence transition, it processed people moving in both directions: emigrants going south, soldiers and pieds-noirs coming back. The structure remembers all of that, even if the building doesn't say so directly.

The museum, opened in 2013, put its collection of Orientalist painting inside those walls. French artists in the 19th century used Marseille as the gateway to North Africa — painting the desert, the medinas, the people — and the collection documents that visual tradition. Complex material: colonial-era paintings, displayed in a building that was itself an instrument of the colonial project, in a city where that history has living weight.

The curators acknowledge the tension periodically rather than systematically. The connection between the pictures on the walls and what happened in these rooms is left partly for you to complete. The building's architecture holds up: the ground-floor hall has kept much of its 1950s character, and the sea-facing terraces give you a long view across the harbour mouth to the islands.
