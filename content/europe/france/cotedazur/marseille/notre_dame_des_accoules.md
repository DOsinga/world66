The church was gutted in the Revolution — converted to a saltpetre depot, partially demolished. The 12th-century bell tower survived because it had been physically integrated into what became a residential building. Its stones became someone's walls. It's still there, rising above the rooflines of Le Panier, attached to an apartment block. Easy to miss entirely — there's no complete facade, no forecourt, no obvious approach.

A small oratory rebuilt beneath the tower in the 19th century marks the site. The name comes from the Latin *Aquolae*, meaning springs. The high ground of Le Panier held freshwater sources, and those sources were part of why the earliest Greek settlers chose this hill for Massalia around 600 BCE. The etymology has outlasted the medieval church, the Revolutionary destruction, and everything since.

This is the only physical remnant of medieval Marseille at ground level in Le Panier. Everything else that survived from that period was gone by January 1943, when German occupying forces demolished the entire northern waterfront quarter in a deliberate act of erasure. The tower predates that destruction by eight centuries. It predates most of what gets called Marseille's historical heritage, and almost nobody knows it's there.
