The tower survived because it was structurally integrated into what became an apartment building — its stones became someone's walls. It is easy to miss entirely: there is no complete church facade, no forecourt, no obvious approach. A small oratory reconstructed beneath the tower in the 19th century marks the site; the Romanesque stonework of the tower rises above it and above the rooflines of the surrounding buildings. The name Accoules derives from the Latin *Aquolae*, meaning springs. The high ground of Le Panier held freshwater sources that made it viable for the earliest Greek settlers — Massalia was founded on this hill around 600 BCE, and the springs were part of what made the site worth choosing. That etymology has survived the medieval church, the Revolutionary destruction, and the century since.

The tower is the only physical remnant of Marseille's medieval built fabric at ground level in Le Panier; the rest of what survived from that period was demolished in January 1943 when German occupying forces destroyed the northern waterfront quarter in a deliberate act of urban erasure. The tower predates that destruction by 800 years. It predates most of what is usually described as Marseille's historical heritage, and it stands overlooked in a neighbourhood that is itself regularly overlooked in favour of the waterfront and the museums to the south.
