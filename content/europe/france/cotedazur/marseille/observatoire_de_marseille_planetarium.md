The Marseille Observatory was established in 1702, making it one of the oldest in France, predating the systematic organisation of French science under the Académie des Sciences. It occupies a hilltop site south-east of the city centre, chosen for its elevation above the coastal haze and its distance from the worst of the maritime humidity. The buildings include a handsome eighteenth-century tower and later domed telescopes added as astronomical instrumentation developed through the nineteenth century. The site was declared a historic monument in recognition of its architectural and scientific heritage.

The observatory's scientific legacy is substantial. Data gathered here contributed to the broader French astronomical project that mapped the southern skies and tracked the motions of comets and minor planets. The catalogue of nebulae and star clusters compiled by Charles Messier — one of the foundational documents of modern astronomy — drew partly on observations made at institutions like this one, and several Marseille astronomers contributed to the tradition of careful positional measurement that characterised French science in the age of enlightenment.

Today the facility operates as a working research institute attached to Aix-Marseille University, focused on astrophysics, planetary science, and cosmology. The attached planetarium functions as the public face of this research, offering projection shows and educational programmes that bring the observatory's scientific work to a general audience. Visits to the historic buildings are possible on open days, when the original telescopes and archival instruments go on display.
