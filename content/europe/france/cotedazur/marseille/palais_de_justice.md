The Palais de Justice was built between 1862 and 1869 — though designed in the earlier classical tradition — to replace the city's cramped medieval court facilities. Michel-Robert Penchaud, the architect also responsible for the Porte d'Aix, gave it a monumental Corinthian portico and a long, austere facade that dominates the Cours Pierre Puget, a formal tree-lined boulevard in the centre of the city. The building reads as an architecture of authority: heavy, symmetrical, meant to project the gravity of the French judicial system.

Inside, the building follows the standard plan of French palais de justice of the period, with a grand hall distributing into chambers and administrative wings. High-profile trials in Marseille have been held here across two centuries, and the building remains the seat of the city's Tribunal de Grande Instance. The steps outside are a gathering point during significant hearings, as they are at courthouses across France.

The Cours Pierre Puget is one of Marseille's less-visited central streets, quieter than the Canebière but worth a detour for its 17th-century architecture and the nearby Jardin Pierre Puget. The Palais de Justice anchors the southern end of the cours and is a five-minute walk from the Vieux-Port.
