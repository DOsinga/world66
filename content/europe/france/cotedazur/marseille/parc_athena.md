Parc Athéna's 14 hectares in the 13th arrondissement occupy former agricultural land at the edge of Château Gombert, the village-within-the-city that sits at the northern rim of Marseille. The park's centrepiece is the Villa Athéna, an eclectic early-20th-century mansion built by Jean Marie Joseph Barlatier, then director of the Marseille newspaper *Le Sémaphore*. His son Paul, passionate about ancient Greece and classical theatre, commissioned the Athena Niké Theatre on the grounds: a small open-air venue deliberately modelled on the ancient theatre in Athens, designed by architect Jean Boët and inaugurated in the 1910s.

The theatre drew opera singers, tragic actors, and dancers in its early years, an unlikely venue for high culture on what was then the city's agricultural outskirts. It did not remain a theatre for long: by 1916 it had been converted to a cinema studio, the Phocéa Film studios, and by 1919 French film journals were describing the facility as a model of modern production — Marseille's brief attempt at its own Hollywood. The studios are long gone, and the former theatre stands in the park as a curiosity in garrigue stone, its form still legible even stripped of its original purpose.

The park today includes a pond fed by natural streams — the Grave and Bessons waterways that generate small wetlands with willows and reed beds — plus sports facilities, a skate bowl, children's play areas, and cycle paths for road safety education. It sits within the Château Gombert Technology Park, a cluster of high-tech firms and university laboratories that surrounds what was once a hilltop village, making Parc Athéna a counterpoint of green and historical strangeness in an otherwise modernised landscape.
