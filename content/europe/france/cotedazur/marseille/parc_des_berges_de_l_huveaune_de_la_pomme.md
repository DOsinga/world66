The Huveaune is one of those rivers that most visitors to Marseille never think about — it rises in the Sainte-Baume massif some sixty kilometres to the east, cuts through the limestone hinterland, and reaches the sea just beyond the city's eastern edge. In the La Pomme neighbourhood, in the 11th arrondissement, the Parc des Berges de l'Huveaune follows the river's banks through what remains a surprisingly green corridor of poplars and willows amid the surrounding suburban development.

The park is linear rather than centred: it runs along the water rather than opening into a broad cleared space, which gives it a different rhythm from a classic public garden. Walking it end to end feels more like a riverside path than a park visit, with the sound of the Huveaune — modest and swift after rain, sluggish in high summer — as a constant companion. Fishermen occupy the better-sheltered banks early in the morning; families spread out on the grassy verges in the afternoon.

La Pomme (*the apple*) is a quiet residential quarter, and the park is very much a neighbourhood amenity rather than a destination. But for anyone exploring eastern Marseille by bicycle or on foot, the berges offer a welcome green thread through an otherwise ordinary suburb, and the river itself — with its origins deep in Provence — is a reminder that this city, for all its urban density, sits within a landscape of considerable wildness.
