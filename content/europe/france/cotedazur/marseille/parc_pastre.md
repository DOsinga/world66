Parc Pastré is the largest green space in southern Marseille, a former aristocratic estate of some ninety hectares that was acquired by the city in the 1940s and opened as a public park. The property belonged to the Pastré family, one of the notable Marseille bourgeois dynasties of the nineteenth century, and the estate retains the bones of a grand country retreat: a château at its centre, long alleys of mature trees, and terraced grounds that rise from the coastal plain toward the limestone ridges above.

The park became a place of particular historical weight during the Second World War. The Pastré family, and especially Lily Pastré, used the château to shelter Jewish musicians, artists, and intellectuals who had fled Nazi persecution and were stranded in unoccupied France. Concerts and performances took place in the grounds, allowing artists to continue working while they awaited the papers that might allow them to emigrate. The memory of that wartime hospitality gives the park a certain gravity beyond its scale.

Today Parc Pastré is used for walking, running, and picnicking by residents of the southern arrondissements. The grounds include woodland paths, open meadows, and views toward the coast and the Calanques. The château is not regularly open to the public, but the park itself is free to enter and one of the better places in the city to spend an unhurried afternoon.
