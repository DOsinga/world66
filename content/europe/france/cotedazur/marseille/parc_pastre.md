During the Second World War, when Nazi persecution had pushed artists and intellectuals into unoccupied France with nowhere left to go, Lily Pastré opened the château. She sheltered Jewish musicians, painters, and writers on the estate — gave them somewhere to work, somewhere safe — and organised concerts in the grounds while they waited, often for years, for papers that might let them leave. It was an act of extraordinary practical courage dressed up as culture.

The Pastré family had owned this ninety-hectare estate since the nineteenth century — one of the great Marseille bourgeois dynasties, with a château, long allées of mature trees, and terraced grounds running toward the limestone hills above the coast. The city bought it in the 1940s and opened it as a public park.

Today it's the largest green space in southern Marseille: woodland paths, open meadows, views down toward the Calanques. The château is rarely open, but the park is free. One of the genuinely good places in the city to spend an afternoon.
