A parvis is the open forecourt in front of a church or public building, and the Parvis Madeleine et André Villard is exactly that: a modest paved gathering space serving the surrounding neighbourhood. It is named jointly after Madeleine and André Villard, a couple commemorated here almost certainly for long service to the local community — the kind of quiet civic dedication, through associations, schools or neighbourhood institutions, that cities like Marseille honour by inscribing ordinary names into public space alongside the statesmen and artists.

The parvis occupies a part of Marseille's northern residential quarters, away from the tourist circuits of the Old Port and Le Panier. In these streets the city has a more workaday character — apartment blocks, local markets, schools, and the sort of neighbourhood church that anchors daily life for families who have lived here for generations. The forecourt provides a small pause in that fabric, a place to sit on a bench or let children run while adults talk.

For visitors who venture beyond the centre, the parvis offers a glimpse of a Marseille that is entirely functional and genuinely inhabited — a city that names its squares after the Villards as readily as after poets and politicians, maintaining the texture of local memory block by block.
