Paul Valéry was born in 1871 at Sète, the small port city at the western end of the Languedoc coast, to a Corsican father and a Genoese mother. The Mediterranean — its light, its ancient intellectual tradition, its particular quality of consciousness — was for Valéry not a backdrop but a philosophical condition. He came to know Marseille through travel and connection, and the monument here places him within the broad Mediterranean culture that he articulated more precisely than almost any other modern writer in French.

His poetry is among the most technically demanding in the French tradition. "La Jeune Parque" (1917), composed over four years of intensive work, is a meditation on consciousness, identity, and the body that operates through a near-musical structure of interlocking sounds and rhythms. "Le Cimetière Marin" (1920), set at a seaside cemetery overlooking the Mediterranean at Sète, is his most celebrated poem — a meditation on mortality, the sea, and the intellect that achieves a formal perfection that has made it a touchstone of French literary culture. He was elected to the Académie Française in 1925.

Valéry also wrote extensively on aesthetics, mathematics, and the nature of the mind, and his notebooks — the Cahiers, kept obsessively over decades — are among the most extraordinary private intellectual documents of the 20th century. This monument acknowledges the Mediterranean inheritance that gave his work its particular temper: the clarity, the precision, the refusal of vagueness that he associated with the southern light and the long tradition of thinking by the sea.
