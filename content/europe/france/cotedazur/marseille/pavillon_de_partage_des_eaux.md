In 1849, after centuries of chronic water shortage, the Canal de Marseille finally opened. It ran 80 kilometres from the Durance river through tunnels, aqueducts, and siphons to a city that was booming and desperately thirsty. Engineer Franz Mayor de Montricher built it in eight years. Fountains appeared in quarters that had never had reliable water.

This pavilion was where the flow got divided and directed — the node where the canal's water was shared out across the city's distribution network. The name says it plainly: partage des eaux, the sharing of the waters.

They built it with architectural seriousness, not as plain utilitarian infrastructure, because Marseille understood this wasn't just plumbing. It was the reason the city could keep growing.
