The Canal de Marseille was one of the great infrastructure achievements of nineteenth-century France. Completed in 1849 under engineer Franz Mayor de Montricher, it carried water from the Durance river near Pertuis across more than 80 kilometres of aqueducts, tunnels, siphons and open channels to a city that had suffered chronic water shortages for centuries. Marseille's population was booming — the city would eventually become France's second largest — and the canal transformed daily life: fountains appeared in neighbourhoods that had never had reliable water, and the city's industrial and sanitary capacity expanded dramatically.

The Pavillon de Partage des Eaux — the Water Distribution Pavilion — is one of the functional and symbolic nodes of this system. "Partage des eaux" literally means the dividing or sharing of waters: these structures regulated how the flow from the canal was split and directed to different parts of the city and its distribution network. They were built with a degree of architectural seriousness that reflected how important the canal was; a plain utilitarian shed would not have done justice to the civic achievement it represented.

The pavilion sits in the northeastern arc of the city, in the zone where the canal's infrastructure was most dense before water entered the urban distribution grid. It belongs to a tradition of treating civil engineering as architecture — a tradition that Marseille, a city that has always taken its public works seriously, maintained well into the industrial era.
