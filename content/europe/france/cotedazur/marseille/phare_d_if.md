The Phare d'If stands at the eastern tip of the Île d'If, the smallest and closest of the islands in Marseille's Frioul archipelago, roughly three kilometres west of the Vieux-Port. Its practical function is to guide vessels through the approaches to the harbour, marking the northern limit of the channel between the Château d'If promontory and the Ratonneau island to the west. The lighthouse is still operational; the beam is visible from the sea long before the tower itself becomes distinct from the pale limestone of the island.

The island beneath it is dominated by the Château d'If, a fortress built between 1524 and 1531 under François I as part of the coastal defences of the Gulf of Lion. It served briefly as a military garrison and then, from the 17th century onward, as a state prison — remote enough to be inescapable, close enough to Marseille for the city to see it from the waterfront. Real prisoners held here included Huguenots, political opponents of successive French governments, and various figures the crown found inconvenient. The historical cell of the Man in the Iron Mask is among those shown to visitors, though the identity of that prisoner remains genuinely disputed.

What draws most visitors to the island is fictional: Alexandre Dumas set his 1844 novel The Count of Monte Cristo here, and the cell attributed to his invented prisoner Edmond Dantès has been marked and shown to tourists since the 19th century. The guides who conduct tours through the château make the distinction between the historical and the fictional cells with varying degrees of emphasis. Boats to the island leave from the Vieux-Port; the crossing takes roughly 20 minutes, and the combination of the lighthouse, the fortress, the sea views back toward the city, and the accumulated weight of real and invented history makes the trip one of the more rewarding half-days available from Marseille.
