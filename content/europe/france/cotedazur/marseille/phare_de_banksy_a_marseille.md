Banksy, the anonymous British street artist whose sardonic stencils have appeared on walls from Bristol to Bethlehem, left his mark on Marseille with a painted lighthouse. The work depicts a simple lighthouse beam sweeping out from a crumbling or ordinary urban surface, the kind of juxtaposition Banksy favours: a navigational beacon rendered on a wall that nobody would ordinarily mistake for the sea.

Lighthouses carry a dense freight of symbolism — guidance for the lost, warning against hidden rocks, hope glimpsed from a dark sea. In a port city that has welcomed, and sometimes repelled, millions of arrivals over three millennia, the image reads with particular force. Marseille's waterfront has guided Phoenician traders, Crusade fleets, colonial steamships, and today's container giants; Banksy's gesture finds the universal in the local.

Street art in Marseille has long thrived on the city's layered, rough-edged energy. Locating the piece requires a little detective work — Banksy rarely announces coordinates and the work's condition depends on the wall's owners and the weather — but that search is part of the experience. Treat it as a small urban pilgrimage, and when you find it, consider what it means to post a lighthouse on land.
