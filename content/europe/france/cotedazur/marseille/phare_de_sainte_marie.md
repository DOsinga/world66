The Phare de Sainte-Marie stands at a rocky coastal promontory east of central Marseille, where the white limestone cliffs typical of the Calanques begin to take over from the city's urban waterfront. The lighthouse marks a stretch of coast that has proved hazardous to vessels over the centuries — reefs and submerged shelves extend offshore, invisible on the chart except as contour lines that tighten alarmingly close to the surface.

The surrounding area is within the orbit of the Parc National des Calanques and the coastline here alternates between small pebbly coves accessible on foot and sheer cliffs plunging straight into translucent green water. Hikers on the Sentier des Douaniers — the old customs officers' path that traces the coast — pass close to the lighthouse, and on a clear day the views take in the Frioul archipelago to the west and the jagged Calanques horizon to the east. The lighthouse itself is a modest structure, its white tower unremarkable from a distance, but it anchors the headland visually and gives the otherwise unmarked cape an identity.

Sailors know this coast as one of the more demanding approaches to Marseille, with the mistral capable of building short, steep seas that can push a vessel sideways faster than expected. The light's characteristic flash — white to seaward, red sector over the shallows — is the key signal that tells an incoming skipper exactly where the danger lies. Coming in by sea at night with the lighthouse winking its sequence is one of the more satisfying arrivals the Mediterranean offers.
