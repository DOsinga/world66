Pierre Puget (1620–1694) was born in Marseille and is the city's most famous artist — a sculptor, painter, and architect of European stature whose life story is partly the story of a provincial genius navigating a court that preferred its art domesticated and decorative. His two greatest sculptures, the Milon de Crotone and the Perseus and Andromeda, were commissioned for Versailles but placed there reluctantly, in peripheral spots, after Louis XIV's superintendent of buildings Nicolas Colbert found the work too turbulent for the formal gardens. They now hang in the Louvre, recognised as among the finest Baroque sculptures in existence.

Puget spent long periods working in Genoa and Toulon — the Génois were more appreciative of his dynamism — and his Marseille years produced public works that shaped the city's seventeenth-century character: the Hôtel de Ville, the Vieille Charité, the Hôtel de Cabre, all bear his influence or his hand directly. The Vieille Charité hospice, which he designed, is one of the most beautiful buildings in Provence and now houses the Musée d'Archéologie Méditerranéenne.

The monument near the Vieux-Port places him within walking distance of the buildings he shaped. Puget is Marseille's answer to the question of what its cultural production consists in: not literary, not musical, but stone — hard, worked, physical, resistant to easy placement in canonical hierarchies. He belongs with Rubens and Bernini and yet spent his career fighting for royal recognition that came too slowly and departed too quickly.
