In 1848, for a brief window after the revolution, the French National Assembly opened to men who had actually worked with their hands. Antide Boyer, a Marseille typographer, walked through that door. He was not a theorist or a pamphleteer — his authority came from the workshops of the printing trade. He organised, and the workers of Marseille elected him.

He served one term. Louis-Napoleon's coup closed the door again, and Boyer died in 1850. The political space that had produced him shut down almost immediately. The square in the Noailles district is all that's left of the memory.

Noailles today is one of the most vivid parts of the city — dense, largely North African and Comoran in character, anchored by the food market on the rue Longue-des-Capucins five minutes east. The political radicalism Boyer represented has other addresses now.
