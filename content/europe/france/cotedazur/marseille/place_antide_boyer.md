Place Antide Boyer occupies a quiet corner in the dense residential fabric between the Noailles market and the cours Julien, in a part of Marseille that has long been associated with labour politics and immigrant communities. It is named after Antide Boyer (1792–1850), a Marseille typographer who became one of the first working-class deputies elected to the French National Assembly following the revolution of 1848. Boyer was not a theorist but an organiser — a man whose authority came from the workshops of the printing trade, not from any bourgeois political tradition.

The 1848 elections were remarkable across France for briefly opening the National Assembly to men of manual trades, and Boyer's election from Marseille was part of that moment. He served a single term before the Second Republic gave way to Louis-Napoleon's coup, and the political space that had produced him closed again. The square's name is a reminder that the working-class politics that would define Marseille's 20th-century identity had its roots in the street culture and trade organisations of the mid-19th century.

Today the square functions as a neighbourhood breathing space — a few benches, some shade, and the ordinary street life of a district that is home to a large North African and Comoran community. The Noailles market on the rue Longue-des-Capucins, a five-minute walk east, remains one of the most vivid food markets in the city.
