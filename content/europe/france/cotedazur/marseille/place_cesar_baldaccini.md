César Baldaccini was born in 1921 in Belle de Mai, to Italian immigrants from Puglia. He later dropped the surname professionally. As simply César, he became one of the defining sculptors of postwar France — famous for the "Compressions," blocks of automobiles crushed into dense rectangles of compacted metal, and for the "Expansions," poured polyurethane forms that solidified mid-flow.

In 1975 he designed the Trophée César, France's equivalent of the Academy Award. The statuette is a direct casting of his own thumb, enlarged. It has been reproduced in the hundreds every year since. The connection between a sculptor from this working-class neighbourhood and France's most prestigious cinema prize is exactly the kind of improbable arc Marseille specialises in.

Belle de Mai today is known for La Friche Belle de Mai, a former tobacco factory converted into one of France's largest arts centres — an industrial neighbourhood that produced a sculptor famous for transforming industrial materials into art. The square is quiet. The Friche is ten minutes on foot.
