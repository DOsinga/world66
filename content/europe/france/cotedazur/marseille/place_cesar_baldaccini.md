Place César Baldaccini sits in the Belle de Mai district of Marseille's 3rd arrondissement, the neighbourhood where César Baldaccini was born in 1921 to Italian immigrant parents from Puglia. The family name was later shortened to César for professional use, and under that name he became one of the defining sculptors of postwar France. The square's location in his birth neighbourhood is deliberate: in a city of Italian immigrant descent, naming a square in the popular quarter after its most internationally famous son carries particular weight.

César's two most recognisable bodies of work are the "Compressions" — automobile sculptures crushed into dense rectangles of compacted metal — and the Trophée César statuette, which he designed in 1975 for what became France's equivalent of the Academy Awards. The Trophée César is a direct casting of his own thumb, enlarged, and it is reproduced in the hundreds each year for France's film industry. The connection between a sculptor from a working-class Marseille neighbourhood and France's most prestigious cinema prize is one of those improbable biographical arcs that the city seems to produce with regularity.

Belle de Mai today is known primarily for La Friche Belle de Mai, a converted tobacco factory that houses one of France's largest contemporary arts centres. The juxtaposition is fitting: an industrial neighbourhood that produced a sculptor famous for transforming industrial materials into art, and which has since become a centre for cultural production. The square is a quiet residential space; the Friche is a ten-minute walk away.
