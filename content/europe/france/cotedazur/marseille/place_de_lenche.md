The Greeks who founded Massalia around 600 BCE needed an agora — a public square for assembly, commerce, and argument. Archaeologists haven't pinned down the exact location, but the evidence points here: the topography matches, the elevation above the original harbour is right, and this is where the earliest strata keep turning up. You might be standing on the oldest public square in France.

Today it's café terraces and the occasional tourist who climbed the steep streets of Le Panier. The Théâtre de Lenche occupies a converted fish market on the western edge — contemporary productions in a neighbourhood that's been staging scenes for two and a half millennia. Order a coffee and look south: the Fort Saint-Jean and the MuCEM's latticed concrete sit in the same sightline — the ancient harbour and its 21st-century reinvention at once.

The Phocaean Greeks who built this city eventually gave their name to Marseille's motto. The square they built their city around probably looked something like this: a high point above the water, a clear line of sight to the sea.
