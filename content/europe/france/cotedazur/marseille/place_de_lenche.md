Place de Lenche sits in the heart of Le Panier, Marseille's oldest quarter, on ground that may be the most historically layered in France. Archaeological investigation of the surrounding streets — the excavations beneath the Jardin des Vestiges and the Musée d'Histoire de Marseille near the Vieux-Port — has confirmed Greek and early Roman occupation at this level of the city, and the weight of scholarly interpretation places the agora of ancient Massalia in this area. The agora was the central public space of a Greek polis: the place for assembly, commerce, debate, and the daily encounter of citizens. A small piazza where Marseillais now drink their morning coffee.

The colony of Massalia was founded around 600 BCE by Phocaean Greeks from Anatolia — the same people whose name survives in Marseille's official motto: Massalia then Massilia then Marseille. The city they built was the first urban settlement in what would become France, and its agora was the first public square in Gallic territory. Whether the precise alignment of the original space corresponds to the present Place de Lenche cannot be confirmed with certainty, but the topographic position — on a rise above the original harbour, within the walled area of the early colony — is consistent with the archaeological evidence.

The Théâtre de Lenche occupies a converted fish market on the western edge of the square, staging contemporary theatre in a neighbourhood that functions simultaneously as a tourist attraction and a genuine residential community. The café terraces fill in summer with a mixture of Le Panier residents and visitors who have climbed the steep streets from the Vieux-Port. The view south over the harbour from the square's upper edge takes in the Fort Saint-Jean and, beyond it, the MuCEM's concrete lattice — the ancient harbour and its 21st-century reinvention in the same sightline.
