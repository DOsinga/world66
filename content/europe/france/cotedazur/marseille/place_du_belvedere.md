Place du Belvédère takes its name from the belvédère — the viewpoint, the overlook — which in Marseille can be a precise description rather than an aspirational one. The city's topography is relentlessly uneven: limestone ridges, valleys, and elevated plateaux appear at intervals throughout the urban fabric, and a square positioned on a ridge or rise in the northern arrondissements can offer views that extend across the city toward the sea and the islands, even deep in residential territory that tourists never visit.

The northern arrondissements where this square sits — between the city centre and the suburban fringe — are everyday Marseille: apartment buildings from the 1960s and 1970s, neighbourhood bakeries, pharmacies, and bars. The name Belvédère functions here less as a boast than as a straightforward topographic label of the kind that appears throughout the city, where high and low, ridge and valley, are still the most natural way to describe where you are.

Squares named Belvédère in French cities often mark former garden terraces or promenades where 19th-century urban development included a public belvedere as civic amenity. Whether this one had such an origin — a formal terrace with iron railings overlooking a planted slope — or whether it simply describes the local terrain, it remains a square where the orientation of the city is perceptible in the way the ground rises away from the streets below.
