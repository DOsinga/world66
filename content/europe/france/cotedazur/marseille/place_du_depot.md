Place du Dépôt sits in the northern districts of Marseille, in a neighbourhood shaped by decades of public transit infrastructure. The *dépôt* in question was a tram depot — one of the maintenance yards that kept Marseille's extensive tramway network running through the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. When the trams were withdrawn in the 1950s, the depots were repurposed or demolished, but the squares and street names that grew up around them survived as quiet memorials to the age of iron rails.

The place itself is modest: a pause in the street grid rather than a grand civic statement, with a few plane trees offering shade and benches where residents wait for buses on routes that trace, roughly, the lines the trams once followed. The surrounding streets mix residential blocks with small workshops and repair garages — trades that have always clustered near transit yards wherever they existed.

Marseille's tramway returned in 2007 on two central lines, and the city has been expanding the network since. The Dépôt square, named for the infrastructure that came and went, now sits just beyond the modern network's reach — a quiet corner that reminds you how much of urban geography is written by the machinery of getting around.
