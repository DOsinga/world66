In the eastern reaches of Marseille, the street plan has to negotiate with geology. Limestone outcrops surface between the houses. Retaining walls are cut straight into pale rock. The rocher that named this square was probably a specific crag that defined a corner of the neighbourhood before the streets arrived — or simply an acknowledgment that in this part of the city, stone is always underfoot and often overhead.

The surrounding district has the feel of incremental growth: houses added to houses, gardens filling the gaps between rock and road. Walking toward the Calanques National Park from here, the geology shifts from urban substrate to landscape feature in a matter of streets.

In a city built almost entirely on limestone, naming a square after a rock is not a modest choice. It is naming the thing the whole place is made of.
