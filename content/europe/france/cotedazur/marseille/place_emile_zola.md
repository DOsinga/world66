Émile Zola was born in Paris in 1840 but grew up in Aix-en-Provence, just 30 kilometres north of Marseille, where his father François — an Italian-born civil engineer — had come to build a canal. The Zola Canal still supplies water to Aix today, and the novelist's childhood in Provence left a permanent mark on his imagination. Several of his Rougon-Macquart novels are set thinly disguised versions of Aix and its surroundings, which he called Plassans.

Place Émile Zola sits in the residential northeastern quarter, a district of apartment blocks and small commerce that is far removed from the tourist circuit. The square functions as a local gathering point — a place to sit, argue, and let children run between the benches while adults keep half an eye on them. Streets named after writers tend to anchor neighbourhoods that are proud of their cultural references without making a show of it.

The square is not a destination in itself, but Zola's name on the street sign gives it a certain weight. His 1898 open letter *J'accuse*, published to defend Alfred Dreyfus against a wrongful treason conviction, is one of the most consequential pieces of journalism in French history. Cities across France named streets and squares after him partly in recognition of that act, as much as his literature.
