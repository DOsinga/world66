François Mireur (1770–1798) was a medical student and Revolutionary enthusiast from Castellane, in the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, who came to Marseille in 1792. At a Republican banquet that year he sang a new song he had brought from Strasbourg — the *Chant de guerre pour l'armée du Rhin*, composed by Rouget de Lisle. The Marseille volunteers adopted it as their marching song and carried it north to Paris, where the crowds heard them singing it in the streets. The song was renamed La Marseillaise.

Mireur went on to serve as a military surgeon and then general in Napoleon's armies. He was killed at the age of 28 during the Egyptian campaign in 1798. His brief life crossed one of the more improbable hinge points in French cultural history — the moment when a local military march became a national anthem — and the city has remembered him for it.

The square sits in the central arrondissements, in a neighbourhood that was already dense and commercial in Mireur's time. The grid of streets here preserves traces of the nineteenth-century city built over the medieval fabric, and the names on the street signs do much of the historical work that plaques and monuments do elsewhere. Mireur's name here is a reminder that the anthem was not composed in Marseille but adopted and transmitted by the city — which in this case amounts to the same thing.
