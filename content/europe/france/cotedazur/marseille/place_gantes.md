Place Gantès sits in the neighbourhood that rises from the Canebière toward the Palais Longchamp, the 19th-century ensemble of museum and park that marks the arrival point of the Canal de Marseille. This part of the 1st arrondissement was developed during the Second Empire and Third Republic as a prosperous residential district, and its street names reflect the families and institutions that shaped the bourgeois city.

The Gantès name belongs to Provence's administrative and professional class — the magistrates, notaries, and landowners whose fortunes were tied to the port city's commercial expansion. Streets and squares bearing such names were often established in recognition of civic services, donations to municipal institutions, or simply the presence of prominent households at the addresses concerned.

The square today is a modest space in a neighbourhood that has retained much of its nineteenth-century architectural fabric: tall limestone apartment buildings with wrought-iron balconies, ground-floor commerce, and the particular sound ecology of a city centre that is busy but not overwhelmed. The Réformés church — the neo-Gothic landmark visible from the Canebière — is a few minutes' walk downhill, and the Longchamp park a few minutes uphill, placing Place Gantès in the middle of a quietly dignified residential axis.
