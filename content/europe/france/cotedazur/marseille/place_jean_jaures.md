The name Jean Jaurès is on the official signs; nobody uses it. The square has been La Plaine for as long as anyone can remember, and the neighbourhood identifies itself that way regardless of the formal designation. Jaurès — the Socialist leader and pacifist assassinated in Paris on 31 July 1914, two days before France declared war — is commemorated across France with civic loyalty. Here his name was installed but the place was already its own thing.

The square hosts markets on Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday mornings — not a tourist market but a working local one, with fruit and vegetables, North African produce, secondhand goods, and books. Cours Julien begins immediately to the north, with its cafes, record shops, murals, and the density of bars that makes the area Marseille's main alternative nightlife district. The trees provide shade in summer; in winter the market operates in the grey Mediterranean light that makes Marseille more honest than it looks in the photographs.

The square was reshaped in the Haussmann period in the 1860s and has resisted several subsequent attempts at redevelopment, including a 20th-century proposal to convert it into a car park — an outcome that local residents successfully fought off. The functional contrast with the Vieux-Port two kilometres to the west is total: where the port is the tourist's Marseille, La Plaine is where people who live there go.
