Jean-Joseph Espercieux (1757–1840) was born in Marseille and became one of the foremost French sculptors of the Neoclassical period. He won the Prix de Rome in 1785, spent years studying in Italy, and on his return established himself in Paris as a sculptor of official monuments. His work appears on the Arc de Triomphe — the bas-reliefs and decorative figures that most visitors walk past without reading the attribution — and on several civic monuments and church interiors across France. He eventually became a professor at the École des Beaux-Arts.

Espercieux belongs to the generation of French artists shaped by the encounter with Roman antiquity and then reshaped by the demands of Revolutionary and Napoleonic patronage. His career followed the political weather: Roman civic virtue under the Republic, Imperial grandeur under Napoleon, restored classicism under the Bourbons. Sculptors of his period were required to be flexible.

The square named for him sits in the neighbourhood between the Réformés church and the Palais Longchamp — an area that was among Marseille's more prosperous residential quarters in the nineteenth century, and that still carries some of that character in its buildings if not always its population. Espercieux made his career in Paris but Marseille claimed him as its own, which is how cities treat artists who leave for the capital and succeed there.
