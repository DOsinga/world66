Léon Blum (1872–1950) led France's Popular Front government from 1936 to 1937, one of the most consequential administrations in the history of the Republic. In a single year his government introduced the 40-hour working week, paid annual holidays for all workers, and the right to collective bargaining — changes that restructured the relationship between labour and capital in France and whose effects persist to the present day. For the French left, the Popular Front summer of 1936, with its factory occupations and paid holidays, is a founding myth.

Blum was Jewish, which made him a target from the beginning. In 1936, as he was about to take office, a crowd of right-wing demonstrators attacked his car and beat him severely. During the Occupation the Vichy regime tried him for treason — the trial was eventually suspended because the evidence refused to cooperate — and deported him to Buchenwald and then Dachau. He survived. After liberation he returned to politics, leading a caretaker government in 1946–47 and remaining a significant voice in the socialist movement until his death.

Squares named after Blum are disproportionately common in left-leaning municipalities, and Marseille's relationship with the left has been complicated — the city was dominated for decades by the Gaston Defferre machine, which was Socialist in name and deeply clientelist in practice. A square named Léon Blum here is a gesture toward the ideological tradition rather than a statement about the local political reality.
