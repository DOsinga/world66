One year in power and Léon Blum's government changed France permanently: the 40-hour working week, paid annual leave for all workers, the right to collective bargaining. The Popular Front summer of 1936 is a founding myth for the French left — factory occupations, workers at the beach for the first time with paid leave, the sense that something had actually shifted.

Blum was Jewish. Before he even took office, right-wing demonstrators attacked his car and beat him in the street. During the Occupation, Vichy put him on trial for treason — the trial collapsed because the evidence wouldn't cooperate — then deported him to Buchenwald and Dachau. He survived. He came back, led a caretaker government in 1946–47, and kept working until his death in 1950.

Squares named Léon Blum cluster in left-leaning municipalities across France. In Marseille — where the Socialist machine under Gaston Defferre was as much clientelist as ideological — this one is a statement of principle rather than party loyalty.
