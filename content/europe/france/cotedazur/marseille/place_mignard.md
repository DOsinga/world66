Pierre Mignard (1612–1695) was one of the most celebrated French painters of the seventeenth century — a portraitist, fresco painter, and court artist whose career played out in parallel with, and eventually in competition with, Charles Le Brun, the dominant figure of French academic painting under Louis XIV. Mignard spent much of his career in Rome before being recalled to France, and when Le Brun died in 1690, Mignard succeeded him as First Painter to the King. He was also made director of the Académie Royale de Peinture.

His portraits of the French court — of Molière, of Cardinal Mazarin, of the Duchesse de Fontanges — defined a certain official image of the age. His large allegorical fresco for the dome of the Val-de-Grâce in Paris is among the most ambitious decorative commissions of French Baroque painting. Molière, who was Mignard's friend, wrote a poem praising it: a painter celebrated in verse by the greatest playwright of the period is a measure of where Mignard stood in his time.

Place Mignard sits in the quiet residential southern district, where the city transitions from dense apartments to individual houses and the pace of life slows considerably. Names from the French artistic tradition appear throughout Marseille's street grid — the city has always positioned itself as a cultural capital as well as a commercial one — and Mignard belongs to the stratum of figures respected enough for a square but not famous enough to appear on the major tourist maps.
