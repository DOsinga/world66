Pierre Roux is a name carried by several figures in Marseille's history — the most significant being Pierre Roux-Alphéran (1778–1858), a local historian and archivist who spent decades documenting the city's streets, buildings, and topography. His work *Les rues de Marseille*, published in 1846–48, is still consulted by researchers tracing the evolution of the urban fabric: a meticulous inventory of what was where and what had stood there before, written by someone who understood that cities have layers and that the layers matter.

The square sits in the 9th arrondissement, the large residential district south of the Prado that developed substantially in the postwar decades. In a neighbourhood built largely after the Second World War, a name from the mid-nineteenth century performs a particular function: it anchors a recently built environment in a longer history, claiming continuity with the pre-war city even where the architecture does not.

The immediate setting is resolutely residential — streets of apartments and individual houses, local commerce serving the immediate neighbourhood, the rhythms of daily life that persist regardless of what name appears on the municipal sign. The square is where the neighbourhood pauses: a bench, some shade if there are trees, a moment between errands.
