Place Vasarely sits in the northeastern residential arrondissements, in a part of Marseille where postwar urban development produced the broad streets and apartment blocks that characterise much of the city east of the centre. The square takes its name from Victor Vasarely (1906–1997), the Hungarian-born painter who became the founding figure of Op Art — kinetic and optical abstraction using geometric forms and colour contrast to produce the illusion of movement on a flat surface.

Vasarely settled in France in 1930, working initially in commercial graphics before developing his distinctive visual language in the 1940s and 1950s. He established his Fondation Vasarely in Aix-en-Provence in 1976 — a building designed in collaboration with architect Jean Sonnier, a series of hexagonal black-and-white towers that is itself one of the most distinctive works of architectural art in France. Vasarely's ambition was not just to make gallery art but to integrate optical art into architecture and public space, and the foundation was the fullest expression of that programme. The drive from Marseille to Aix passes close enough to the foundation that the building is visible from the motorway.

Naming a square in northeastern Marseille after Vasarely places him in the tradition of civic commemoration that runs through the city's street names — a tradition that acknowledges artists and cultural figures alongside the politicians, military officers, and local worthies who dominate French toponymy. In a neighbourhood built in the decades when Vasarely's work was internationally celebrated, the name carries its moment: the optimistic, geometrically ordered vision of postwar modernism applied to the public space of an expanding city.
