Place Victor Hugo occupies a residential block in the 4th arrondissement, a few minutes from the Cours Julien and the tram lines that connect the city centre to the northern quarters. Like the dozens of streets and squares across France that carry Hugo's name, this one speaks less to any personal connection between the writer and Marseille than to the naming conventions of the Third Republic — the period after 1870 when French municipalities systematically replaced imperial and royalist street names with republican heroes.

Victor Hugo was the pre-eminent candidate: a poet, dramatist, and novelist whose opposition to Napoleon III had made him an exile in Guernsey for nineteen years, and who returned to Paris in 1870 as a living symbol of the republic's defeat of empire. He was already a monument before his death in 1885, and his funeral — one of the largest public gatherings in French history — confirmed his status as the republic's secular saint. Streets named Victor Hugo were being planted across the country while he was still alive.

In Marseille, the name attaches to a neighbourhood square of the kind that anchors an urban block without calling attention to itself: plane trees, possibly a fountain or a bench cluster, a tabac or a small bar at the corner. The neighbourhood around Place Victor Hugo is a working residential district, denser and less touristic than the Vieux-Port or Le Panier, with the mix of older apartment stock and small commerce that gives this part of the city its unhurried character.
