Victor Hugo's funeral in 1885 was one of the largest public gatherings in French history. But French cities didn't wait for him to die — they were planting his name on streets and squares while he was still breathing. He'd spent nineteen years in exile in Guernsey for opposing Napoleon III, came home in 1870 as the republic's living symbol, and the Third Republic needed heroes. Hugo was the obvious one.

This square in the 4th arrondissement inherits that tradition without any personal connection to the writer. Hugo didn't come here. He didn't write about Marseille. The square simply tells you when France decided what it believed in: plane trees, a corner tabac, and a name that doubles as a political statement.
