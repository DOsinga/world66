The Plages Gaston Defferre are Marseille's main public beaches, stretching south from the Prado roundabout along a two-kilometre arc of reclaimed seafront. They exist because of a remarkable act of civic engineering: in the 1970s, rubble and fill from excavations in the Vieux-Port and the city centre were used to push back the sea and create a broad sandy shoreline where before there had been rocky foreshore and coastal road. The beaches opened in 1984 and were named, on Defferre's death in 1986, after the man who had governed Marseille for thirty-three years.

Gaston Defferre (1910–1986) was one of the most consequential French politicians of the twentieth century. Elected mayor of Marseille in 1953, he held the post without interruption until his death — the longest mayoral tenure of any major French city in the modern era. A Socialist and a Résistant, he served as a minister under de Gaulle, Pompidou's opponents, and finally under Mitterrand, whose interior minister he became in 1981. His signature achievement as minister was the *Loi Defferre* of 1982, France's decentralisation law, which transferred real executive power from prefects appointed in Paris to elected regional and departmental councils. For a country with a centuries-old tradition of Jacobin centralisation, it was a constitutional revolution.

The beaches that bear his name are among the most democratic spaces in Marseille: free, well-served by buses and the Prado Metro station, and crowded from June through September with the full cross-section of the city. Volleyball nets, a water-sports area, children's play zones, and a small nautical club share the shoreline with families who simply lay out towels on the sand. The sea here faces southwest, toward the open Mediterranean, and the sunsets behind the Frioul islands are worth staying for.
