Construction on the Porte d'Aix began under the Ancien Régime, stalled when the planned campaign against Austria collapsed, resumed under Napoleon, and was finally completed in 1839 under Louis-Philippe — a generation after the empire it celebrated had ended. Architect Michel-Robert Penchaud modelled it on the Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel; the bas-reliefs depicting the Battle of Austerlitz and the crossing of the Rhine were carved as period pieces for a vanished regime. The formal name — Arc de Triomphe de Marseille — never displaced the popular one.

The arch stands at the northern end of the Cours Belsunce, the point where the ancient Via Aurelia entered Massalia from Rome. For two millennia this was the road north from the city: the gateway used by traders, soldiers, and pilgrims moving between Marseille and Italy. The arch inherits that threshold function. The pavement below it has been a main artery since before the city was French.

Today it sits on a traffic island at the junction of the Cours Belsunce and the boulevard de la Madeleine, surrounded by the dense commercial quarter that North African and Comorian communities have made their own since the 1960s. Spice merchants, telephone-card sellers, and halal butchers trade in the shadow of Napoleonic bas-reliefs. The contrast — imperial monument, immigrant neighbourhood — is one of Marseille's more eloquent accidental juxtapositions.
