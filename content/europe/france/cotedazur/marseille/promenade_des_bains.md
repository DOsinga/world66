The Promenade des Bains belongs to an era when entering the sea was a medical act as much as a pleasure. In the nineteenth century, sea bathing swept through bourgeois Europe as a fashionable therapy: physicians prescribed saltwater immersion for nervous complaints, respiratory ailments, and the vague category of disorders grouped under *neurasthenia*. Marseille, already France's largest Mediterranean port and a city whose climate was praised by northern European doctors, became a destination for health-seeking visitors who arrived by the new railway and proceeded directly to the therapeutic foreshore.

This promenade, in the northern coastal districts where the coastline opens out toward the Estaque, served the bathing establishments that sprang up along the Marseille shore from the 1840s onward. The *bains* — bathing houses — were substantial wooden structures built on piers over the water, divided by gender and class, equipped with changing cabins and attendants who guided bathers into the sea. They were not for swimming in the modern sense: the prescribed treatment involved immersion, guided by a schedule, supervised by attendants, and followed by rest. The promenade connecting them was where bathers took their constitutionals between sessions.

The bathing establishments are long gone, replaced by modern waterfront infrastructure, but the promenade retains its name and its orientation toward the sea. The stretch of coast here looks across toward the limestone cliffs of the Chaîne de l'Estaque and the small islands beyond — views that nineteenth-century visitors would have contemplated from the same vantage point, between dips, convinced that the Mediterranean air and water were restoring them to health.
