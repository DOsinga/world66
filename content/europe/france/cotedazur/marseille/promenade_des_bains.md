In the nineteenth century, entering the sea was medicine. Physicians across Europe prescribed saltwater immersion for nervous complaints, respiratory ailments, and the catch-all diagnosis of neurasthenia. Marseille, with its railway connection and celebrated climate, drew health-seeking visitors who came directly to the therapeutic foreshore.

The bathing houses here were substantial wooden structures on piers over the water, divided by gender and class, with attendants who guided bathers in on a doctor's schedule. You didn't go for a swim — you went for a treatment. The promenade connected the establishments; bathers took their constitutionals between supervised dips.

The bathhouses are long gone. The coast here still looks across toward the limestone cliffs of the Chaîne de l'Estaque — the same view those nineteenth-century patients contemplated between sessions, convinced the Mediterranean air was rebuilding them from the inside out.
