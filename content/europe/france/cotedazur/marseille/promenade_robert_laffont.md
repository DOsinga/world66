Robert Laffont (1916–2021) was born in Marseille and went on to become the most consequential independent publisher in twentieth-century France. He founded Éditions Robert Laffont in 1941 in occupied France — an act of cultural confidence in the darkest of times — and over the next eighty years built a house that published everyone from Papillon (Henri Charrière's memoir of Devil's Island) to Boris Vian to a succession of major foreign authors in translation. The list was deliberately eclectic: Laffont believed that a great publisher should follow his curiosity rather than a formula, and the catalogue reflected a lifelong appetite for adventure, literature, and ideas from beyond France's borders.

Laffont remained active as a publisher into his late eighties, one of the last representatives of the era when French publishing was dominated by individual personalities with strong editorial visions rather than by corporate conglomerates. He died in 2021 at the age of 104, having seen the literary world he helped shape transformed almost beyond recognition by digital technology and media consolidation. His publishing house survived him, absorbed into the Editis group but still bearing his name — and still, occasionally, finding the unexpected book that becomes a phenomenon.

Marseille named this promenade in his honour to acknowledge a native son who exported the city's restless, outward-looking energy to the world of books. The promenade runs near the western waterfront, in the neighbourhood of new developments and cultural institutions that Marseille has been building since its 2013 year as European Capital of Culture — a fitting location for a tribute to someone who spent his life arguing that good books could change how people see the world.
