Before Marseille there was Massalia — a Greek colonial city founded around 600 BCE. In the fourth century BCE, two of its citizens sailed in opposite directions and came back with stories their contemporaries refused to believe.

Pytheas went north, past Spain and Britain, into waters no Mediterranean navigator had charted. He returned with accounts of a land called Thule — barely any night in summer, the sea itself solidifying at the edges of the world. His contemporaries called him a liar. Modern scholars identify his Thule as Norway, Iceland, or Shetland. He was describing sea ice and the midnight sun. He was right.

Euthymenes sailed south along the African coast and reached a great tidal river teeming with crocodiles — almost certainly the Senegal. He was wrong about it being a source of the Nile. He was right about the tides, which baffled Greek philosophers who believed the outer ocean was tideless. From the same small harbour, in the same generation: the first documented European observations of the Arctic and of tropical West Africa.
