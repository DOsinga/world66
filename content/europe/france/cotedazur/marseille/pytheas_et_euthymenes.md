Before Marseille was Marseille it was Massalia, a Greek colonial city founded around 600 BCE, and from that city in the fourth century BCE two explorers departed in opposite directions and came back with accounts that the ancient world found almost too extraordinary to believe. Pytheas sailed north, past Spain and the British coast, through waters no Mediterranean navigator had charted, and returned with descriptions of an island he called Thule — a land where the sun barely set in summer and where the sea itself seemed to solidify at the edges of the world. His contemporaries thought he was lying. Modern scholarship identifies his Thule as somewhere in Norway, Iceland, or possibly Shetland; his description of sea ice and midnight sun is now understood as accurate observation.

Euthymenes sailed south along the African coast at roughly the same period, reaching a great river teeming with crocodiles that he identified as a source of the Nile — almost certainly the Senegal River. His account of tidal ocean waters baffled Greek philosophers who believed the outer ocean was tideless; he was right about the tides and wrong about the Nile. Between the two of them, Massalia could claim the first documented European observations of the Arctic and the first documented European exploration of tropical West Africa — two journeys from the same small harbour, in the same generation.

The monument stands as a declaration of civic priority: Marseille's claim to be not just old but formative in the story of human exploration. Most cities along this coast point to their Roman inheritance; Marseille points further back, to the Greek scientists who looked at the edges of the known world and decided to go and see.
