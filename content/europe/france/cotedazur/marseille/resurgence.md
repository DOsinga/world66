A *résurgence* is the point where underground water, having traveled through cavities and fissures in karstic rock, re-emerges at the surface as a spring. Marseille sits on and around some of France's most dramatic limestone geology — the white Jurassic calcaire that forms the calanques and the hills ringing the city — and underground water systems threading through this rock are a defining feature of the landscape. Several such resurgences exist in and around the city, points where the interior aquifer finds its way to daylight.

At these coordinates in eastern Marseille, the name *Résurgence* may mark a natural spring, a water feature built to acknowledge or channelled from one, or a public artwork that takes the geological phenomenon as its theme. Public spaces across southern France often incorporate references to water — fountains, basins, plaques — both for practical reasons and as a form of respect for a resource that was historically precious in a dry climate.

The eastern districts of Marseille, spreading towards the limestone massifs of Saint-Cyr and the Plan-de-Cuques plateau, retain a more rugged, semi-rural character than the city centre. Here the geology is closer to the surface and the connection between the built environment and the underlying rock is easier to feel. Whether *Résurgence* here is a spring, a sculpture, or simply a name on a building, it acknowledges something true about this particular patch of Provençal ground.
