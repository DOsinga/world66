Rue Corneille honours Pierre Corneille (1606–1684), the Norman playwright who is generally regarded as the father of French classical tragedy. His 1637 masterpiece *Le Cid* — a drama of honour, duty, and impossible love set in medieval Spain — caused a sensation in Paris and sparked a furious literary debate known as the Querelle du Cid. The Académie française weighed in; Cardinal Richelieu, who had his own theatrical ambitions, was reportedly displeased. None of it dimmed the play's success: it has been in continuous repertoire ever since and is still performed today.

Corneille went on to write *Horace*, *Cinna*, and *Polyeucte*, consolidating a body of work that defined the heroic dilemmas French theatre would wrestle with for the next century and a half. He was eventually eclipsed in fashionable esteem by the younger Racine, but his reputation never truly faded. Streets named after Corneille appear in virtually every French city — a quiet, omnipresent tribute to the playwright who first convinced Paris that tragedy could be written in French rather than Latin.

Marseille's Rue Corneille sits in the southern part of the city, a residential street where the name above the sign is more famous than anything visible from the pavement. It is the kind of place that rewards a slow walk: balconied apartment buildings, the smell of diesel from a passing bus, a cat on a ledge. The heroic grandeur is all in the name.
