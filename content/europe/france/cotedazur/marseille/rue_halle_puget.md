The Rue Halle Puget takes its name from the Halle Puget, a covered market hall that once stood nearby and that commemorated Pierre Puget (1620–1694), Marseille's most celebrated sculptor and one of the greatest artists of the French Baroque. Born in the city and trained in Rome and Florence before returning to work in Toulon and Marseille, Puget left his mark on the region in stone — his masterpiece, the *Milon de Crotone*, now hangs in the Louvre — and his name was given to civic buildings and public spaces in his home city as a permanent act of local pride.

The Halle Puget itself was a nineteenth-century covered market, the kind of iron-and-glass structure that transformed French urban food retail in the decades after Baltard's famous Les Halles in Paris set the template. Marseille built several such markets as the city's population expanded rapidly, and the Halle Puget served the residents of its neighbourhood with fish, produce, and provisions under a roof that kept buyers and sellers out of the Provençal summer heat. Whether the hall itself survives or has been converted or replaced, the street that bore its name continues to link Puget's reputation to this quarter of the city.

Walking this part of central Marseille, between the Vieux-Port and the higher ground of Le Panier, is to move through layers of naming — streets, squares, and markets carrying the names of artists, soldiers, and civic figures whose contributions were deemed worth remembering. Pierre Puget belongs to the first rank of these, a Marseillais who achieved international recognition at the height of Louis XIV's France.
