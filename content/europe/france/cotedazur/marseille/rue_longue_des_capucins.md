Rue Longue des Capucins runs through the Noailles neighbourhood, the district just east of the Vieux-Port that has been the centre of Marseille's North African community since the postwar period. The street takes its name from a Capuchin convent that stood here until the Revolution; the "long street of the Capuchins" is now the main artery of a spice market district that has no equivalent in scale or intensity anywhere in France outside Paris.

The street and its immediate neighbours host the most concentrated collection of aromatic ingredient shops in the country: spice merchants with open sacks of cumin, coriander, ras el hanout, and sumac; dried fruit traders with dates, figs, and apricots piled in pyramids; tea importers selling mint and jasmine alongside imported varieties from Morocco and Tunisia; rose water suppliers; and sellers of dried flowers used in both cooking and folk medicine. The smell reaches adjacent streets before you arrive — a mixture of cumin and rose water that is identifiable from thirty metres away on a still morning.

Noailles is sometimes called the "belly of Marseille," a reference to Zola's description of Les Halles in Paris as the belly of France. The market here is less formal than a covered hall: much of the trade happens from street-level displays in front of small shops, with the transaction conducted across a pavement covered in a shifting arrangement of baskets and boxes. The Marché des Capucins, just off the rue at the Place du Marché des Capucins, extends the same sensibility to fresh produce. The neighbourhood is also the best place in Marseille to find the ingredients for a proper harissa or chermoula, sold fresh and ground to order.
