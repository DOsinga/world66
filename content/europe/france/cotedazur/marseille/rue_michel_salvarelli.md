This street runs near the edge of Le Panier, Marseille's oldest neighbourhood, in the lanes that climb from the Vieux-Port toward the hill of la Croix. The name Salvarelli points south and east — Corsica, the Italian peninsula — exactly the regions that sent waves of migrants to Marseille over two centuries of port-driven growth. Families with names like this got woven deep into the city's fabric: its trades, its politics, its street committees.

Michel Salvarelli earned a named street through something specific — wartime service, public office, a neighbourhood contribution that outlasted him. The streets around Le Panier are thick with names like his: men and women whose importance was local rather than national, remembered in stone because someone decided they should be.

Walk the street and you get the everyday texture of one of France's oldest urban quarters: worn steps, shuttered windows, the smell of a bakery somewhere below, and the constant noise of the city pressing in from all sides.
