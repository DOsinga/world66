Rue Michel Salvarelli runs near the edge of Le Panier, Marseille's oldest neighbourhood, in the densely layered streets that climb away from the Vieux-Port towards the hill of la Croix. The surname Salvarelli has roots in Corsica and the Italian peninsula — both regions that sent large numbers of migrants to Marseille throughout the eighteenth, nineteenth, and early twentieth centuries, drawn by the port's commercial activity and the opportunities of a fast-growing city. Families with names of this origin became deeply woven into Marseille's fabric, contributing to its trades, its politics, and its civic life across generations.

Michel Salvarelli would have been remembered by the city's street-naming committee for some act of particular local significance — wartime service, public office, or a contribution to the neighbourhood that outlived him. The streets around Le Panier carry the names of many such figures, men and women whose importance was neighbourhood-scale rather than nationally celebrated, and whose commemoration in stone keeps a granular history of the city alive even as the population changes and building facades are renovated.

The street itself is part of the working urban fabric of this part of Marseille, connecting the lanes of Le Panier to the wider street grid. Walking it, visitors encounter the everyday texture of one of France's oldest urban neighbourhoods: worn stone steps, shuttered windows, the occasional whiff of a bakery, and the persistent sound of the city below.
