Jerusalem's Old City has been defined by its gates for millennia. The Ottoman walls built by Suleiman the Magnificent in the 1530s enclosed the city through seven historic entrances: the Jaffa Gate, Damascus Gate, Herod's Gate, Lions' Gate, Golden Gate (sealed), Dung Gate, and Zion Gate. Each gateway accumulated its own history — of pilgrims, conquests, processions, and everyday commerce — until the gates became inseparable from the city's identity. A new gate, the New Gate, was cut through the walls in 1889 to ease access to the growing Christian Quarter, but the seven original gates remain the ones that carry the weight of sacred geography.

*Sept Portes de Jérusalem* (Seven Gates of Jerusalem) brings that symbolism to Marseille, a city with centuries-old ties to the eastern Mediterranean and the Levant. Marseille's Armenian community — one of the largest in France, many of them descended from survivors of the 1915 genocide — has long maintained a spiritual and cultural connection to the Holy Land. The city also has a substantial Jewish community with Sephardic roots in North Africa and the Middle East. An artwork invoking Jerusalem's gates speaks to both communities, and to the broader Marseillais tradition of a port city that has always been a point of arrival for people carrying memories of other holy places.

The installation sits in the southern arrondissements, where the city's Mediterranean character is most concentrated. Whatever its precise form — sculpture, architectural fragment, or symbolic marker — it asks passersby to hold two cities in mind at once: the Marseille around them and the Jerusalem that remains, for many people in this neighbourhood, a place of profound meaning even across thousands of kilometres of sea and history.
