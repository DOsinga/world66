Soghomon Tehlirian was born in 1897 in the Ottoman Empire and survived the Armenian Genocide of 1915, during which most of his family were killed. In March 1921, he shot and killed Talaat Pasha — the Ottoman interior minister widely held responsible for organizing the deportations and massacres — on a Berlin street in broad daylight. Tehlirian made no attempt to flee, and his subsequent trial became one of the most remarkable legal proceedings of the twentieth century.

At his trial in Berlin, Tehlirian's defence attorneys presented extensive testimony about the genocide itself, effectively putting the Ottoman regime on trial before the court. The jury acquitted Tehlirian after deliberating for just over an hour. The case drew international attention and contributed to the development of legal thinking around what would later be termed crimes against humanity — a concept formalized in the Nuremberg trials twenty-five years later.

Marseille has long been home to one of France's largest Armenian communities. Many families arrived in the years following 1915, finding in Marseille a port city with a tradition of absorbing refugees from the eastern Mediterranean. The memorial near these coordinates stands as a reminder of that community's history and the painful twentieth-century origins of much of the Armenian diaspora in France.
