In 1720, plague arrived in Marseille and killed roughly 50,000 people in two years — about half the population. Almost everyone who could afford to leave, did. Bishop Henri-François Xavier de Belsunce stayed. He organised relief efforts, spent his own fortune on food and medicine, and was seen repeatedly in the streets administering last rites. He became a civic hero regardless of religion.

The cours Belsunce and the district that carries his name aren't what they were in 1720. Today this is the heart of Marseille's North African and West African commercial quarter — market stalls, halal butchers, Arabic bakeries, mobile-phone shops. The economic infrastructure of communities that arrived mostly in the postwar decades.

The bishop who stayed when everyone else fled is, in a neighbourhood that has always been a landing point for new arrivals, not an unsuitable patron for a square.
