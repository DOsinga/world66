Square Belsunce is a small planted garden in the Belsunce district, the dense commercial and residential quarter between the Canebière and the cours Belsunce that has served as the principal entry point for successive waves of immigration to Marseille. Today the district is predominantly North African and West African, with a busy street life of market stalls, halal butchers, Arabic bakeries, and mobile-phone shops — the economic infrastructure of communities that arrived mostly in the postwar decades.

The name commemorates Henri-François Xavier de Belsunce de Castelmoron (1671–1755), who was Bishop of Marseille during the catastrophic plague outbreak of 1720–22. The Grand Peste killed approximately half of Marseille's population — some 50,000 of a city of 90,000 people — in two years. While most of the wealthy fled the city, Belsunce remained. He organised the relief effort, spent his personal fortune on food and medicine for the dying, and was repeatedly seen in the streets administering last rites. His conduct made him a civic hero regardless of religious affiliation, and his name was given to the principal street running through the quarter, the cours Belsunce, and to the district itself.

The square bearing his name sits in the heart of a neighbourhood that has nothing to do with 18th-century Catholicism and everything to do with the continuing history of people coming to Marseille from elsewhere. The bishop who stayed when others left is, in that light, not an unsuitable patron.
