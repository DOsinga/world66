La Verdière is a small village in the Var département, roughly eighty kilometres northeast of Marseille in the hills behind the Luberon. Like many communities across Provence, it was touched by the deportations and reprisals of the Second World War. This square in Marseille bears the name of the village's children — a form of civic memory that was common in the postwar decades, when towns and cities named streets, squares, and parks after communities that had suffered.

The square itself is a modest neighbourhood space, the kind of shaded enclosure with benches and plane trees that gives Marseille's residential arrondissements their character. It lies in a part of the city where the street grid loosens and gardens extend behind stone walls — a quieter Marseille than the one most visitors know.

The naming of public spaces after wartime victims was a deliberate act of urban memory: city councils across France understood that a name on a map, consulted daily by residents, carries a different kind of weight than a plaque on a wall. Walking past the Square des Enfants de la Verdière, most Marseillais today may not know the specific story the name encodes, but the act of commemoration remains in the fabric of the city.
