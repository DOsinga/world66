Édouard Estaunié (1862–1942) led a double life that was unusual even by the standards of the French grandes écoles. By day he was a senior engineer and administrator at the Direction générale des Postes et Télégraphes, eventually rising to director-general of the French telegraph and telephone services. In 1904 he wrote a technical manual in which he introduced the word "télécommunication" — a compound he coined to describe the transmission of signals over distance, whether by telegraph, telephone, or any technology that might follow. The term is now used in essentially every language on earth.

By night, or at least in parallel, Estaunié was a novelist of considerable reputation. He wrote psychological fiction in the tradition of late nineteenth-century realism — quiet, interior novels about solitude, provincial life, and the gap between the self that people present to the world and the self they carry privately. His novels were praised by critics who had no idea he also ran a national communications infrastructure.

This square, tucked into the residential fabric of Marseille's eastern arrondissements, commemorates a man whose technical coinage outlasted his literary reputation by many decades. Few people who send a text message or discuss telecommunications policy know they are using a word invented by a French civil engineer who also wrote novels about loneliness.
