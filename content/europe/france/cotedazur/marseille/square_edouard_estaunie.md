In 1904, Édouard Estaunié wrote a technical manual for the French postal and telegraph service and slipped in a new word: "télécommunication." He meant the transmission of signals over distance — telegraph, telephone, whatever came next. The word is now used in essentially every language on earth.

That would be enough for most people. Estaunié also had a parallel career as a novelist — psychological fiction in the late nineteenth-century realist tradition, quiet books about provincial life and the gap between the self you show the world and the one you carry privately. Critics praised them. He ran France's national communications infrastructure in the same years.

Nobody sending a text message knows they're using a word invented by a French civil engineer who wrote novels about loneliness. This square, in Marseille's residential east, honours the man who coined it.
