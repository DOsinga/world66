Marseille has a long tradition of naming its squares, streets, and public gardens after people who never made the national history books but shaped the life of a particular quartier — a schoolteacher, a local elected official, a doctor who stayed through a hard winter. Étienne Portelli almost certainly belongs to that category: a man of the neighbourhood whose contribution was solid enough to earn this small rectangle of public space his name, even if the wider record of his life has grown thin.

The square sits in the 13th arrondissement, a working and residential district on the city's eastern fringe that lacks the tourist circuits of the Vieux-Port or the Cours Julien but compensates with an unhurried pace and a population that has lived in the same streets for generations. Squares like this one function as the punctuation marks of daily life — somewhere to sit between errands, somewhere children can be released briefly from walking in a straight line, somewhere neighbours can stop and talk without standing in anyone's way.

There is not much to see here in the way of monuments or landmarks, which is rather the point. The square does what neighbourhood squares are supposed to do: it provides a pause, a patch of shade, a bench. Visitors who find themselves in the 13th for reasons of their own — catching a bus, visiting family, exploring the market at La Capelette nearby — may pass through it without noticing the name on the sign. That is a perfectly acceptable way to use a square named after Étienne Portelli.
