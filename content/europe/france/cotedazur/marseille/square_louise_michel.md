Louise Michel trained as a schoolteacher and arrived in Paris in time to become a central figure in the Paris Commune of 1871 — the workers' insurrection that held the city for 72 days before the army crushed it. She commanded a barricade in Montmartre. At her military trial she demanded the death sentence rather than imprisonment. They sent her to New Caledonia instead.

She spent seven years in the Pacific, teaching Kanak children and learning to take indigenous resistance to French colonialism seriously — something almost no deportee managed. When she was amnestied in 1880 she came back as a celebrity radical. She spent the rest of her life writing poetry, novels, and memoirs, and speaking at the cost of repeated imprisonment.

She died in Marseille in 1905, which is why her name attaches to this city's public spaces with particular weight. The square is modest — a few benches, some shade, a quiet residential street around it. The "Red Virgin of Montmartre" was a schoolteacher before she was a revolutionary. The square suggests those two things were never in contradiction.
