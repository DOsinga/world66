Louise Michel spent her life standing where the authorities told her not to stand. Born in 1830, she trained as a schoolteacher and arrived in Paris in time to participate in the radical politics of the 1860s, becoming a central organizer and fighter during the Paris Commune of 1871 — the workers' insurrection that held the city for 72 days before the French army crushed it in the Bloody Week of May. Michel commanded a barricade in Montmartre and later stood trial before a military court, where she demanded the death sentence rather than imprisonment and was instead deported to New Caledonia.

She spent seven years in the Pacific, teaching Kanak children and learning to respect indigenous resistance to French colonialism in a way that few deportees had managed. Amnestied in 1880, she returned to France as a celebrity radical and spent the rest of her life writing — poetry, novels, memoirs — and speaking, often at the cost of imprisonment. She died in Marseille in 1905, which is one reason her name attaches itself to this city's public spaces with particular weight.

The Square Louise Michel sits in a residential neighbourhood of the city, a modest green space of the kind that French municipalities scatter through their urban grids as gifts to the surrounding streets. The naming is a small act of civic memory: the "Red Virgin of Montmartre" was a schoolteacher before she was a revolutionary, and the square suggests that these two things were never in contradiction.
