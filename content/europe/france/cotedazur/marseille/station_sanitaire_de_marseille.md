Before antibiotics and before the germ theory of disease was widely accepted, the maritime sanitary station at Marseille was Europe's front line against epidemic. Ships arriving from the Levant, North Africa, and South Asia — ports where plague, cholera, and yellow fever were endemic — were required to stop here before any passenger or crew member was permitted ashore. The inspectors who worked at the station held the power to quarantine an entire vessel for weeks, a decision that could ruin a merchant's season and strand passengers in limbo aboard a ship at anchor in the harbour.

Marseille's position as the principal French gateway to the Mediterranean made its sanitary apparatus unusually elaborate. The station developed a systematic protocol: crews were examined, cargo was aired or fumigated, and arriving passengers were classified by their port of origin and the health conditions reported there. Those coming from declared plague ports faced the most stringent conditions. The facility had its own isolation wards, its own medical staff, and its own bureaucracy — a small city within the port devoted entirely to preventing the entry of contagion.

The station's history intersects with some of the great epidemic scares of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including the cholera pandemics that swept across Europe and the outbreaks of bubonic plague that periodically alarmed Mediterranean cities. Today the site is a reminder of how seriously port cities once took the connection between maritime trade and disease — a connection that the world relearned, briefly but powerfully, in the early 2020s.
