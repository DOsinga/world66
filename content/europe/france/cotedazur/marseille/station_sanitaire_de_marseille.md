Before antibiotics, before the germ theory of disease had been proven, Marseille was Europe's front line against epidemic. Ships arriving from the Levant, North Africa, and South Asia — ports where plague, cholera, and yellow fever were endemic — stopped here before anyone was permitted ashore. The inspectors held genuine power: they could quarantine a whole vessel for weeks, ruining a merchant's season and stranding passengers aboard a ship at anchor in the harbour.

The station was elaborate. Crews were examined, cargo was aired or fumigated, passengers were classified by their port of origin and the conditions reported there. Anyone arriving from a declared plague port faced the strictest protocols. The facility had its own isolation wards, its own medical staff, its own bureaucracy — a small city within the port devoted entirely to stopping contagion at the gate.

The history here runs through cholera pandemics and outbreaks of bubonic plague that periodically alarmed Mediterranean cities across the 19th and early 20th centuries. The logic was simple: Marseille was the gateway to France, and the gateway had to be controlled. The world relearned this, briefly and powerfully, in the early 2020s.
