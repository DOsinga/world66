The David shown here is the biblical shepherd-king — the young man who defeated Goliath, the figure whose representation by Michelangelo became so canonical that no subsequent depiction can entirely escape the comparison. Nineteenth-century France returned to David repeatedly: the pose of the vigilant youth with his sling, calm before the confrontation, made him an effective emblem of republican virtue — courage and readiness without imperial swagger. Cities that commissioned civic statuary in the decades of the Third Republic often chose figures from scripture or antiquity whose symbolism was clear and non-partisan.

The statue's position in the 8th arrondissement places it in the bourgeois residential belt south of the city centre, the Marseille of wide boulevards and Second Empire apartment buildings laid out as the city expanded toward the sea. Public sculpture here served a different function from the plague memorials and naval monuments of the Vieux-Port district: it decorated and dignified a neighbourhood rather than marking a site of historical event.

The piece is best approached as an example of the civic-aesthetic vocabulary of its era — the idealised male figure, the smooth stone or bronze, the base that elevates the subject above the pedestrian. It invites comparison with the more assertive public figures elsewhere in the city — the golden Virgin above Notre-Dame de la Garde, the triumphal stairs of the Gare Saint-Charles — as a quieter, more intimate scale of public art.
