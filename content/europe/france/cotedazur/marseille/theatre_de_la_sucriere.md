Sugar was one of the defining commodities of Marseille's colonial port economy. Ships from the French Caribbean islands — Martinique and Guadeloupe principally — brought raw cane sugar to Marseille's quays, where it was unloaded into port warehouses and refined before distribution across southern France. The sucreries, the sugar warehouses and refineries, were physical evidence of the Atlantic trade routes that shaped the city's wealth and its demography.

The Théâtre de la Sucrière occupies one of these former industrial buildings, now converted for performance. The conversion is part of a pattern that has transformed the Joliette and Belle de Mai areas north and east of the old port: nineteenth-century industrial structures — warehouses, workshops, depots — repurposed as theatres, galleries, rehearsal spaces, and cultural centres. The process accelerated in the run-up to Marseille's tenure as European Capital of Culture in 2013.

The theatre retains the spatial character of its industrial origins: high ceilings, a certain rawness of material, and a floor plan that allows flexible staging. Productions here tend toward work that benefits from unconventional space — immersive theatre, large-cast pieces, and events that use the whole volume of the building rather than a conventional stage-and-stalls arrangement.
