Named after the half-man, half-horse creatures of Greek mythology, the Théâtre du Centaure takes its identity from a figure that has always occupied the border between civilisation and wildness. In ancient myth the centaurs were divided: most were violent and unruly, drunk on instinct and appetite, but Chiron was the exception — the centaur who became the wisest teacher in Greece, tutor to Achilles and Asclepius, the one who bridged the animal and the rational. The theatre's name holds both possibilities without resolving them.

Set in the southern reaches of Marseille near the coastline of the 8th arrondissement, the Centaure programs work that leans into physical theatre, circus arts, and performance forms where the body is as much instrument as voice. The centaur is the right emblem for that kind of work: a creature whose power comes from the integration of two natures that logic says should not combine. Productions here tend toward spectacle with intellectual weight, the kind of theatre that makes demands on both the eye and the mind.

Marseille was founded by Greeks who understood the centaur well — Massalia was a colony trading between civilisations, always at the edge of one world and the beginning of another. A theatre carrying that name in this city is not reaching for a random classical reference; it is reaching for something precise about what art does when it is honest about its own contradictions.
