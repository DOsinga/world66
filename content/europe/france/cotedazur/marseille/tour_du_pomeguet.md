The Île de Pomégues is the southern and larger of the two main islands in the Frioul archipelago, which sits roughly four kilometres off Marseille's Vieux-Port in the open water of the Gulf of Lion. The Tour du Pomèguet is one of several fortified structures scattered across these islands, legacies of the centuries during which controlling the approaches to Marseille's harbour meant controlling the islands that guarded them.

The Frioul islands have a layered history of quarantine and military use. From the seventeenth century onward, the archipelago's lazaret — one of the oldest in the Mediterranean — held ships and their passengers suspected of carrying plague. The structures built for isolation and observation give the islands an architectural character quite unlike anything on the mainland: long vaulted buildings, walled enclosures, and watchtowers positioned to see approaching vessels before they reached port. The writer Alexandre Dumas used the Frioul's quarantine fortress as the model for the Château d'If, though the actual Château d'If sits on a separate island nearby.

Today the Frioul islands are accessible by ferry from the Vieux-Port and attract walkers, swimmers, and day-trippers looking for rocky coves and open water. The Tour du Pomèguet and the other fortified remains can be seen on foot across terrain that is scrubby, windswept, and entirely unlike the continental Marseille visible across the water. The islands feel genuinely remote, which is precisely what made them useful as a place to keep infected ships at a safe distance from the city.
