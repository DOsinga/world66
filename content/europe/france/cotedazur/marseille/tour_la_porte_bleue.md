The Tour La Porte Bleue — the Blue Gate Tower — takes its name from a painted or ironwork gate that once marked, or still marks, an entrance to a private estate or residential enclosure in this part of Marseille. The coordinates place it inland from the coast, in the band of the city between the main urban core and the southern arrondissements where large properties with walls and gates were common among the bourgeoisie who built their villas here in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Gates of this kind were the architectural grammar of status in a Mediterranean city where summer heat made enclosed gardens valuable and where the boundary between street and property was a social statement as much as a practical one. A blue gate, in particular, was a common choice — the colour resisting fading in strong sunlight better than others and carrying associations of coolness and the sea that made it popular along the Provençal coast.

The tower element suggests something more substantial than a simple gateway: perhaps a gatehouse with a small tower above it, or a freestanding turret that gave a name to the whole entrance composition. Such features were common affectations of the villa-building period, borrowing the language of fortification for purely decorative purposes. The Blue Gate Tower now lends its name to this corner of the city, surviving as a toponym even if the gate itself has changed or the estate it once enclosed has been subdivided.
