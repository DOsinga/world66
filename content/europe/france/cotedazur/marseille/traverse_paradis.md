Traverse Paradis is one of the named traverses that characterise the pre-Haussmann street plan of Marseille's southern residential arrondissements. These short connecting lanes run between the main north-south arteries — Rue Paradis, Boulevard Périer, Avenue du Prado — linking them across the residential blocks with passageways that are wide enough for foot traffic and occasionally a single car, but never designed for through-traffic. They appear on maps but are rarely signed at street level, which makes them effectively invisible to anyone who doesn't already know they're there.

Rue Paradis, which gives this traverse its name, was named in the 18th century by the English community that settled in the southern part of the city. The British merchants and consular staff who lived in this quarter between roughly 1700 and 1800 left their mark on the area's nomenclature — "Paradis" being a rough translation of their assessment of this part of the Mediterranean coast. The traverse connects the main street to the quieter residential blocks behind it, running through the 8th arrondissement, which is the most affluent of Marseille's residential quarters: wide apartment buildings from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, plane trees, and a population that has remained largely stable and prosperous.

Walking the traverses is the best way to understand Marseille's residential scale south of the Canebière. The city's famous avenues and corniche are one face of Marseille; the traverses — quiet, worn, practical — are another. Traverse Paradis is unremarkable in itself, but that is precisely the point: it is a working piece of urban infrastructure that has quietly served the same neighbourhood for a century and a half, connecting two streets that most visitors would never think to connect.
