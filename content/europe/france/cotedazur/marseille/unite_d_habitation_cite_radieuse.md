Le Corbusier completed the Unité d'Habitation in 1952, after six years of construction delayed by material shortages and political opposition. The concept was total: not a residential building with some amenities, but a self-contained community at a density that freed the ground plane for greenery. The 337 apartments are double-height with interior split levels; the mid-building floor holds shops, a pharmacy, a hotel; the roof carries a running track, a gymnasium, a paddling pool, and a nursery school with a curved concrete wall designed for children to run against.

The building is the foundational text of postwar modernist housing — a prototype that directly influenced social housing construction across Europe, from the *grands ensembles* of France to the tower blocks of Britain and beyond. Whether that influence was beneficial is debated; what is not debated is that this particular building, the original, achieves what the copies generally did not. The quality of construction, the detailing, the relationship between private apartment and collective amenity, hold up 70 years later.

The Cité Radieuse is now a listed historic monument and the apartments change hands at prices that bear no resemblance to the social housing programme it was meant to inspire. The rooftop is accessible to non-residents and is the right place to understand the building: stand on the running track, look at the roof sculptures Le Corbusier designed for the skyline, and consider what a 1950s city planner thought a good life for working people should look like.
