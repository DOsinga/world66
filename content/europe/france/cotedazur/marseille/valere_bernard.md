Valéry Bernard was born in Marseille in 1860, and the city's port — its labour, its smells, its social world of dockers and traders and migrants — was the great subject of his literary life. He wrote in both French and Occitan, and his commitment to the Provençal language placed him within the Félibrige movement that Frédéric Mistral had founded to preserve and revive the literary culture of the south. Bernard was one of the movement's most significant urban voices: where Mistral wrote of the Camargue and the Rhône delta, Bernard wrote of the quays and warehouses and tenement streets of a working Mediterranean port city.

His novels and stories documented a world that was physically present all around him but rarely appeared in French literary fiction: the port workers who loaded and unloaded ships from across the Mediterranean and beyond, the immigrant communities who had settled in Marseille's oldest neighbourhoods, the small traders and craftsmen of the Panier district. His prose was realist and unsentimental, shaped by direct observation rather than the picturesque conventions of Provençal literature.

Bernard died in 1936. His work is little read today outside specialist circles, but it remains historically important as testimony — a record in fiction of the social life of Marseille at a moment of great industrial and demographic change. This monument acknowledges his place in the city's literary tradition and his role in the broader revival of Occitan writing.
