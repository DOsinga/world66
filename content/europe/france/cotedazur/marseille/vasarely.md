Victor Vasarely (1906–1997) was the Hungarian-born French artist who, more than anyone else, invented Op Art — the movement that uses geometric patterns, contrasting colours, and carefully calibrated visual tension to create the impression of movement, depth, and vibration on a static surface. Born in Pécs, he trained in Budapest under a follower of the Bauhaus before moving to Paris in 1930, where he worked as a graphic designer before developing the abstract visual language that would make him famous worldwide in the 1960s and 1970s.

Vasarely spent his later decades in the south of France, and the region around Marseille and Aix-en-Provence became closely associated with his work. The Fondation Vasarely, housed in a striking hexagonal building of his own design on the outskirts of Aix-en-Provence, opened in 1976 and remains the definitive collection of his monumental works. His presence in Marseille reflects the city's long openness to constructivist and geometric abstraction — a tradition rooted in its Mediterranean identity as a place where the clarity of light and the geometry of sea and sky have always encouraged a certain kind of formal rigour.

This work belongs to Vasarely's mature period, when he was producing large-scale public art designed to bring optical experience out of the gallery and into everyday urban life. His belief was that art should be reproducible, democratic, and environmental — integrated into architecture and public space rather than confined to museums. A Vasarely in the street is exactly what he intended: art that makes you stop, look again, and question whether what you are seeing is flat or dimensional, still or moving.
