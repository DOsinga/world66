The Vieux-Port is both the founding site and the living heart of Marseille. Around 600 BCE, Phocaean Greeks from Anatolia sailed into this rectangular inlet and established Massalia on its northern shore. The city that grew from that landing became one of the great ports of the ancient Mediterranean, and the inlet itself has been in continuous use as a working harbour ever since. Every building that surrounds it today is, in some sense, a later chapter of the same story.

The Norman Foster ombrière installed at the Quai des Belges in 2013 — a vast polished steel canopy that mirrors the port and sky below it — marks the moment Marseille turned its waterfront into public space. The 2013 European Capital of Culture programme removed the car traffic that had hemmed in the quays for decades, creating what is now the largest pedestrian waterfront in France. The morning fish market at the Quai des Belges, where local fishermen sell their catch directly from the boats, predates the redesign by centuries and survives it unchanged.

Ferries leave from the Quai des Belges for the Frioul archipelago and the Château d'If throughout the day. The restaurants running along both sides of the port serve bouillabaisse, the saffron-and-rockfish stew that originated here among the fishing community and became the city's defining dish. The Forts Saint-Jean and Saint-Nicolas stand at the entrance to the port on opposite sides, built in the seventeenth century to control access to the harbour and, some historians argue, to keep a watchful eye on the city itself.
