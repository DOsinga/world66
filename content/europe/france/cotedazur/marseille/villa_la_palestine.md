Villa La Palestine belongs to a naming tradition that flourished among Marseille's prosperous classes in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. As wealth accumulated from trade with North Africa, the Levant, and the wider Mediterranean, the families who built summer villas along the coast and in the southern arrondissements reached for names that evoked the Holy Land and the Orient: Villa Jérusalem, Villa Nazareth, Villa Palestine, Villa du Liban. The choice was partly religious — Marseille's bourgeoisie was predominantly Catholic, and the Biblical resonances of these names carried genuine piety — and partly fashionable, in the way that Orientalism permeated European decorative culture throughout the period.

Marseille was better placed than almost any other French city to absorb this Orientalist aesthetic. It was the principal port of departure for pilgrims and travellers heading to the Holy Land, and its trading houses maintained connections across the eastern Mediterranean. Names like Palestine were not entirely abstract here: they pointed to places that some Marseillais families had actually visited, or with which they did business. The name on a villa gate was a statement of cosmopolitan identity as much as a religious gesture.

The villa's location in the northern arrondissements, not far from the port itself, suggests an older stratum of the city's bourgeois residential geography — the northern hills above the docks were desirable before the southern coast became the preferred address. Villa La Palestine, whatever its current condition, preserves in its name a fragment of the commercial and cultural world that made Marseille what it was.
