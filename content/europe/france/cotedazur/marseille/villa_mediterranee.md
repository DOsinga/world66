Villa Méditerranée was designed by the Italian architect Stefano Boeri and completed in 2013 as part of Marseille's year as European Capital of Culture. The building's defining gesture is a dramatic cantilever: the upper floors project 40 metres over a shallow reflecting pool, the white volume appearing to float at the water's edge beside the older Fort Saint-Jean and the MuCEM. On a bright Provençal day the effect is genuinely arresting — a piece of architectural optimism planted at the hinge between the city and the sea.

Its founding mission was equally ambitious: to serve as an international forum for the countries bordering the Mediterranean, bringing together politicians, researchers, and civil society around shared questions of migration, environment, and culture. That vocation proved difficult to sustain. The institution cycled through governance structures and struggled to draw consistent audiences, and by the late 2010s the building had become something of a beautiful white elephant, its events sparse and its identity unclear.

The turnaround came in 2022 with the opening of the Cosquer Méditerranée replica inside the building. The original Cosquer Cave, discovered by diver Henri Cosquer in 1985, contains Palaeolithic paintings — hand stencils, horses, auks — dating back 27,000 years, but lies entirely underwater beneath the Calanques cliffs and is inaccessible to the public. The replica recreates 2,300 square metres of the cave at full scale, giving visitors the remarkable experience of standing among some of Europe's oldest art in the heart of a twenty-first-century architectural statement.
