The Corniche was lined with similar properties in the late 19th century — the villas of Marseille's shipping and trading families built on the bluff above the sea when the road first opened in the 1850s. One by one the others were demolished for apartment blocks during the 20th century. Villa Valmer survived because the cliff descent made standard apartment development economically unattractive: the terraced garden that now constitutes its main attraction was, in effect, what made it undevelopable.

The City of Marseille acquired the property and operates the main villa as a conference centre, but the gardens are open to the public without charge. The terraces descend in formal Provençal style — clipped hedges, gravel paths, Mediterranean plantings — with direct views to the Îles du Frioul to the west and open sea to the south. At low points in the garden you are close enough to the water to hear it.

The estate dates from the period when the Corniche itself was under construction: the coastal road was built in stages from 1848 onward as a public works project and promenade for the bourgeoisie. The villas that appeared alongside it reflected the new prosperity of Marseille's commercial class during the Second Empire — a prosperity built on the opening of the Suez Canal trade routes and on the colonial commerce with North Africa.
