The Hospices de Beaune is one of the great buildings of medieval Europe, and the reason most people make the detour to this corner of Burgundy. Built in 1443 by Nicolas Rolin, chancellor to the Duke of Burgundy, as a hospital for the poor, it remained in operation as a medical facility until 1971. The exterior is striking enough — Gothic stone towers and steep slate roofline — but step inside to the main courtyard and the famous glazed polychrome roof tiles stop you in your tracks. Burgundian yellow, green, black, and red arranged in intricate geometric patterns: it is one of the most photographed rooftops in France, and deservedly so.

The interior is equally impressive. The Grande Salle des Pôvres (Great Hall of the Poor) is a vast, vaulted ward where the original painted wooden beds still stand in rows beneath a timber ceiling, each curtained for privacy. At the far end hangs Rogier van der Weyden's 'Last Judgement' polyptych, commissioned by Rolin and displayed here in impressive detail — the museum provides magnifying glass screens so you can see every extraordinary face.

Buy your ticket at the entrance and allow at least ninety minutes. The audio guide (available in English) is genuinely good and adds a lot of context. Every November, the Hospices holds one of the world's most famous wine auctions in its courtyard — a useful piece of trivia if you visit in the weeks around it and find the town unexpectedly busy.
