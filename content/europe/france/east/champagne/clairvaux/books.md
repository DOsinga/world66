A small literature has grown up around Clairvaux, both for its monastic and its carceral history.

**The Letters of Saint Bernard of Clairvaux** (various editions, including a Cistercian Publications translation). Read a handful before visiting and the abbey becomes a different place — these are the letters of a 12th-century power broker writing to popes, kings, and would-be Crusaders from the cell where he died.

**Le Dernier Jour d'un Condamné** by Victor Hugo (1829). Hugo's blistering early novel against the death penalty was written when Clairvaux Prison was still relatively new, and the case of Sébastien Bornet — executed at Clairvaux — was part of what moved him. Reading this in the village adds an uncomfortable layer to the prison-abbey visit.

**Vie de Saint Bernard de Clairvaux** by Pierre Aubé (Fayard, 2003). The standard modern biography in French. Aubé is good on the contradictions: Bernard the contemplative who launched a crusade; the saint who could be ferociously combative; the man who insisted on austerity while building the wealthiest monastic network in medieval Europe.
