  The two cupolas at either end of the Carlton's roof are said to have been modelled on the breasts of La Belle Otero, a celebrated Spanish dancer and courtesan who was one of the most famous figures in Belle Époque Europe. The story may be apocryphal — the architect denied it — but it has attached itself to the building so firmly that it is now inseparable from the hotel's legend.

The Carlton is the grandest of the grands hotels along the Croisette, a Belle Époque palace opened in 1911 that has been the social centre of the Cannes Film Festival since the very first edition. Its white facade with the distinctive twin cupolas at each end is one of the most recognisable images in French Riviera photography.

Even if you are not staying, the bar terrace facing the Croisette is worth stopping at for a drink — the people-watching during the Film Festival is extraordinary, but it is pleasant enough at any time of year. The hotel's interior is impeccably maintained Belle Époque grandeur: chandeliers, marble, polished wood. A coffee here costs a small fortune, but it includes the setting.
