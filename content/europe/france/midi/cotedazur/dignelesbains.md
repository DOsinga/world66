Digne-les-Bains is the quiet capital of the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, tucked into a valley well north of the coast. It is a thermal town first and foremost — people have been coming here for the hot springs since Roman times — but it also serves as a gateway to some of the most unspoiled mountain country in southern France. In August, the Corso de la Lavande takes over the streets with lavender-scented floats and processions, celebrating the crop that turns the surrounding hills purple each summer.

The [Train des Pignes](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/dignelesbains/train_des_pignes) is one of the best reasons to visit. This narrow-gauge railway winds 150 kilometres through gorges and mountain valleys between Digne and [Nice](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/nice), crossing bridges and tunnels that took 27 years to build. The journey takes about three and a half hours and the scenery is spectacular. The heritage steam version runs on summer weekends.

The town itself is small and manageable. The [Cathédrale Saint-Jérôme](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/dignelesbains/cathedrale_saint_jerome) dates from the 13th century, and the [Musée Gassendi](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/dignelesbains/musee_gassendi) mixes local natural history with contemporary art in a surprisingly compelling way — works by Andy Goldsworthy, made from local materials, are among the highlights. The [Jardin Botanique des Cordeliers](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/dignelesbains/jardin_botanique) in the centre of town focuses on the flora of the surrounding region.

The [Réserve Géologique de Haute-Provence](/europe/france/midi/cotedazur/dignelesbains/reserve_geologique), one of the largest geological reserves in Europe, stretches north of town across 2,000 square kilometres of mountain country. The fossil beds — ammonites up to 70 cm across, still embedded in the hillside limestone — are openly accessible on marked trails. Good walking country with very few other people on the paths.
