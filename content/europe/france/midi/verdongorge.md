The Verdon Gorge is Europe's answer to the Grand Canyon — a dramatic slash through the limestone plateau of Haute-Provence, carved by the turquoise Verdon river to depths of up to 700 metres. It is the largest canyon in Europe, and one of the great natural spectacles of the continent. The water at the bottom glows an implausible blue-green — the colour comes from the glacial silt suspended in the flow from the Alps upstream — and the walls rise in sheer bands of white, grey, and ochre.

The gorge runs roughly 25 kilometres from the village of Rougon in the east to the artificial Lac de Sainte-Croix in the west, where the Verdon finally slows and widens into the massive reservoir created by the Quinson dam. The rim is accessible by two roads: the Route des Crêtes on the north side passes a series of vertiginous belvederes (viewpoints), while the Route des Corniches on the south side is lower and closer to the water. Both roads are spectacular; the north rim has the better views of the gorge's full depth.

The gorge is not just a viewpoint — it's one of the finest outdoor playgrounds in France. Rock climbers have been working the limestone walls since the 1960s, and hundreds of routes of all grades are bolted across the main face. The Sentier Martel — a long-distance trail that traverses the gorge floor for about 14 kilometres between two laddered descents — is one of the great hikes in Provence, taking a full day and requiring prior arrangement for the ladder sections. Kayaking and canoeing are possible on the upper river and on the lac.

The nearest bases are the village of [La Palud-sur-Verdon](/europe/france/midi/verdongorge/la_palud) on the north rim and [Aiguines](/europe/france/midi/verdongorge/aiguines) at the western end. Both are small and peaceful out of season. In July and August the gorge is extremely popular with French and German visitors, and the viewpoint car parks can be chaotic.
