Fort Risban stands at the entrance to the port of Calais, a squat coastal fortification that has marked the city's harbour mouth since the 14th century. The site was originally a wooden fort built during the English occupation, later reinforced in stone and modified repeatedly as military architecture evolved. Its strategic position — controlling access to the port — made it a key objective in every siege of Calais from the 14th century to 1940.

The fort sits on a low promontory just beside the port entrance, and while public access is limited, it is clearly visible from the nearby seawall walk. The juxtaposition of the medieval masonry against the industrial bustle of the modern port — car ferries, container ships, the Channel Tunnel rail link — captures something essential about Calais: a city where the past is visible at every turn but is always competing with the urgency of the present.
