Caen is far more than a ferry port. William the Conqueror made it his capital, and the two great abbeys he and his wife Matilda founded — the [Abbaye aux Hommes](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/abbaye_aux_hommes) and the [Abbaye aux Dames](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/abbaye_aux_dames) — still anchor the city. Both survived the devastating bombardment of June 1944, when much of medieval Caen was destroyed in the Battle of Normandy.

The city has rebuilt itself as a lively university town with a fine castle at its heart. The [Château de Caen](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/chateau_de_caen), William's fortress, encloses two excellent museums — the [Musée des Beaux-Arts](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/musee_des_beaux_arts) and the [Musée de Normandie](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/musee_de_normandie) — and its ramparts offer good views over the city. The [Mémorial de Caen](/europe/france/normandybrittany/caen/memorial_de_caen), on the northern edge of town, is one of the best Second World War museums in France and an essential stop for anyone visiting the D-Day beaches. The old quarters that survived the war are full of half-timbered houses, and the Sunday market at Place Saint-Sauveur is one of Normandy's best.

Caen sits on the Orne river, connected to the sea by a canal. The ferry terminal at Ouistreham is 15 km to the north, making this a natural first stop for visitors arriving from England.
