  The castle takes its name from the Georgian word "batoni" (lord or master). It was built in the 18th century by Herekle II — the king who eventually signed the treaty uniting Georgia with Russia in 1783 — as his royal residence. The fortified palace complex was designed to function both as a seat of court life and as a last redoubt if the town fell, reflecting the constant military pressure Kakheti faced from Ottoman and Persian raids.
---

Batonistsikhe sits in the centre of Telavi, a compact walled palace-fortress built for the Kakhetian royal family in the 18th century. The complex includes a round tower, a small palace building, a church, and a bath house set within an enclosed courtyard — a complete picture of how Kakhetian royalty lived and defended themselves in the final century before Russian annexation.

The site is now open to visitors as a small museum, with rooms furnished in period style and displays on Kakhetian history. The views from the tower over Telavi and across the valley are the highlight for most visitors. The scale is modest but the setting is genuine — there is no tourist overlay here, and the castle feels like a working piece of local history rather than a showpiece.
