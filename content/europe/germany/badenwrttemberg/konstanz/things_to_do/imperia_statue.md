  The statue is named after Imperia, the fictional courtesan from Honoré de Balzac's novella. She holds two tiny figures aloft: the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor — representatives of the two greatest powers of the age — both made to look ridiculous in her hands. The sculptor Peter Lenk unveiled her in 1993, and the city authorities initially resisted, but she has since become Konstanz's most recognised landmark.
---

Standing nine metres tall at the entrance to the harbour, the Imperia rotates slowly on her plinth, holding a crowned emperor in one hand and a pope in the other. She is a monument to the Council of Constance (1414–1418), when Konstanz hosted the largest gathering in medieval Europe: thousands of church officials, nobles, and hangers-on who spent four years sorting out a schism in which three men simultaneously claimed to be pope. The council deposed all three and elected a single new pope, ending the crisis — but also burned the Czech reformer Jan Hus as a heretic, an act with consequences that reverberated for centuries.

The statue is a ten-minute walk from the old town along the harbour promenade, and the ferry terminal is right beside her.
