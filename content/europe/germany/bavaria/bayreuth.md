Bayreuth is a Franconian city of about 73,000 people, known throughout the world for one thing: Richard Wagner. The composer moved here in 1872, built his own opera house — the [Festspielhaus](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/festspielhaus) — on a hill above the city, and inaugurated his festival in 1876. It hasn't stopped since. Every July and August, the **Bayreuth Festival** draws opera pilgrims from around the world to sit through four- and five-hour Wagner performances in a theatre designed by Wagner himself, with sight lines and acoustics engineered specifically for his music. Tickets are famously hard to obtain — waiting lists run to years.

But Bayreuth offers more than Wagner. The [Margravial Opera House](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/margravial_opera_house) (Markgräfliches Opernhaus), built in 1748 for the Margrave of Brandenburg-Bayreuth, is one of the finest surviving Baroque theatres in Europe and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The ornate interior — tiered boxes, gilded woodwork, painted ceilings — is genuinely breathtaking. Outside the city, the [Hermitage](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/hermitage) gardens were laid out in the early 18th century as a retreat for the Bayreuth court, with fountains, grottoes, and a Chinese teahouse that still feel improbably whimsical.

Wagner's house, [Wahnfried](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/wahnfried), is now a museum dedicated to his life and work. He and Cosima are buried in the garden behind it. The city's historic core around the [Stadtkirche](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/stadtkirche) and the old market square is pleasant to wander, and Bayreuth's relative obscurity — compared to [Nuremberg](/europe/germany/bavaria/nuremberg) or [Munich](/europe/germany/bavaria/munich) — means it's rarely overcrowded. Fans of Franconian beer should seek out [Maisel's brewery](/europe/germany/bavaria/bayreuth/maisel_brewery) on Kulmbacher Strasse, which includes one of the best cooperage museums in Bavaria.
