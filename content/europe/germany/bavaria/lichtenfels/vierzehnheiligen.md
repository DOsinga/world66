  The basilica was built to mark the spot where a shepherd boy claimed to have seen visions of the Fourteen Holy Helpers — a group of saints venerated for interceding in cases of illness — in the 1440s. Balthasar Neumann, the greatest Baroque architect of 18th-century Germany, designed the church between 1743 and 1772, though he died before its completion. The result is remarkable for how it handles the awkward requirement of centering the altar not on the high altar, which is where Gothic churches put it, but on the point of the vision in the middle of the nave — forcing Neumann to solve a spatial problem that no previous architect had faced.

The Basilica of Vierzehnheiligen (Fourteen Holy Helpers) is one of the finest Baroque pilgrimage churches in Germany, sitting on a ridge above the Main valley east of Lichtenfels. Balthasar Neumann designed it with an unusual interior: the central point of pilgrimage is the elaborately decorated Gnadenaltar (Grace Altar) positioned in the middle of the nave rather than at the east end, creating a sequence of oval spaces that flow around the pilgrim as they approach.

The exterior is best seen from across the valley — the twin towers and convex facade rising above the Frankenwald hills. Inside, the stucco work and ceiling frescoes are exceptional, the work of craftsmen from the Wessobrunn school who defined South German Baroque decoration. The church is still an active pilgrimage site. A café and shop near the entrance serve the visiting crowd; the walking trail down to the Main valley takes about 45 minutes.
