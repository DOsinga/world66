Regensburg is one of Germany's best-preserved medieval cities, a UNESCO World Heritage Site on the Danube in eastern Bavaria. For over 600 years — from 1245 until Napoleon arrived in 1803 — it was a free imperial city and a prosperous trading centre. Unlike most German cities, it came through the Second World War largely unscathed, leaving an extraordinary concentration of medieval and early-modern architecture intact.

The [Steinerne Brücke](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/steinernebrcke) (Stone Bridge), built in 1135–1146, is Germany's oldest surviving stone bridge and gives the finest view of the city's skyline — a forest of towers rising above the Danube. At the foot of the bridge, the [Historische Wurstkuchl](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/historische_wurstkuchl) has been grilling sausages on the same riverside spot since the construction workers arrived in 1135. The [Regensburg Cathedral](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/regensburg_cathedral) (Dom St Peter) is the most important Gothic building in Bavaria, with stunning 14th-century stained glass and the Domspatzen boys' choir founded around 975 AD.

The old town is a tangle of narrow lanes, patrician tower-houses (Regensburg has more medieval towers than any German city), and lively squares. [Saint Emmeram's Abbey](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/saint_emmeram_abbey) — now partly a Thurn und Taxis family palace — preserves extraordinary Carolingian cloisters and a crypt full of saints' relics. The [Scots Monastery](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/scots_monastery) has a Romanesque portal from the 1180s that is among the finest carved stonework in Germany. The [Old Town Hall](/europe/germany/bavaria/regensburg/document_reichstag_museum) was where the Perpetual Imperial Diet met from 1663 to 1806 — the first standing parliament in European history.

Regensburg is also a university city with a young population, good restaurants, and a beer garden culture that rivals Munich's — at a fraction of the tourist crowds.
