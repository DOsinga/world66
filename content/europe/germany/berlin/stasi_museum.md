  When the East German regime fell in late 1989, the Stasi — which had employed one informer for every 63 citizens, the densest surveillance apparatus in history — began frantically shredding its files. Citizens occupied the headquarters and stopped the destruction. The torn pieces of 45 million documents are still being reassembled by hand and by computer, decades later. Some people have requested to see their own files only to discover that the informers reporting on them were close friends or family members.

The former headquarters of the Ministerium für Staatssicherheit — the East German secret police, known as the Stasi — is preserved in Lichtenberg exactly as it was when citizens occupied it in January 1990. Erich Mielke's office still has his telephone bank and his personal weapons in the display case; the listening equipment, the wig collection, the smell bottles used to catalogue dissidents' scents for the tracker dogs, all remain. It is one of the most chilling museum experiences in Europe.

The building is a Soviet-era complex in Lichtenberg, a short U-Bahn ride from the centre. Allow two hours; the documentation room with individual case files is sobering but important. The museum charges minimal admission and is less visited than it deserves.
