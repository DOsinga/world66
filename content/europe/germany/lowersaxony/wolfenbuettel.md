Wolfenbüttel is one of those German towns that somehow escaped both wartime destruction and postwar neglect, leaving it with one of the best-preserved collections of half-timbered houses in northern Germany. Over 600 Fachwerk buildings line the streets of the old town, many dating back to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and the overall effect is almost absurdly picturesque. The town sits just south of Braunschweig along the river Oker, and while many visitors treat it as a day trip from Braunschweig, it rewards a slower pace and an overnight stay.

The intellectual heart of the town is the Herzog August Bibliothek, founded in 1572. At its seventeenth-century peak it was the largest library in Europe, and it still holds what was once considered the most expensive book in the world: the Gospels of Henry the Lion, an illuminated manuscript from around 1188. The philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz worked here as librarian, and later Gotthold Ephraim Lessing — author of Nathan the Wise — spent the last decade of his life in Wolfenbüttel as the library's director. His house is now a small museum. The library's baroque reading rooms and rotating exhibitions are open to the public and alone justify the trip.

The dukes of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel made this their residence for nearly two centuries, and their mark is everywhere. Schloss Wolfenbüttel is one of the largest residential palaces in Lower Saxony, surrounded by the canal-laced neighbourhood known as Little Venice (Kleines Venedig) where the ducal officials once lived. The streets here, with their well-preserved gabled facades and quiet canals along the Oker, are ideal for an aimless wander. Wolfenbüttel is also, improbably, the birthplace of Jägermeister — the Mast-Jägermeister headquarters has been here since 1934, and there is a visitor experience attached to the plant.
