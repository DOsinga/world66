Duisburg is a Ruhr city that has reinvented its industrial heritage more creatively than most. The standout is [Landschaftspark Duisburg-Nord](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/landschaftspark), a former ironworks and blast furnace complex converted into a public park. By day you can climb the ore bunkers and walk through the industrial ruins; by night the whole site is lit up with coloured lights by artist Jonathan Park. The old gasometer has been turned into a diving centre, and rock climbers scale the concrete walls of the ore storage bunkers.

The city sits at the confluence of the Rhine and the Ruhr and has the [largest inland port in the world](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/port_of_duisburg). The [Innenhafen](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/innenhafen), the old inner harbour, has been redeveloped with museums, restaurants, and the striking [Küppersmühle museum of modern art](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/museum_kuppersmuhle) designed by Herzog & de Meuron. The [Lehmbruck Museum](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/lehmbruck_museum) — dedicated to Duisburg's own great Expressionist sculptor — is one of the best sculpture museums in Germany and one of the least crowded.

In the south of the city, [Tiger & Turtle — Magic Mountain](/europe/germany/northrhinewestphalia/duisburg/tiger_and_turtle) is a walkable roller-coaster sculpture atop a former slag heap, with panoramic views over the Rhine. Duisburg is not a pretty city, but it is an honest one, and the way it has repurposed its industrial past is genuinely impressive.
