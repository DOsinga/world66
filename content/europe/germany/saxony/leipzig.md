Leipzig is one of the most dynamic cities in eastern Germany — a place with a musical heritage to rival Vienna, a pivotal role in the fall of the Berlin Wall, and a creative energy that has made it a magnet for artists and students since reunification.

The city was home to Johann Sebastian Bach for the last 27 years of his life, and the [Thomaskirche](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/thomaskirche) where he worked as cantor still hosts performances of his music weekly. The [Nikolaikirche](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/nikolaikirche) is where the peaceful Monday demonstrations began in 1989, setting off the chain of events that brought down the Wall. Both churches are worth visiting even if you are not religious — they are the heart of Leipzig’s story.

The compact old town centres on the Markt, dominated by the handsome Renaissance [Altes Rathaus](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/altesrathaus). Good eating and drinking clusters around the Barfußgässchen, and [Auerbachs Keller](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/auerbachs_keller) — featured in Goethe’s *Faust* — is one of Germany’s most famous restaurants. Southeast of the city, the colossal [Völkerschlachtdenkmal](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/voelkerschlachtdenkmal) marks where Napoleon was defeated in 1813 in what was then the largest battle in history.

The [Spinnerei](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/spinnerei) in the western Plagwitz district is Leipzig’s most exciting contemporary art complex — a former cotton mill turned gallery quarter that launched the New Leipzig School of painting onto the world stage. The [Stasi Museum](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/stasi_museum) at the Runde Ecke and the [Zeitgeschichtliches Forum](/europe/germany/saxony/leipzig/zeitgeschichtliches_forum) give the best account of life in the GDR anywhere in Germany.
