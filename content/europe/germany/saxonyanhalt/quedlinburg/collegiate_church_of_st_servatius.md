story: >
  The crypt beneath the nave holds the tombs of Henry I, crowned first King of Germany in 919, and his wife Queen Matilda. Henry made Quedlinburg a royal residence and effectively put the town on the map; you can trace the foundations of his original 10th-century church beneath the later Romanesque structure. The church was consecrated in its current form in 1021 under Emperor Henry II, making it one of the oldest intact Romanesque interiors in Germany.
---

The Collegiate Church of St. Servatius crowns the Schlossberg like a statement of intent — two square towers visible from everywhere in the lower town, a reminder that this modest-looking place was once a seat of imperial power. The interior is austere and all the better for it: pale sandstone, round arches, and a crypt that feels genuinely ancient.

The crypt is the highlight. Henry I and Matilda lie here in stone sarcophagi, and the space has the weight of somewhere that mattered enormously for centuries. The carved capitals in the nave above are worth a close look too — some of the early 11th-century stonework is surprisingly expressive for its age. Allow at least an hour to see the church, crypt, and the attached palace museum.
