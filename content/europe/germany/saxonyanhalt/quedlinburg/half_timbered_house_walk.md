story: >
  Quedlinburg's designation as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994 recognised not just the Schlossberg but the entire old town, specifically for having one of the largest concentrations of half-timbered architecture in Germany. The buildings range from simple 14th-century structures to elaborate 18th-century merchant houses. Unlike many timber-frame towns, Quedlinburg was never substantially rebuilt or modernised, partly because it remained economically quiet after the Reformation stripped the abbey of its imperial status.
---

There is no single prescribed walking route, which is part of the charm. The old town is compact — you can walk from one edge to the other in twenty minutes — but the density of interesting buildings rewards a slower pace. Streets like the Wordgasse, Blasiistrasse, and the lanes around the Münzenberg reward wandering without a particular destination.

What makes Quedlinburg unusual is the survival rate. Many German timber-frame towns lost their oldest buildings to fires, modernisation, or wartime bombing. Quedlinburg avoided all three in ways that were partly luck and partly the town's relative economic obscurity. The result is that you can stand in front of a 14th-century house on a working residential street, with medieval neighbours on both sides, in a way that is genuinely rare. A free town map from the tourist office marks the most notable individual buildings.
