  The halligen were once part of the Frisian mainland, severed by a series of catastrophic storm surges between the 13th and 17th centuries that inundated tens of thousands of hectares of farmland and killed tens of thousands of people. The Grote Mandränke of 1362 and the Burchardi flood of 1634 were the most devastating, and the remnants of drowned villages can still be seen at low tide as dark peat formations in the tidal flats. The communities that survived rebuilt on artificial mounds — warften — and have lived in this precarious way ever since.
---

The halligen are the smallest and strangest of the North Frisian Islands — not true islands but low-lying tidal marshes that flood completely in storm surges, leaving only the farmhouses on their raised warften (artificial mounds) visible above the water. There are about ten halligen, most inhabited by a handful of families, and visiting them feels like stepping into a world where the relationship between land and sea has never been resolved.

Langeness and Hooge are the most accessible, reachable by ferry from Schlüttsiel. Hooge has a small guesthouses and a museum documenting the halligen way of life. The experience of being on a hallig at high tide, surrounded by water in all directions with only the warft above the surface, is completely unlike anything else in Germany. Visit between May and August for the best weather; winter storm surges are spectacular but access can be disrupted.
