Of all Germany's large cities, Erfurt is the one most redolent of pre-war Germany. Although it lost a couple of important monuments in bombing raids, it was otherwise little damaged in World War II — and its streets of grand turn-of-the-century shopfronts were saved from postwar developers by the fact that the city ended up in East Germany.

The city's most photographed sight is the [Krämerbrücke](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/kraemerbrcke), a medieval bridge lined with half-timbered houses that have been continuously inhabited for centuries — one of only a handful of such bridges still standing anywhere in Europe. At the top of the old town, the [Erfurt Cathedral](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/erfurt_cathedral) and the [Severikirche](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/severkirche) rise side by side from a broad flight of steps, a dramatic ensemble that dominates the skyline. Between them and the Krämerbrücke stretches a network of lanes and squares that rewards wandering without a map.

The [Old Synagogue](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/old_synagogue), a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2023, is one of the best-preserved medieval synagogues in Europe — and inside it, the Erfurt Treasure, a hoard of gold and silver hidden in the walls during a medieval massacre and only discovered in 1998, is extraordinary. The [Augustinerkloster](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/augustinerkloster), where Martin Luther studied and was ordained as a monk, is now a museum and hotel. The city has worn its history lightly — it feels lived-in and unpretentious, with a busy market square and a university that keeps it young.

For art, the [Angermuseum](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/angermuseum) on the broad Anger boulevard has the best regional collection of medieval and early modern painting. The [Andreasstrasse Memorial](/europe/germany/thuringia/erfurt/stasi_memorial) occupies the former Stasi prison and is a sobering reminder of the city's more recent history.
