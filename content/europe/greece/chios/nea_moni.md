  In the ossuary adjacent to the monastery church lies one of the most sobering sights in Greece: the skulls and bones of the estimated 3,000 monks and islanders massacred here during the 1822 Ottoman reprisals. They were not reburied but left visible as a permanent memorial. The mosaics in the church survived the massacre; the klados (belltower), which the Ottomans tried and failed to demolish, still stands at an angle, cracked but intact.

Nea Moni is an 11th-century monastery set in the wooded hills of central Chios, about 11 km west of the main town. It was built on the orders of Emperor Constantine IX Monomachos following the reported discovery of a miraculous icon of the Virgin. The mosaics that cover the interior of the church dome and apse are among the finest surviving examples of Byzantine art — vivid, gold-ground, and remarkably intact given the building's age and turbulent history.

The monastery is now a convent; a small community of nuns tends to the building and allows visitors. The grounds are peaceful, with views across the hills to the sea on clear days. The main church and the ossuary are both open. You will need to dress modestly to enter — scarves and coverings for shoulders and knees are required and available at the entrance.

Getting there by public transport is awkward — a taxi from Chios town or a rental car is the practical option. Allow at least two hours including travel time.
