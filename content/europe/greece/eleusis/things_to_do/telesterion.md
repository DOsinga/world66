The Telesterion was the initiation hall of the Eleusinian Mysteries — the building where, once a year, thousands of initiates gathered to witness whatever it was that the priests of Demeter revealed. We still do not know what they saw. Every initiate who ever entered took an oath of silence that, remarkably, held for a thousand years.

What the ruins show is the scale of the ambition. The hall was roughly square, about 54 by 52 metres, and lined on all four sides with stepped seating cut directly into the rock of the hillside. At its peak it could accommodate several thousand people at once, making it one of the largest enclosed spaces in the ancient Greek world. Forty-two columns supported the roof. In the center stood the Anaktoron, a small inner sanctuary where the most sacred objects were kept and the final revelation took place.

The building was rebuilt and expanded repeatedly from the Archaic period onward — Pericles commissioned a major enlargement in the 5th century BCE. What you see today is the Roman version, the most ambitious of all. The rock-cut seats are still visible along the hillside, and the column stump positions give a good sense of the original interior. A model in the museum makes the whole structure legible.
