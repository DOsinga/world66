Near the village of Trypiti, on a terraced hillside below the ancient theatre, a modest marker indicates the approximate spot where the Venus de Milo was discovered in 1820. There is no grand monument, no visitor centre, and no reconstructed display — just an agricultural field on a slope, with a small plaque and a view down to the bay. The understatement is somehow appropriate. One of the most famous sculptures in the world was found here by accident, in the ground, and it left.

The site is worth visiting precisely because of its ordinariness. It forces you to imagine the moment: a farmer's plough catching something in the earth, the slow uncovering of a marble arm, then a torso, then the full figure of a goddess in two pieces. The statue is now in the Louvre, where it is seen by millions of visitors a year. The field is still a field. The catacombs and the ancient theatre are both within easy walking distance, making this corner of the Trypiti hillside one of the most historically dense areas on the island.
