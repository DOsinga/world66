  The Philippeion was the first monument at Olympia built to honour living
  humans rather than gods. Philip II of Macedon began construction after his
  victory at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC, when he effectively became
  master of Greece. Inside stood gold and ivory statues of Philip, his wife
  Olympias, his parents, and his son Alexander — the future Alexander the
  Great. Placing a family portrait gallery inside the sacred Altis was an act
  of political theatre that the Greeks would have found deeply uncomfortable,
  and probably intended to.

A circular marble building — a tholos — that stands at the northwestern corner of the Altis, the sacred precinct of the sanctuary. It was commissioned by Philip II of Macedon, who wanted to mark his new dominance over the Greek world in the most visible religious space available. The structure had an outer ring of Ionic columns and an inner ring of Corinthian half-columns, an unusual combination that gave the interior a layered grandeur.

Today several columns have been restored upright and the round foundations are clearly visible. It is one of the best-preserved structures on the site and gives a good sense of Macedonian architectural ambition. Philip was assassinated before its completion; it was finished by Alexander.
