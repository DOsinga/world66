  It stood for only 54 years before an earthquake toppled it in 226 BC. The
  fallen statue lay on the ground for nearly nine centuries — an ancient tourist
  attraction in its own right, according to Pliny — before Arab raiders sold
  the bronze for scrap in 654 AD. Where exactly it stood has been debated since

  Renaissance invention with no basis in the ancient sources.

The harbour mouth at Mandraki is traditionally — if almost certainly incorrectly — given as the site where the Colossus once stood. Two stone columns topped with bronze deer now mark the spot, a modern replacement for whatever ancient marker once identified it. The deer are the symbol of Rhodes and appear throughout the city, including on the harbour mole and on the coats of arms of the Knights.

Nothing survives of the Colossus itself, and the site is more a conceptual marker than an archaeological one. But standing at the harbour mouth, looking out toward the sea and then back at the tower of St. Nicholas at the end of the mole, gives a useful sense of the scale of ancient Rhodes — a city prosperous and confident enough to build one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World to celebrate victory over a siege. The harbour walls and fortified moles date from the period of the Knights and make for a good early-morning walk.
