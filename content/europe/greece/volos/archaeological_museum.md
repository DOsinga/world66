The Archaeological Museum of Volos is one of the best mid-sized museums in Greece, and easily the most important reason to spend time in the city itself. Its collection spans thousands of years of Thessalian history, from the Neolithic settlements at Sesklo and Dimini — among the earliest farming communities in Europe, dating back to around 6500 BCE — through the Bronze Age, the classical period, and into Hellenistic times. The breadth is impressive, but the museum wears it lightly: the displays are well-organised and not overwhelming.

The centrepiece is the collection of painted funerary stelae from Demetrias, an ancient Macedonian city on the outskirts of modern Volos. These grave markers, produced in the third and second centuries BCE, are exceptional in Greek art for depicting real people in recognisable human situations — a young woman at her toilet, a warrior in armour, a couple facing each other with an intimacy rare in ancient carved stone. The painted surfaces have retained extraordinary colour, and the quality of observation in the faces is closer to portraiture than to the idealised figuration typical of the period.

The prehistoric galleries, covering the Neolithic settlements of Sesklo and Dimini a few kilometres outside Volos, show the material culture of some of the earliest European farmers in fine detail. The decorated pottery from Sesklo is particularly striking, and the models of Neolithic houses help give a sense of what these settlements looked like. Budget at least two hours; the museum deserves them. It is open daily except Mondays; admission is modest.
