Snæfellsnes is a peninsula jutting west into the Atlantic from the coast of western Iceland, and it compresses an extraordinary range of Icelandic scenery into a single 90-kilometre strip of land. Glacier-capped volcano, lava fields, dramatic sea cliffs, fishing villages, whale-watching grounds, black sand beaches, and ancient saga landscapes all within an hour's drive of each other. Jules Verne chose Snæfellsjökull — the glacier-capped volcano at the peninsula's tip — as the entrance to the centre of the earth in his famous novel, and on a dramatic cloud-moving day, with the glacier floating above the lava fields, you can see why.

The dominant feature is [Snæfellsjökull](/europe/iceland/snaefellsnes/snaefellsjokull) — a 1,446-metre stratovolcano capped by a glacier, now a national park. In clear weather it is visible from Reykjavik 120 kilometres away. The volcano's slopes have several marked hiking routes, and glacier tours operate from the base. The most rewarding coastal walking is between [Arnarstapi](/europe/iceland/snaefellsnes/arnarstapi) and [Hellnar](/europe/iceland/snaefellsnes/hellnar) — a two-kilometre cliff path through lava arches, sea caves, and basalt formations above the crashing surf.

The peninsula's north coast is wilder and less visited than the south. [Stykkishólmur](/europe/iceland/stykkisholmur) at the eastern end is the main town and a comfortable base, with ferry services to the Westfjords. The fishing village of Grundarfjörður on the north coast sits below the remarkable Kirkjufell mountain — a symmetrical wedge of rock rising directly from the sea that has become one of Iceland's most photographed peaks. The saga landscape around Búðir and Ytri-Tunga on the south coast includes a rare black church and a beach where grey seals haul out year-round.

A car is essential for exploring Snæfellsnes; the peninsula is served by occasional buses but the frequency makes independent travel awkward. Most visitors do it as a long day trip from Reykjavik or as a two-day circuit, staying one night in Stykkishólmur or at a farmhouse along the way. Spring and early summer bring fewer crowds and the possibility of seeing the glacier in good visibility before summer haze builds.
