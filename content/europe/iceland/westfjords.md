The Westfjords — Vestfirðir in Icelandic — are a rugged peninsula in Iceland's northwest, a tangle of deep fjords and mountains that is the country's least visited and most dramatic region. The peninsula juts into the sea between the Greenland Sea and the Denmark Strait, connected to the rest of Iceland by a neck of land barely 7 kilometres wide. This near-isolation has shaped everything about it: the roads are slow and winding, the population is small, and the landscape is relentless.

The Westfjords have the longest coastline of any region in Iceland — the endlessly folded fjord coastline means that driving the perimeter takes days. Most visitors come for the big set pieces: [Dynjandi](/europe/iceland/westfjords/dynjandi), the tiered waterfall that drops 100 metres into the sea in a series of diminishing falls; [Látrabjarg](/europe/iceland/westfjords/latrabjarg), the westernmost point in Europe, where kilometres of sea cliffs host Iceland's largest puffin colony; and [Rauðasandur](/europe/iceland/westfjords/raudasandur), a beach of rust-red sand impossible to believe is real until you stand on it. The uninhabited [Hornstrandir nature reserve](/europe/iceland/isafjordur/hornstrandir), accessible only by boat from [Ísafjörður](/europe/iceland/isafjordur), offers one of the most remote hiking experiences in Europe.

[Ísafjörður](/europe/iceland/isafjordur) is the region's only real town, with around 2,600 people, a surprising concentration of good food, and an airport with one of the more dramatic landing approaches in commercial aviation. The [Westfjords Heritage Museum](/europe/iceland/isafjordur/westfjords_heritage_museum) there is excellent. Hólmavík in the south has a museum dedicated to Icelandic witchcraft and sorcery that is genuinely interesting. The hot springs at Drangsnes and Grettislaug can be reached by those willing to drive the mountain roads.

Getting here takes effort. The drive from Reykjavik is at least five hours in good conditions, much of it on narrow single-lane roads with steep switchbacks. Domestic flights to Ísafjörður take 40 minutes and offer views that justify the ticket price. Some fjords can only be crossed by seasonal ferry, cutting hours off overland routes. The region rewards the journey.
