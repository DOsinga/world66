  The cathedral is dedicated to St Peter and St Paul and was the largest church in the Glendalough monastic complex at its peak. The monastery was sacked repeatedly — by Vikings in 775, 834, 851, and 1163 — and by the Anglo-Normans in 1214, after which it was never fully rebuilt. The ruins you see today show the accumulation of construction, destruction, and partial reconstruction over five centuries.
---

The cathedral ruins are the largest structure in the Glendalough monastic enclosure, with walls still standing to considerable height on the nave and chancel. The Romanesque doorway and chancel arch show the quality of craftsmanship that the monastery supported at its peak in the 10th through 12th centuries, when Glendalough was one of the most important centres of learning in Ireland.

The surrounding graveyard contains headstones dating from the medieval period to the recent past, some in the traditional Irish form with carved Celtic patterns. It's still an active burial ground, which adds a continuity to the site that purely archaeological ruins rarely have.
