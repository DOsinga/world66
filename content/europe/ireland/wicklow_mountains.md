The Wicklow Mountains begin barely 20 kilometres south of Dublin city centre and feel like a different world. The national park covers some 200 square kilometres of blanket bog, heather moorland, granite peaks, and wooded glens — the largest expanse of unbroken upland in Ireland. Despite the proximity to the capital, you can walk for hours up here and meet no one but sheep and the occasional hill walker.

The Military Road runs south through the heart of the range. It was built by the British army after the 1798 rebellion to flush out insurgents who had been hiding in the hills, and today it makes for one of the most dramatic drives in the country, climbing over high bog and past dark mountain lakes such as Lough Tay and Lough Dan. Lugnaquilla, at 925 metres, is the highest peak, but it is the brooding, open character of the whole landscape rather than any single summit that defines the place.

At the heart of the mountains lies [Glendalough](/europe/ireland/glendalough), the "valley of the two lakes," where St Kevin founded a monastic settlement in the 6th century. Its round tower, ruined cathedral, and scattered hermit cells make it one of the finest early Christian sites in Ireland, and the wooded glacial valley around the two dark lakes is beautiful in its own right. Powerscourt Waterfall, Ireland's highest at 121 metres, tumbles off the northern flank of the range.

For visitors based in Dublin, the mountains make an excellent day out. Bus tours run daily from the city centre, usually taking in Glendalough and the most scenic stretches of the Military Road. By car, Glendalough is about an hour and a quarter from the city — but check return bus times if you are not driving, as the last service back leaves in the late afternoon.
