The Costa di Maratea is Basilicata's only stretch of coastline — roughly 30 kilometres of dramatic cliffs, hidden coves, and clear Tyrrhenian water wedged between Calabria to the south and Campania to the north. It is one of southern Italy's least-known coastal areas, which is precisely its appeal.

The main town, Maratea, is actually a collection of small settlements strung along the hillside above the sea. The [medieval borgo](/europe/italy/basilicata/costa_di_maratea/maratea_borgo) sits high up, clustered around narrow lanes and stone churches, with views down to the water. On the peak of Monte San Biagio above the town stands the [Cristo Redentore](/europe/italy/basilicata/costa_di_maratea/cristo_redentore) — a 22-metre marble statue smaller than Rio's but striking against the sky, and a good hike or short drive for the panorama.

The beaches are small and rocky by Italian standards, many accessible only by boat or steep paths through the macchia. [Cala Jannita](/europe/italy/basilicata/costa_di_maratea/cala_jannita), known locally as the Black Beach for its dark sand, is probably the most famous. Others — Cala di Filocaio, Spiaggia d'a Gnola — are quieter and feel genuinely wild. In summer, boat excursions from [Maratea Porto](/europe/italy/basilicata/costa_di_maratea/maratea_porto) visit the best coves.

The coast is not a resort strip — there are no high-rise hotels or big beach clubs. Accommodation is mostly small hotels and agriturismi scattered through the hills. The food is excellent: fresh seafood, hand-rolled pasta, and the fiery peperoni cruschi (fried dried peppers) that are Basilicata's signature. Getting here by public transport is possible but slow; a car opens up the winding coastal road properly.
