  The Riace Bronzes were discovered by a scuba diver named Stefano Mariottini in August 1972, lying in the sand about eight metres deep off the coast of Riace Marina, nearly ninety kilometres up the coast. They had probably been on a ship that sank in antiquity — the ship itself has never been found. After decades of painstaking conservation work, the bronzes remain in near-perfect condition, their copper lips and silver teeth still intact. They are classified as "Statue A" and "Statue B" and scholars still debate whether they represent heroes, athletes, or gods.

The Museo Nazionale della Magna Grecia is the reason people come to Reggio di Calabria, and it deserves every superlative. The museum holds one of the finest collections of ancient Greek art in the world — pottery, sculpture, coins, jewellery, and architectural fragments from the Greek colonies of southern Italy — but everything else is secondary to the Riace Bronzes, housed in their own specially controlled room on the ground floor.

The two bronzes are full-sized male figures, naked warriors in the classical tradition, cast in bronze with extraordinary technical mastery around 460–450 BC. Standing before them is one of those experiences that the word "museum" barely prepares you for: they are not archaeological artefacts, they are people, and they face you with an intensity that makes the rest of the room disappear. The museum building itself is a stately early twentieth-century palazzo designed by Marcello Piacentini. Allow at least two hours, more if you want to do justice to the rest of the Greek colonial collection.
