---
title: "Eating Out"
type: section
---

Eating in Sant'Andrea Apostolo dello Jonio means eating Calabrian, and in this part of Calabria that means eating well and without fuss. This is strictly a family trattoria town — there are no tourist restaurants, no menus translated into English, no photographs of pasta on laminated boards. The places here cook for the people who live here, and that is the best possible recommendation.

The fish comes from the Ionian directly below town, and if the boats were out the previous night you will find it grilled simply and served with local olive oil and a wedge of lemon. Pasta dishes lean heavily on the regional pantry: 'nduja — the famously fiery, spreadable pork sausage that is one of Calabria's great contributions to Italian food — turns up in a sauce for pasta, spread on bread, or stirred into eggs. If you see **pitta 'mpigliata** on offer, order it: a tightly wound pastry stuffed with figs, honey, nuts, and spices, it is a Calabrian speciality that predates most of what we now think of as Italian cooking, and it is rarely made as well as it is in the homes and small bakeries of these hill towns.

The local cheeses deserve attention too. Caciocavallo Silano — the teardrop-shaped aged cow's milk cheese made on the Sila plateau nearby — is the one to look for, eaten with a little honey or alongside cured meats. Wash it all down with the Cirò wines produced just up the coast, one of Calabria's oldest DOC zones and still undervalued outside the region.

Portions are large, prices are low by any standard, and the hours follow the local rhythms rather than the tourist trade. Lunch is serious and unhurried, beginning around 1pm and rarely over before 3pm. In summer some places open in the evening; outside peak season dinner may not be available at all unless you ask in advance. The phrase **"si mangia quello che c'è"** — you eat what there is — applies, and what there is will be good.
