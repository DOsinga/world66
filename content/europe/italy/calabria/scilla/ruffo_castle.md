  The castle stands on the exact promontory that Homer described as the cave of the monster Scylla, who reached down to snatch sailors from passing ships. The Romans knew the spot as "Scyllaion" and built the earliest fortifications here. The Ruffo family, who gave the castle its current name, dominated Calabria for three centuries — the castle was their most prized possession and the source of their title, Princes of Scilla.

Castello Ruffo occupies the rocky headland that divides Scilla's two beaches and gives the town its commanding silhouette. The structure visible today dates primarily from the 17th century, though fortifications have stood on this rock since antiquity. The castle houses a lighthouse and a small museum, and the ramparts offer spectacular views in both directions — north along the Calabrian coast and south across the Strait of Messina toward Sicily.

The climb up from Marina Grande is short but steep. From the top on a clear day you can see Etna rising above Sicily, and if the atmospheric conditions are right, you may witness the Fata Morgana: a mirage that makes the city of Messina appear to float upright in the water below. The phenomenon is rare but real, and standing on this particular headland is exactly the right place to wait for it.
