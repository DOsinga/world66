  Sybaris was so wealthy and its citizens so devoted to luxury that it gave the ancient world a new word: "sybarite." According to ancient sources, the Sybarites banned blacksmiths and other noisy tradespeople from working inside the city, trained their horses to dance to music, and ate from gold plate. The city had a hundred thousand inhabitants at its peak — enormous for antiquity. When Croton destroyed it in 510 BC, the victors diverted two rivers over the ruins to ensure no one could rebuild.

The Archaeological Park of Sybaris covers the excavated remains of three overlapping cities: ancient Sybaris, destroyed in 510 BC; Thurii, the successor city founded by Greek colonists in 444 BC with Pericles' support; and Copia, the later Roman colony. The archaeology is complex because the layers sit one atop another, preserved under river silt and groundwater that simultaneously obscured and conserved them remarkably well.

The site is divided into three excavation areas — Parco del Cavallo, with Roman-era remains including a theatre; Casa Bianca, with Hellenistic walls and a circular tower; and Stombi, which shows the most ancient Sybaris-era structures. The excavations have been going on for decades and are still ongoing. The adjacent Museo Nazionale della Sibaritide (see separate entry) houses the portable finds.
