  The museum's most famous work is Caravaggio's "Flagellation of Christ" (1607-1610), painted during Caravaggio's final years in Naples while he was a fugitive from Rome. He had killed a man in a brawl in Rome in 1606 and fled south; he painted this enormous canvas — two and a half metres high — under conditions of personal danger and financial pressure, and the violence in the work feels more than aesthetic. He died in 1610, still trying to arrange a papal pardon, on his way back to Rome.

The royal palace of Capodimonte, on the hill above Naples, now houses one of the great art collections in Italy — assembled by Charles of Bourbon from 1738 onwards when he inherited the Farnese collection from his mother, Elisabeth Farnese, and kept adding to it throughout his reign. The collection includes Titian, Raphael, Botticelli, El Greco, Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, Jusepe de Ribera, and major decorative arts from the Bourbon period.

The museum is less visited than the National Archaeological Museum downtown partly because of its inconvenient location on the Capodimonte hill — bus or taxi required — and partly because it lacks a single famous work that functions as a logo. This is a mistake on the visitor's part: the gallery is superb, the palace itself is magnificent, and the park surrounding it is one of the few large green spaces in Naples. Allow at least three hours. The park is open every day and free; the museum is closed Tuesday.
