story: >
  San Gennaro (Saint Januarius), the patron saint of Naples, was martyred near Pozzuoli around 305 AD. His relics were brought to Naples in the 5th century, and his blood — preserved in two small vials in the Duomo — is said to liquefy several times a year. The failure of the blood to liquefy is traditionally considered an omen of disaster; when it didn't liquify in 1527, the city was struck by plague; in 1980, an earthquake. The ritual still draws tens of thousands to the Duomo each September.

Naples' most impressive underground site, carved into the Capodimonte hill above the city. These are proper Christian catacombs — paleo-Christian burial tunnels from the 2nd century that grew into a vast multilevel necropolis, lined with niches, frescoes, and mosaics. Unlike the Roman catacombs, the Naples version has never been fully excavated, and parts remain unexplored. The oldest frescoes date from the 2nd century; others, depicting local bishops and saints, are from the 5th and 6th centuries.

The catacombs are entered from the Basilica di Madre del Buon Consiglio, one of the most dramatic approaches to any underground site in Italy. Guided tours (obligatory) last about 45 minutes and cover the main levels. The social cooperative that runs the tours is also working on restoration of the surrounding Sanità neighbourhood — visiting is a direct contribution to that project.
