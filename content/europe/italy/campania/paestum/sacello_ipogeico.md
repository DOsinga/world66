  No one knows with certainty what the Sacello Ipogeico was for. The leading theory is that it was a heroon — a shrine to the founder or founding hero of Posidonia, the kind of sacred space that a Greek colonial city was expected to maintain as a civic duty. The city buried its founder's memory underground and tended it for centuries, right in the heart of the sacred precinct.
---

The Sacello Ipogeico is easy to miss — which is, in a way, the point. Found in 1954 inside the ancient temenos (the sacred enclosure), it is an underground chamber whose only visible above-ground element is the shallow ridge of its roof pressing up through the earth. The archaeologists who excavated it found a sealed room containing bronze vessels, honey, iron objects, and painted slabs similar in style to the Tomb of the Diver. It had been deliberately buried and sealed, probably in the fifth century BC, and never disturbed.

Whatever rituals took place here happened underground and out of sight. The painted decorations inside suggest a connection to the same funerary or heroic traditions as the Tomb of the Diver. You will find it in the archaeological area south of the Temple of Neptune — look for the low stone surround that marks where the roof emerges from the ground. It is a strange, quiet thing to stand next to, this invisible room.
