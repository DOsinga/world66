The Schola Medica Salernitana was the first systematic medical school in the Western world, founded in the ninth century and reaching its peak between the tenth and thirteenth centuries. At a time when much of Europe had lost contact with Greek and Roman learning, the Schola assembled scholars from Arab, Jewish, Greek, and Latin traditions who translated ancient medical texts and developed an empirical approach to medicine centuries before universities existed elsewhere. The school produced treatments, surgical techniques, and anatomical knowledge that spread across Europe through its graduates and manuscripts.

Today the physical legacy of the Schola is scattered across the old town — a street name here, a memorial plaque there, the Giardino della Minerva that served as its physic garden. The best place to understand its history is the dedicated section of the Diocesan Museum and the Giardino itself. There is no grand building to visit, but that absence is part of the point: the Schola's importance was intellectual rather than architectural, its influence spread through books and trained physicians rather than stone. Walking the old town with this in mind transforms what looks like an ordinary medieval Italian city into something more remarkable.
