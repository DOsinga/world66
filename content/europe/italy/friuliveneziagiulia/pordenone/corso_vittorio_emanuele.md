  The frescoes that decorate the facades along the Corso were applied over many centuries as successive wealthy merchants tried to outdo their neighbours. The fashion for painted facades in Pordenone peaked in the sixteenth century, when Giovanni Antonio de' Sacchis — known as Il Pordenone — was working nearby, and some art historians believe he may have designed or influenced several of the decorative programmes. Most of the frescoes have faded significantly, but traces remain on almost every building.

The Corso Vittorio Emanuele II is the main artery of Pordenone's historic centre and one of the most elegant streets in Friuli. It runs for about half a kilometre under continuous arcades — the loggias that give Venetian-influenced towns their particular character — lined with Gothic and Renaissance palaces decorated with the remnants of painted facades. The whole ensemble has an architectural coherence rare even in Italy.

Walking the corso end-to-end takes twenty minutes at a stroll; taking it properly, looking up at the surviving frescoes and the varied architectural details of the arcade piers, occupies an agreeable hour. The café tables under the loggias are particularly pleasant in the evening.
