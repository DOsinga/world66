Cerveteri, ancient Caere, is one of the most important Etruscan sites in Italy and well worth the short detour from [Rome](/europe/italy/lazio/rome) or [Tarquinia](/europe/italy/lazio/tarquinia). The Necropoli della Banditaccia — a vast city of the dead — is where most of the Etruscan objects now filling the museums of Rome and beyond were excavated.

The [necropolis](/europe/italy/lazio/cerveteri/necropoli_banditaccia) is unlike any other ancient site in Italy. It was planned on the same principles used by the Etruscans for their cities of the living: laid out in streets, with tomb-houses that have doorways, windows, and rooms carved from the tufa rock. Walking through it with a torch is an extraordinary experience — the chambers are cool and quiet, the carvings remarkably intact. Several hours can easily pass. The site covers about three kilometres and involves a fair amount of climbing.

The [Regolini-Galassi tomb](/europe/italy/lazio/cerveteri/regolini_galassi_tomb), one of the most significant Etruscan burial discoveries ever made, is within the necropolis, though its extraordinary contents — golden jewellery, bronze furniture, a ceremonial chariot — were taken to Rome and the Vatican after the 1836 excavation. The [National Archaeological Museum](/europe/italy/lazio/cerveteri/national_museum_cerveteri) in the town's [Castello Ruspoli](/europe/italy/lazio/cerveteri/castle_ruspoli) holds what remained.

Bring a good torch. The site has no artificial lighting inside the tombs.
