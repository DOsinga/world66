  Lake Garda lies further north than any other major olive oil-producing area in the world. The lake acts as a thermal reservoir, storing summer heat and releasing it through the winter to keep lakeside temperatures mild enough for olive trees. The DOP Garda oil — light, delicate, slightly bitter — is prized by Italian chefs and largely unknown outside the country.

The western shore of Lake Garda is olive country, and has been since antiquity. The trees — some of them ancient, gnarled, and centuries old — cover the steep terraces above the lake from Salò northward, producing a delicate, grassy oil with a mild flavour quite different from the more assertive southern Italian varieties.

Many lakeside producers welcome visitors for tastings and direct sales, particularly in October and November during the harvest. The combination of the olive groves, the lake views, and the autumn light is one of the most pleasant experiences the lake offers outside of summer. Alongside the olives, the ruined limonaia (lemon-growing structures) on the western shore are testimony to another vanished agricultural tradition — citrus cultivation was central to the local economy until the late 19th century, when it was undercut by cheaper fruit from Sicily and Sardinia.
