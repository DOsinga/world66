San Donato Milanese is a satellite town about 7 kilometres southeast of Milan, primarily known as the global headquarters of ENI, the Italian energy company. For most visitors it is a stopover rather than a destination — the metro connects to central Milan in 15 minutes, Linate Airport is 5 kilometres north, and the town is correspondingly full of business hotels at reasonable prices. But for anyone interested in mid-century Italian architecture and urban planning, San Donato is genuinely worth a half-day of attention.

The reason is Metanopoli. When ENI began its dramatic postwar expansion under founder Enrico Mattei, the company did not merely build offices — it built an entire town from scratch on agricultural land southeast of Milan. The Metanopoli neighbourhood, developed through the 1950s and 1960s, was a complete modernist settlement: rationalist apartment buildings, schools, a cinema, sports facilities, shops, and churches, all designed by leading Italian architects of the era and laid out on generous planning principles. The BBPR studio — architects of Milan's Torre Velasca — designed the main ENI headquarters campus. Mario Bacciocchi, one of Italy's foremost architects of sacred modernism, designed the church of San Francesco. The result is an unusually coherent and well-preserved example of the Italian company town, a form of idealistic urbanism that was once common but has mostly been demolished or disfigured elsewhere.

Beyond Metanopoli, the older village core around the church of San Donato has modest historic character, and the surrounding suburban fabric is pleasant if undistinguished. The Po Valley south of here is flat, quiet, and excellent cycling country.
