  The Samnites were Rome's most tenacious enemy. They fought three major wars against Rome between 343 and 290 BC — and nearly won the second. After the Battle of the Caudine Forks in 321 BC, the entire Roman army surrendered and was forced to pass under a yoke of spears in humiliation. It took Rome decades to avenge this defeat and finally subjugate the Samnite confederation.

Campobasso's provincial archaeological museum focuses on the Samnites, the pre-Roman Italic people who dominated the central Apennines and gave the region its distinctive character before Roman conquest. The collection includes weapons, pottery, jewellery, and votive objects from sites across Molise, and gives real depth to the region's ancient landscape — Molise is dotted with Samnite ruins that most visitors drive past without knowing what they are.

The museum is small and unhurried. The explanatory material is in Italian only, but the objects speak clearly enough: the Samnites were skilled metalworkers and traders with a developed material culture that their later reputation (as merely Rome's enemies) doesn't capture.
