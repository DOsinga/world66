Castellana Grotte is a small agricultural town in the Murge plateau of central Puglia, unremarkable on the surface but sitting atop one of the most extraordinary cave systems in Europe. The [Grotte di Castellana](/europe/italy/puglia/castellana_grotte/grotte_di_castellana) — a series of limestone caverns stretching nearly four kilometres underground — draw visitors from across Italy who would otherwise have little reason to stop here.

The town itself is pleasant enough: a compact [Centro Storico](/europe/italy/puglia/castellana_grotte/centro_storico) around the church of [San Leone Magno](/europe/italy/puglia/castellana_grotte/san_leone_magno), a few decent restaurants, and the unhurried pace of the Pugliese interior. But the caves are the reason to come. Discovered in 1938 by speleologist Franco Anelli — to whom the [Museo Speleologico Franco Anelli](/europe/italy/puglia/castellana_grotte/museo_speleologico) at the cave entrance is dedicated — they descend to 70 metres below ground and contain formations that took millennia to build: towering stalagmites, delicate aragonite crystals, and the famous [Caverna Bianca](/europe/italy/puglia/castellana_grotte/caverna_bianca), whose white calcite walls glow like a cathedral lit from within.

Castellana makes a practical base for exploring the Valle d'Itria — [Alberobello](/europe/italy/puglia/alberobello) is 15 kilometres away and [Locorotondo](/europe/italy/puglia/locorotondo) not much farther. The train line connecting Bari to Taranto stops here, making it easy to visit as a half-day excursion from the coast.
