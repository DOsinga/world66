This small Norman church is one of the genuine survivors of 1908 — it predates the earthquake by seven centuries and emerged with its essential structure intact. Built in the 12th century, possibly on the site of an ancient temple, it shows the characteristic Norman-Arab fusion of Sicilian architecture: blind arcading, interlaced ornament, and an octagonal dome that reflects Byzantine influence absorbed through the Arab intermediaries who preceded Norman rule in Sicily.

The church sits below street level — a measure of how much the post-earthquake city was rebuilt on a raised grid — which gives it a slightly stranded quality among its newer surroundings. But that half-buried position makes the medieval stonework feel more present, not less: you descend to a different time. In front of the church stands a bronze equestrian statue of Don John of Austria, who assembled the Christian fleet here before the Battle of Lepanto in 1571. The combination of Norman architecture, the Lepanto connection, and the earthquake story packs more history into a small space than most sites three times the size.
