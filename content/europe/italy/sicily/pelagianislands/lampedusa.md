Lampedusa sits closer to Tunisia than to Sicily, and the landscape makes no secret of it. Flat, sun-scorched rock, wild rosemary, and a sea that turns an improbable shade of turquoise — this small island belongs geologically to Africa, even if it flies the Italian flag.

The town is compact and unpretentious: a working harbour, a single main street, a handful of trattorias that know how to handle a grilled fish. Almost all the beaches and coves are a short distance away, reached by scooter, boat, or on foot along dusty tracks. The most famous is [Spiaggia dei Conigli](/europe/italy/sicily/pelagianislands/lampedusa/spiaggia_dei_conigli) — Rabbit Beach — which regularly tops lists of the world's best beaches and deserves the reputation.

Lampedusa is also a nesting ground for loggerhead sea turtles, and the waters around the island are some of the clearest in the Mediterranean. [Divers](/europe/italy/sicily/pelagianislands/lampedusa/diving_lampedusa) come from all over Italy for the visibility and the wrecks. The [Lampedusa Nature Reserve](/europe/italy/sicily/pelagianislands/lampedusa/lampedusa_nature_reserve) protects the southwestern coast and its sea caves. On the cliffs above the sea, the [Porta d'Europa](/europe/italy/sicily/pelagianislands/lampedusa/porta_d_europa) monument faces Africa as a memorial to the thousands who have died crossing the strait. Outside July and August the island is genuinely quiet, and the contrast with the summer crowds is dramatic.
