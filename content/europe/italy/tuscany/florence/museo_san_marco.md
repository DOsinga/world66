story: >
  Girolamo Savonarola was prior here from 1489 and used San Marco as the base for his religious reform movement that eventually overthrew the Medici in 1494. He organised the Bonfire of the Vanities from this convent, burning mirrors, cosmetics, books, and artworks. He was hanged and burned in Piazza della Signoria in 1498 — the same square where his bonfire had been. His cell and the objects in it are preserved in the museum.

A Dominican convent converted into a museum centred on Fra Angelico's extraordinary frescoes. Cosimo de' Medici commissioned Michelozzo to rebuild the convent in 1437, and Fra Angelico painted each monk's cell with a devotional fresco designed for meditation — 44 cells, each with a different scene from the life of Christ. The result is unlike any other experience in Florence: a quiet, intimate space where some of the most beautiful paintings in Western art wait at the end of narrow corridors.

The Annunciation at the top of the stairs is Fra Angelico's most celebrated work. The monks' library — a long, barrel-vaulted hall lined with reading desks — is among the most elegant Renaissance interiors in Florence. The museum also preserves Savonarola's cell exactly as it was. Much less visited than the major museums.
