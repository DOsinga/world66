  The frieze was commissioned in the early 16th century from Giovanni della Robbia, nephew of the more famous Luca della Robbia. It depicts the Seven Works of Mercy — feeding the hungry, giving drink to the thirsty, clothing the naked, welcoming strangers, visiting the sick, visiting prisoners, and burying the dead — in brilliantly coloured glazed terracotta. As a medical teaching image displayed on a working hospital, it was both a theological statement and an advertisement.

The Ospedale del Ceppo is a former hospital that operated continuously from the 13th century until 2013, making it one of the longest-running hospitals in the world. What draws visitors today is the remarkable frieze that runs along the top of its 16th-century portico: a continuous band of glazed terracotta by Giovanni della Robbia depicting the Seven Works of Mercy in vivid colour against a blue background. The figures are lively and specific — you can read the facial expressions even from the street below.

The hospital building has been restored and is now open as a museum, with an exhibition on its history and an anatomical theatre from the 18th century that was used for medical education. The anatomical theatre is small but interesting — wooden galleries around a central dissection table, with instruments displayed in cases. The frieze is viewable from the street, but entering the museum gives access to the ground-floor loggia where the terracotta can be examined at closer range.
