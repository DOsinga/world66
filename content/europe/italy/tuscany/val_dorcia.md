The Val d'Orcia is a stretch of southern Tuscany between Siena and Monte Amiata where the landscape looks exactly like the paintings it inspired: gentle hills folding into one another, a lone cypress on a ridge, a winding white road through vineyards, golden light at dusk. The valley takes its name from the Orcia river, which drains south toward the Maremma, but the name has come to mean the whole quietly dramatic scenery that was declared a UNESCO World Heritage landscape in 2004. What the UNESCO designation recognises is not just the scenery but its making: this is a humanised landscape, shaped by centuries of farming and fortification, that has barely changed since Sienese painters used it as background in the 14th century.

The hill towns are as good as the views between them. [Pienza](/europe/italy/tuscany/pienza) was rebuilt in the 1460s as a Renaissance ideal city by Pope Pius II, who was born here, and its compact cathedral square and papal palace are intact and extraordinary. The pecorino cheese made in the surrounding Val d'Orcia is excellent — the aged version, rolled in ash or walnut leaves, is sold from every other shop in Pienza's main street. [Montalcino](/europe/italy/tuscany/montalcino) sits on a hill above the western edge of the valley and produces Brunello, one of Italy's most celebrated red wines; the views from the medieval fortress stretch across the valley to Monte Amiata. [Bagno Vignoni](/europe/italy/tuscany/bagno_vignoni) is extraordinary in a different way: the thermal spring that has drawn people here since Roman times fills a large stone pool that serves as the town's main square. [San Quirico d'Orcia](/europe/italy/tuscany/san_quirico_dorcia) is the most central town, with an excellent Romanesque collegiate church and formal Renaissance gardens within the walls.

The best way to experience the valley is by car — the white gravel roads (strade bianche) between the towns pass through countryside that can't be seen any other way. The road near Monticchiello, with its cypress avenue curving over a bare hill, is the most photographed stretch; arrive early in the morning before the cars and photographers arrive. Late April and May bring wildflowers and the green of new wheat; June turns the fields gold and the poppies disappear; September and October bring the grape harvest and the gentler light of autumn.
