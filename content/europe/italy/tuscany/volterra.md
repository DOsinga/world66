Volterra sits 545 metres above sea level on a dramatic ridge in the Tuscan hills, looking out over the Cecina and Era valleys. It is less visited than [Siena](/europe/italy/tuscany/siena) or [San Gimignano](/europe/italy/tuscany/sangimignano), which is precisely the point — you can walk its narrow stone streets without feeling like you are in a theme park.

The town is ancient in the truest sense. The Etruscans settled here before Rome was a city, and traces of their civilisation — walls, urns, the [Porta all'Arco](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/porta_allarca) gateway — sit alongside medieval towers and Renaissance palaces. The Etruscan finds fill the magnificent [Museo Guarnacci](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/museo_etrusco_guarnacci), one of the oldest public museums in Europe. The Romans left a fine theatre, the [Teatro Romano](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/teatro_romano), still buried up to its lower tier in medieval rubble that paradoxically saved it. The medieval town gathers around the [Piazza dei Priori](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/piazza_dei_priori) and its 13th-century [Palazzo dei Priori](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/palazzo_dei_priori), the oldest town hall in Tuscany.

The local stone is alabaster, a creamy translucent material that has been quarried and carved here for over two thousand years. Volterra's craftsmen still work it today, and the [alabaster workshops](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/alabaster_workshops) along the main street are worth a look.

The landscape around the town is remarkable. Directly to the west, [Le Balze](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/le_balze) — deep clay chasms carved by erosion — have slowly eaten into the hillside for centuries, swallowing an Etruscan necropolis and a medieval church. The effect is strange and quietly unsettling. The brooding [Fortezza Medicea](/europe/italy/tuscany/volterra/fortezza_medicea) crowns the highest point of the town; it has been a prison for five centuries and still is one.

Volterra is a half-day town for most visitors coming from Florence or Siena, but it rewards a night's stay. Once the day-trippers leave, the streets belong to residents, and the town shows its real character — modest, self-contained, and pleasingly indifferent to tourism.
