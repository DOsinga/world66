  Saint Anthony of Padua was Portuguese, born in Lisbon in 1195, and only spent the last year of his life in Padova. He died in 1231, was canonised in 1232 — less than a year after his death, one of the fastest canonisations in Church history — and the construction of this enormous basilica began immediately. His tomb has attracted pilgrims continuously for nearly 800 years, and today the number of ex-votos (offerings left in thanks for miracles attributed to him) filling the side chapels is beyond counting.

The Basilica of Sant'Antonio is one of the great pilgrimage churches of Christendom, with eight domes visible from across the city and a hybrid architecture that mixes Romanesque, Gothic, Byzantine, and Islamic elements in a manner that should not work but does. Construction began in 1232 and the building evolved for the better part of two centuries; the result is a vast, dim, impressive interior lit by banks of candles from pilgrims who file past the saint's tomb in the left transept.

The high altar has bronze reliefs by Donatello — some of the finest Renaissance bronzes in Italy, often overlooked because they are in a church rather than a museum. The Treasury holds relics including what the basilica claims is Anthony's tongue, preserved incorrupt. For non-Catholic visitors the building is still remarkable as architecture; for the devout, it is one of the most emotionally intense spaces in Italy.

The piazza outside holds Donatello's equestrian statue of Gattamelata, the first large-scale equestrian bronze cast since antiquity and a work that influenced every equestrian monument that came after it.
