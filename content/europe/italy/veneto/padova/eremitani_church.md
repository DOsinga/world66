story: >
  The Ovetari Chapel inside the Eremitani originally contained a fresco cycle by Mantegna, his first major commission, painted in the 1440s and considered the moment that perspective-based painting arrived in northern Italy. On 11 March 1944, Allied bombing reduced the chapel to rubble and destroyed almost the entire cycle. What survived was painstakingly reassembled from fragments and photographs; the one section that escaped the bombs — the Assumption of the Virgin — can still be seen. The scale of what was lost is measured by the photographs taken before the war.
---

The Church of the Eremitani stands a few metres from the Scrovegni Chapel, which means it is often skipped by visitors who have already spent their emotional energy on Giotto. This is a mistake. The 13th-century Gothic church is a fine building in its own right, with a ship's-keel ceiling similar to the Palazzo della Ragione, and the Ovetari Chapel at the end of the right transept tells one of the great stories of art and destruction in the 20th century.

The reconstruction effort — piecing together thousands of fragments of Mantegna's frescoes using pre-war photographs as a guide — is ongoing and well explained inside the chapel. The result is partly restored, partly missing, and entirely moving. For anyone interested in Renaissance painting, the Eremitani is essential. Entry is free or included with the Scrovegni Chapel ticket.
