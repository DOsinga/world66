  Erasmo da Narni, known as Gattamelata ("Honeyed Cat"), was a condottiere who served the Republic of Venice faithfully enough that when he died in Padova in 1443, Venice funded this monument. Donatello spent a decade casting it and it was unveiled in 1453 — the first equestrian bronze of a non-royal subject since antiquity. Michelangelo reportedly studied it before beginning his own work. The rider wears Roman armour but his face is a realistic portrait: the first time in over a thousand years that a public monument had shown a real person's actual face.
---

Donatello's equestrian statue of the condottiere Gattamelata stands in the Piazza del Santo, directly in front of the Sant'Antonio Basilica. Cast between 1443 and 1453, it was the first large-scale bronze equestrian monument produced since the late Roman Empire — a technical achievement that astonished contemporaries and influenced every equestrian sculpture that followed, including Verrocchio's Colleoni statue in Venice and eventually the equestrian traditions of European royal courts.

The scale is imposing: horse and rider together stand over three metres high on a tall marble plinth. The realism of the casting, particularly in the horse's musculature and the texture of the rider's armour, was revolutionary for its time. The statue is easy to overlook if you are focused on the basilica behind it — make a point of walking around it slowly and looking at the detail.
