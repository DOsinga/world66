  Giotto painted the chapel's 39 fresco scenes between 1303 and 1305 — about two years of continuous work on scaffolding, applying pigment to fresh plaster section by section. The commission came from Enrico Scrovegni, who built the chapel partly to atone for his father's usury (Dante put the father in the seventh circle of Hell). Giotto painted Enrico himself into the Last Judgment scene, presenting a model of the chapel to the Virgin — a rare case of the patron appearing in the work that was meant to redeem him.
---

The Scrovegni Chapel is one of the most important single rooms in the history of Western art. Giotto's fresco cycle covering every wall and the barrel-vaulted ceiling depicts scenes from the life of the Virgin and the life of Christ, completed around 1305. Art historians credit it as the moment when painting broke from the flat, symbolic tradition of Byzantine art and began moving toward the naturalistic representation that would define European painting for the next six centuries.

The chapel holds only 25 visitors at a time; you enter through a climate-controlled antechamber and have exactly 15 minutes inside. Booking well in advance is essential — the queue is long in summer. The 15 minutes is enough to appreciate what Giotto achieved but not quite enough to look at everything properly; some visitors book twice.

The chapel is part of a larger museum complex that includes Roman and Venetian archaeological collections, worth an hour if you have the time.
