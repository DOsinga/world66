  The cave complex at Orheiul Vechi was carved by Orthodox monks in the 13th century into the same limestone cliffs that Bronze Age peoples and later the Mongol Golden Horde had used as fortifications. The site has been continuously occupied by hermit monks — with a few forced interruptions during the Soviet period — for over 700 years. During Soviet rule, religious practice was banned, but the caves were never sealed and monks returned within days of independence in 1991.

The cave monastery at Orheiul Vechi is cut directly into a sheer limestone cliff above a loop of the Raut River — a remarkable feat of medieval construction that has been in continuous use, on and off, for over seven centuries. The complex includes a series of interconnected chambers: a restored altar room with Orthodox iconography, living quarters for the resident monk, and a terrace that juts from the cliff face with an unrailed drop of many metres to the river below.

If the resident monk is present — which is most days during the warm season — he will let visitors descend into the altar chamber. The interior is simple and functional: whitewashed walls, brass candleholders, and a small collection of icons. The monk speaks Romanian and very basic English, but the experience of visiting an active monastery embedded in a cliff, maintained by a single cleric living essentially as a medieval hermit, is difficult to describe. Above the cave complex, a more conventional nineteenth-century church with a blue exterior stands on the clifftop and is usually open to visitors.
