Johan van Oldenbarnevelt was born in Amersfoort in 1547 and went on to become the most powerful politician in the Dutch Republic, serving as Land's Advocate of Holland for over thirty years. He negotiated the Twelve Years' Truce with Spain, guided the Republic through its formative decades, and championed the cause of religious tolerance at a time when such a position carried serious risk.

It killed him. In 1618, caught in a power struggle with Prince Maurice of Orange and accused of treason on politically motivated charges, Van Oldenbarnevelt was arrested, tried in a rigged process, and beheaded on the Binnenhof in The Hague in 1619. He was 71 years old. The historian Johan Huizinga later called it the greatest judicial murder in Dutch history.

The bust in his home city honours the man rather than the manner of his death, though you cannot entirely separate the two. His face in the sculpture has something of the long-serving official about it — composed, watchful, a little weary. Amersfoort remembers him as a son who went out and shaped a nation, and who paid a ruinous price for it.
