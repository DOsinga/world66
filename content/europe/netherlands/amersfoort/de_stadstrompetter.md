Before newspapers, before loudspeakers, before anything electronic, news in a Dutch city arrived through a human throat. The stadstrompetter — the city trumpeter — was a municipal official who stood in public squares, sounded his instrument to draw a crowd, and then proclaimed whatever the city fathers needed everyone to know: market days, curfews, decrees, deaths of notable citizens, the arrival of important visitors.

The bronze figure near the Hof captures this role with instrument raised and posture purposeful — a man about to make himself heard. The tradition of municipal trumpeters in the Dutch Republic lasted for centuries and gave public squares their function as civic gathering points. The Hof, Amersfoort's old market square, would have seen hundreds of such proclamations over the medieval and early modern period.

There is something pleasingly analogue about the sculpture in the age of push notifications: a reminder that the basic human need to share news publicly is very old, and that it once required a real person, a brass instrument, and a good pair of lungs.
