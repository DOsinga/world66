In medieval and early modern Europe, keeping pigeons was a privilege reserved for the nobility and the church. Pigeons were a significant food source — fresh meat year-round, at a time when winter livestock were slaughtered in autumn — and the right to maintain a pigeon loft (duiventoren or duifhuis) was a status marker that commoners were legally excluded from.

The restriction was also practical and resented: pigeons fed on grain from surrounding fields, and peasant farmers watched birds from noble lofts eat their crops while being prohibited from harming them. Pigeon rights were one of the many feudal privileges that sparked rural resentment in the pre-industrial period.

The Duifhuizen in Amersfoort marked a building or complex where the city's privileged pigeon lofts were maintained — a reminder that the urban hierarchy expressed itself in what you were allowed to keep, not just what you were allowed to own.
