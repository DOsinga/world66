De Gaaper is the Dutch name for the wide-mouthed yawning figure used as a trade sign by pharmacies, inns, and certain other establishments from the 17th century onwards. The name comes from "gapen" — to gape or yawn — and the sign was typically a carved or painted face with the mouth open wide, sometimes showing a pill or medicine on the tongue (for pharmacies) or simply gaping in welcome (for inns and lodgings).

The tradition of gaper signs is distinctively Dutch. While most European towns used pictorial signs to identify trades — a boot for a cobbler, a barrel for a brewer — the gaper was specifically associated with the Dutch apothecary and inn tradition. A few original carved gaper signs still exist in Dutch cities, though most have been replaced by modern imitations or simply by the name.

This logement — a lodging house — took the name as its identity. Travelling merchants, army officers, and wealthy visitors would have stopped here, in a building whose sign told the street what it was before anyone read a word.
