Saint Roch (Sint Rochus) was the patron saint of plague victims, invoked across Catholic Europe during the centuries when bubonic plague periodically devastated urban populations. The chapel dedicated to him in Amersfoort dates from around 1500, built during a period when the plague was not a historical memory but a recurring present threat — Amersfoort lost significant portions of its population to plague outbreaks in the 15th and 16th centuries.

Plague chapels were typically sited outside or at the edge of the main settlement, partly because plague victims were isolated from the healthy population, and partly because the chapels served as places where the sick and dying could receive last rites away from the city centre. The location of this chapel reflects that logic.

Saint Roch is depicted in art with a sore on his thigh — the characteristic bubo of the plague — and a dog licking the wound. The image turns the most feared disease of the medieval world into an act of canine devotion. The chapel carries all of that weight in a small building that most people now walk past without registering.
