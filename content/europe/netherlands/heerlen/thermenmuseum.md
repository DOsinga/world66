  The Roman bathhouse at Heerlen — ancient Coriovallum — was discovered in 1940 during building work. Rather than removing the remains, the city built a museum over and around them. The ruins lie in situ beneath a protective structure, and you walk through the excavation on raised walkways. The hot room, cold plunge pool, underfloor heating channels, and changing rooms are all visible and labelled. Coins, tools, and personal objects found at the site are displayed alongside.

The Thermenmuseum is built directly over the remains of a Roman bathhouse dating from around 100 AD, making it one of the most intact Roman sites in the Netherlands. Heerlen — then Coriovallum — sat on the main Roman road from Boulogne to Cologne, and the bathhouse served the garrison and civilian population of what was a significant stopping point on the empire's northern frontier.

The museum does an unusually good job of explaining Roman daily life rather than just presenting stones. Interactive displays, scale models, and well-chosen artefacts put the ruins in context. The hypocaust system — channels and pillars under the floor that circulated hot air — is particularly clear here, and gives a vivid sense of the engineering behind Roman comfort.

The museum is small and can be covered in about an hour. It is in the centre of town and easily combined with a look at the Glaspaleis a few streets away.
