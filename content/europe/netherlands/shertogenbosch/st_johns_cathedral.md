  The cathedral took about 150 years to build, from the early 14th to the early 16th century, spanning multiple architectural generations. When the city turned Protestant in 1629, the cathedral was stripped of its Catholic furnishings and used for Reformed services — but the building itself survived intact. It returned to Catholic use in 1810 under Napoleon, and the 19th and 20th centuries brought a major restoration campaign that added many of the flying buttresses and their famous stone figures, including the walking man on the north buttress who appears to be striding purposefully nowhere.

St. John's Cathedral is one of the great Gothic churches of the Netherlands, a building that has been continuously surprising visitors for six centuries with its stone forest of flying buttresses, its 600 figures of stone, and its interior which manages to feel both vast and intimate. The exterior, with its extraordinary array of carved figures perched on the buttresses at various heights, is unlike anything else in the country.

The interior holds a famous 15th-century bronze baptismal font and a 16th-century organ. Climb the tower for views over the city and the North Brabant plain. The cathedral is still an active Catholic church, which means it closes during services. Allow at least an hour and preferably more.
