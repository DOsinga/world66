  In October 1944, as German forces retreated before the advancing Soviet army, they carried out a systematic scorched-earth policy across Finnmark and Troms. Every building was burned. Residents were forcibly evacuated by boat, some choosing to hide in the mountains rather than leave. When Norwegians returned to Hammerfest in 1945, they found nothing standing except a single chapel. The rebuilding effort, largely carried out by the residents themselves in the late 1940s and 1950s, is one of the great acts of collective determination in modern Norwegian history.
---

The Museum of Reconstruction documents the German scorched-earth destruction of Finnmark in 1944 and the remarkable effort to rebuild the region from nothing afterward. The story is told through personal testimonies, photographs, objects, and reconstructed room settings — a wartime kitchen, a makeshift dwelling from the early postwar years — that give a visceral sense of what the evacuation and rebuilding actually felt like for ordinary people.

The museum is one of the most affecting small museums in Norway. The wartime section is appropriately sombre; the reconstruction section is ultimately a story of defiance and community. Allow at least an hour. The museum occupies a modern building in the town centre, and the admission includes access to a good collection of photographs from the rebuilding era.
