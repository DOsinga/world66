Hardangervidda is Europe's largest mountain plateau, a vast elevated tableland in central Norway covering more than 3,400 square kilometres. Most of it sits at between 1,200 and 1,400 metres above sea level, giving it a landscape unlike anything else in the country — treeless, wind-swept, and magnificent in a way that takes some getting used to. The plateau supports the largest wild reindeer herd in Europe, and in summer the tundra-like terrain blooms with wildflowers, berries, and dwarf willows.

The contrast between the plateau itself and the fjord valleys that cut into it from the west is extraordinary. Approaching from [Geilo](/europe/norway/geilo) or descending towards [Eidfjord](/europe/norway/eidfjord) on the Hardangerfjord, you move through densely forested hillsides reflected in deep blue lakes, past narrow fjords stretching far inland, before the landscape opens out into treeless, snowfield-dotted expanse. Even in August, large patches of unmelted snow dot the plateau. The Vøringfoss Waterfall, which plunges about 280 metres into a narrow gorge, is among the most dramatic in Norway and lies near the western edge of the park.

The national park is outstanding walking territory. Well-maintained DNT (Norwegian Trekking Association) huts dot the plateau, allowing multi-day traverses without carrying full camping gear. Summer walking reveals views back across the Hardangerfjord to the south and the snow-capped ridges of Hallingskarvet to the north. Winters bring deep snowfields — tracks that carry hikers in July are buried a metre under snow by January — and the plateau is a classic Nordic skiing destination. The town of Geilo on the eastern edge is the main gateway and has facilities for both summer and winter visits.
