Henningsvær sits at the foot of Mount Vågakaillen, a cluster of isles and islets scattered across the blue waters of the Vestfjord. With mountains at its back and the sea on every other side, it was a natural hub during the Lofoten winter fishery, and in the 1800s the village prospered into one of the most prominent fishing communities in Lofoten.

Unlike many other fishing villages, Henningsvær's population has remained stable in recent years, with over 500 people still living here. The islands were not connected to the rest of Lofoten by bridges until 1983, and that isolation probably saved the community from the concrete-block architecture that blighted most Norwegian towns in the 1960s and 70s. The result is something quite special — a vibrant, working fishing village with well-preserved traditional buildings, a handful of art galleries, and [one of the most photogenic football pitches in the world](/europe/norway/lofotenislands/henningsvr/henningsvr_stadion), wedged between rock and sea on a tiny islet.

Rock climbing has put Henningsvær on a different map in recent decades — the granite walls above the village offer some of the best routes in northern Europe, with the Vestfjord spread out below and peaks rising in every direction. The [Kaviarfabrikken](/europe/norway/lofotenislands/henningsvr/kaviar_factory), a converted fish-processing factory on the harbour, now shows contemporary art in a setting that somehow makes perfect sense. And for history, the village played a small but real role in the Second World War: British and Norwegian commandos raided the harbour during [Operation Claymore](/europe/norway/lofotenislands/henningsvr/operation_claymore) in 1941, destroying factories supplying Nazi Germany and returning with Enigma machine components.
