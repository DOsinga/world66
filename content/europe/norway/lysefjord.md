Lysefjord cuts 42 km inland from the sea east of Stavanger, its granite walls rising up to 1,000 metres straight from the water in places — one of the most dramatic fjords in a country full of dramatic fjords. Its name, "light fjord," comes from the pale granite of its walls, which catch the light in a way the darker rock of neighbouring fjords doesn't.

Two landmarks make Lysefjord one of Norway's must-do experiences. [Pulpit Rock](/europe/norway/lysefjord/pulpit_rock) (Preikestolen), a flat-topped cliff 604 metres above the water, is reachable by a well-marked hiking trail from the north side and is one of the most photographed views in the country. At the fjord's inland end, near the switchback road down to the village of Lysebotn, [Kjeragbolten](/europe/norway/lysefjord/kjeragbolten) — a boulder wedged in a crevasse a kilometre above the fjord — draws hikers willing to stand on it for the photo, and BASE jumpers who use the cliffs alongside it.

Both landmarks can be reached on foot, but most visitors see the fjord itself from the water: passenger boats run from [Stavanger](/europe/norway/stavanger) into the fjord, passing beneath both Pulpit Rock and Kjerag on the way to Lysebotn, and the Lysebotn road — a hairpin climb of 27 bends carved into the cliff face — makes the return by land its own attraction for anyone driving. Between the two landmarks, waterfalls drop straight into the fjord and the walls close in tight enough that the boats sound their horns to hear the echo.
