Røros is a small mining town in the mountains south of [Trondheim](/europe/norway/trondheim), near the Swedish border. Copper was discovered here in 1644, and for over three centuries the mines drove the town's economy. The result is a remarkably well-preserved wooden town — rows of dark-timbered houses, grass-roofed smelter buildings, and slag heaps stretching into the surrounding plateau. The entire town centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The [Smelting Works](/europe/norway/rros/bergstadens_ziir) is the industrial core of the heritage site — a massive stone hall where copper ore was processed for three centuries, now an evocative museum. Guided tours to the [Olavsgruva copper mine](/europe/norway/rros/olavsgruva) run regularly and take you deep underground, where the temperature is a constant 5°C regardless of the season above. The [Røros Church](/europe/norway/rros/roros_church), built in 1784, has an interior that looks like stone and marble until you tap the columns — all of it is wood, painted with extraordinary skill by craftsmen who clearly enjoyed the illusion.

The [wooden town itself](/europe/norway/rros/wooden_town) is the main attraction. Walking Bergmannsgata and the lanes leading down toward the mine works gives a vivid sense of life in an 18th-century industrial settlement. The buildings are still inhabited, which prevents the place from feeling like an open-air museum. The slag heaps at the western edge of town — black mountains of copper waste — are part of the World Heritage landscape and stranger and more dramatic than any postcard shot.

The surrounding Røros plateau is beautiful in both summer and winter — wide, open, and very cold. The town hosts the Røros Market (*Rørosmartnan*) in February, one of the oldest traditional markets in Norway, and a well-attended Christmas market in December. The [Røros Museum](/europe/norway/rros/roros_museum) manages the historic sites and can advise on what's open on any given day.
