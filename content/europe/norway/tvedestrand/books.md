**Pan** by Knut Hamsun (1894). Hamsun's short, intense novel is set on the Norwegian coast in summer — a hunter in a remote coastal posting, desire, isolation and the strange light of northern evenings. The specific setting is further north than Tvedestrand, but the atmosphere of the Sørlandet shore in summer — the long light, the boats, the particular restlessness of warm Norwegian evenings — runs through the book. Hamsun is complicated (his later politics were disgraceful), but *Pan* remains one of the finest evocations of the Norwegian coastal landscape.

**Kristin Lavransdatter** by Sigrid Undset (1920–22). Undset's three-volume novel follows a Norwegian woman's life across the medieval period, rooted in the landscapes and social world of Norway with a specificity that no other writer has matched. The setting is inland and northern rather than coastal, but Undset — who won the Nobel Prize and is buried not far away in the Agder region — belongs to this part of Norway. Reading her is a way of understanding the depth of Norwegian history beneath the pleasant summer surface of a town like Tvedestrand.

**Two Against the Ice** by Ejnar Mikkelsen (1909, translated). Mikkelsen's account of being stranded for two years in northeast Greenland is not directly about southern Norway, but it belongs to the tradition of Nordic small-boat seamanship and survival that produced the kind of sailors who filled Tvedestrand's harbour in the nineteenth century. A reminder that the sea these coastal towns faced was serious and unforgiving, whatever the July evenings suggest.
