  On 25 April 1940 the Luftwaffe bombed Vossevangen for two days, reducing the centre of the town to rubble. The church, in the middle of it all, took a direct hit on the roof — but the bomb failed to explode. The thick stone walls held, and the building survived the war essentially intact while the town around it had to be rebuilt from scratch.

Voss Church — known locally as Vangskyrkja — is a stone Gothic church from around 1277, one of the oldest stone buildings still in use in this part of Norway. It is unusual for the region: most rural Norwegian churches are wooden stave constructions, and the survival of a medieval stone church here, after centuries of fires and a Second World War bombing, is itself remarkable.

The interior is dominated by a Baroque pulpit and altarpiece from the seventeenth century, painted in deep greens and reds. The ceiling carries a faded depiction of Christ in glory. A runestone outside the south door dates from the eleventh century and predates the building. Services are still held in the church most Sundays; outside service hours, entry is by donation in summer.
