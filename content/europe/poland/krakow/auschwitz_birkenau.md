  The name "Auschwitz" comes from the Polish name of the town Oświęcim, which had a Jewish community going back to the 16th century. After the German occupation in 1939, the town was renamed and its Jewish residents murdered or expelled. The camp that bore the town's name became the largest killing site in human history: approximately 1.1 million people — of whom around 90% were Jewish — were murdered here between 1940 and 1945.

Auschwitz-Birkenau, 70 kilometres west of Krakow, is the preserved site of the largest Nazi concentration and extermination camp. It is not a museum in any ordinary sense — it is a memorial to more than a million people murdered here, and a direct encounter with the physical reality of industrial genocide. The visit is demanding, sobering, and essential.

The site has two main sections: Auschwitz I, the original camp whose gate bears the infamous "Arbeit Macht Frei" inscription, now holds exhibition buildings in the original barracks, including the displays of personal belongings — glasses, shoes, hair, luggage — recovered after liberation. Auschwitz II-Birkenau, 3 kilometres away, is the much larger extermination camp where the majority of the killings took place; the scale of the site — stretching across open fields with the ruins of the crematoria in the distance — is itself deeply affecting. Guided tours are required for the Auschwitz I blocks; Birkenau can be entered independently. Book well in advance; the site receives over 2 million visitors a year. Organized tours from Krakow are available but independent travel by train and bus is straightforward.
