title: "Books"
type: section
---

Krakow has inspired a substantial body of writing, and readers who arrive with the right books will find the city considerably more layered than it first appears.

**Schindler's Ark** by Thomas Keneally (published in the US as *Schindler's List*) is the obvious starting point — not Spielberg's film but the source novel, which is more granular and less sentimental than the adaptation and gives a sharper sense of what Krakow looked like under occupation. Keneally based it on interviews with Schindler's workers and extensive documentary research.

**The Krakow Ghetto Pharmacy** by Tadeusz Pankiewicz is a first-person account by the Polish pharmacist who chose to remain inside the ghetto walls throughout its existence. Short, direct, and devastating — one of the clearest eyewitness accounts of what the occupation meant at street level.

**Unquiet** by Marcin Wicha is a recent Polish memoir about the author's mother's death and his family's Jewish roots in Poland — quieter and more personal than a historical account, but very good at conveying what it means to carry this history as a Pole.

**The Piano** (in Polish, *Pianistka*) by Wislawa Szymborska — better known through her poetry, but Krakow's Nobel laureate in Literature wrote with a wry directness that illuminates the character of the city's intellectual life. Her poetry collection *View with a Grain of Sand* is the accessible introduction.

**Borderland** by Anna Bikont — a journalism investigation into the 1941 massacre of Jews by their Polish neighbours in Jedwabne. Not Krakow-specific but essential reading for anyone wanting to understand the complexity of Polish-Jewish history.
