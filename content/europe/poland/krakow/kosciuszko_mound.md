  The mound was built between 1820 and 1823 by volunteer labour — citizens came from across Poland, then divided between Russia, Prussia, and Austria, to carry handfuls of soil from the battlefields where Kościuszko had fought. The soil thus incorporated includes earth from Saratoga (from the American Revolutionary War), Maciejowice (where Kościuszko was wounded), and Racławice (his great victory). The mound was a political act as much as a memorial: creating a Polish landmark that could not be demolished or denied.

Standing on the western outskirts of Krakow, the Kościuszko Mound is a hand-built earthen hill rising 34 metres above the surrounding plateau, commemorating Tadeusz Kościuszko — the Polish military leader who fought in both the American Revolution and the Polish uprising against Russian and Prussian partition. It is one of four such mounds in Krakow, a distinctively Polish tradition of monumental burial mounds that goes back to prehistoric times.

The views from the top of the mound are excellent — on a clear day you can see the full panorama of Krakow with Wawel in the middle distance, and the Tatra mountains on the southern horizon. The surrounding area includes an Austrian fortress built around the mound in the 1850s, which now houses a small museum and a cafe. The walk up takes about fifteen minutes from the tramstop; the whole visit can comfortably be combined with a stop at Villa Decjusza, the Renaissance garden just below, or the Camaldolese Monastery at Bielany.
