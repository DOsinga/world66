  The Chapel of the Holy Trinity inside the castle contains some of the finest Gothic-Byzantine frescos in Central Europe, painted in 1418 on the orders of King Władysław II Jagiełło. The painter, a Ruthenian master named Andrzej, covered every surface of the small chapel with gold-leaf icons of saints in a Byzantine style entirely at odds with the Gothic vault above them — a visual record of the moment when the Polish and Lithuanian crowns merged and their artistic traditions collided.

Lublin Castle sits on the hill at the top of the old town, its tower visible from across the city. The current neo-Gothic structure dates to the early 19th century — the Austrians rebuilt it as a prison, and Nazis later used it for the same purpose — but the hill has had a fortification since the 10th century. Today it houses the National Museum, with collections of Polish painting, decorative art, and silverwork.

The must-see is the Chapel of the Holy Trinity in the castle courtyard, a small Gothic chapel covered floor-to-ceiling in Byzantine-style frescos commissioned by the Polish king in 1418. The art is extraordinarily well-preserved and the combination of Gothic architecture with Ruthenian iconography is unique in Poland. The chapel has limited visiting hours; check in advance.
