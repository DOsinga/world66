  The earthquake of 1 November 1755 struck while most of Lisbon was in church
  for All Saints' Day. The Carmo Convent's roof collapsed, as did thousands of
  other buildings across the city. Unlike most ruins, Carmo was not demolished
  or rebuilt — its roofless Gothic nave was left open to the sky and has remained
  so for 270 years. Walking into the ruined church is one of the most powerful
  architectural experiences in Lisbon.

The Carmo Convent was the largest Gothic church in Lisbon when the 1755 earthquake flattened its roof and left the nave open to the sky. Rather than rebuild, the city preserved the ruin, and today the roofless Gothic arches frame a view of the sky that is genuinely moving — nature reclaiming a great stone building, slowly, for centuries.

The nave now serves as an archaeological museum, with display cases containing an eclectic collection: Egyptian mummies, Peruvian mummies, pre-Columbian artefacts, medieval stonework, and Roman finds from across Portugal. The collection is charmingly old-fashioned in its presentation, which adds to the atmosphere. The Carmo is reached via the Santa Justa Lift or by walking up from Chiado. Open Monday to Saturday; entry fee required.
