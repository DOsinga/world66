The Matchbox Museum (Museu dos Fósforos) is exactly what its name promises: a museum dedicated to matchbox labels, housed in a former Franciscan convent at the edge of the old town. The collection runs to well over 60,000 labels assembled by a Franciscan friar, and it is probably the largest collection of its kind in Europe. If that sounds like an unlikely destination, the labels themselves are genuinely fascinating — miniature graphics from virtually every country in the world across more than a century, ranging from Soviet propaganda and Japanese woodblock-inspired designs to advertisements for long-dead Portuguese sardine factories.

The displays are organised roughly by theme and geography, with cases full of labels depicting animals, ships, famous monuments, political figures, sporting events, and everyday life. The labels are uniformly small — matchbox-sized — but they reward close attention. Some are beautiful, some are merely odd, and some are little windows into industries and aesthetics that have entirely disappeared. Portuguese matchbox design, in particular, was a serious graphic art for much of the twentieth century.

The museum shares the convent building with a few other small collections. Not for everyone, but those who find it often spend longer than they expected.
