Norilsk is not an easy place to visit, and that is part of the point. This closed industrial city above the Arctic Circle was built around nickel and copper, much of it by Gulag prisoners, and it still sits in a landscape of permafrost, smokestacks, polar weather, and vast distances.

Foreign travellers normally need special permission and usually come only through arranged tours. If you can get in, Norilsk is one of Russia's strangest urban experiences: Soviet avenues under Arctic light, the [Norilsk Museum and First House](/europe/russia/krasnoyarsk_region/norilsk/norilsk_museum_and_first_house), the prisoner memorials of [Norilsk Golgotha](/europe/russia/krasnoyarsk_region/norilsk/norilsk_golgotha), and cultural outliers like the [Norilsk Polar Drama Theatre](/europe/russia/krasnoyarsk_region/norilsk/norilsk_polar_drama_theatre).

The city is also the practical launch point for serious expeditions to the Putorana Plateau, but Norilsk should not be treated as just a gateway. Its appeal is hard, dark, and specific: an Arctic mining city where modern Russia, Soviet memory, and extreme geography are impossible to separate.
