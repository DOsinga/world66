Ryazan is a city of about half a million people in central European Russia, capital of Ryazan Oblast, sitting on the Oka River about 180 kilometres southeast of Moscow. It is an old place — originally founded in the eleventh century some 50 kilometres downstream — but the original city was completely destroyed by the Mongol khan Batu in 1237, one of the first and most thorough obliterations of the invasion. The city was rebuilt at a new site, eventually taking the Ryazan name in 1778. That history gives the city an unusual distinction: it was essentially started over from nothing after the Mongol destruction.

The historic core centres on the [Kremlin](/europe/russia/ryazan/ryazan_kremlin), perched above the confluence of the Oka and Trubezh rivers. Its seventeenth-century Cathedral of the Assumption is one of the finest examples of Moscow baroque outside the capital. The [Pozhalostin Art Museum](/europe/russia/ryazan/pozhalostin_museum) holds a serious collection of Russian painting and is the best gallery for hundreds of kilometres in any direction.

Two famous Russians were born here. The physiologist Ivan Pavlov — who won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his work on conditioned reflexes — was born in Ryazan, and his family home is now a [memorial museum](/europe/russia/ryazan/pavlov_museum). The poet Sergei Yesenin was born in the village of Konstantinovo, 40 kilometres away, and the [Yesenin Museum-Reserve](/europe/russia/ryazan/yesenin_museum) there is one of Russia's most-visited literary pilgrimages. The meadows and birch forests around Konstantinovo look much as they did when Yesenin was writing about them.

Ryazan is primarily a destination for those who enjoy digging into Russian history and culture off the main tourist circuit. It does not have the grandeur of Vladimir or Suzdal to the north, but it rewards visitors who make the effort.
