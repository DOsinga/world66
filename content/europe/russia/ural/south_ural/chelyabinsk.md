Chelyabinsk is the administrative capital of Chelyabinsk Oblast and one of Russia's ten largest cities, sitting just east of the Ural Mountains on the border between Europe and Asia. Founded as a Cossack fortress in 1736, it grew into a major industrial powerhouse during the Soviet era — famously part of the wartime "Tankograd" (Tank City) complex — a reputation that has left it with an image of grey factory smokestacks, though the reality is considerably more nuanced.

Walk the broad avenues of the city centre and you'll find an unexpected richness: fountains and sculpture compositions, a lively pedestrian street along Kirovka, and an arts scene that punches well above its industrial weight. Five state and four municipal theatres, a philharmonic orchestra, a circus, an art gallery, and several museums fill the calendar through the long Ural winters. The concert hall organ — 2,504 pipes made by the German firm Hermann Eule of Bautzen — is a point of civic pride, and annual organ music festivals draw audiences from across the region.

Chelyabinsk earned a very different kind of global fame on 15 February 2013, when a superbolide meteor streaked across the morning sky and exploded some 23 km above the city with the force of thirty Hiroshima bombs. The shockwave shattered windows across thousands of buildings, injured roughly 1,500 people (mostly from flying glass), and rained fragments across the South Ural. The largest recovered piece — a 654 kg chunk fished from the bottom of Lake Chebarkul — now dominates the Chelyabinsk Regional Museum. The event remains the largest natural space object to strike Earth since the 1908 Tunguska event, and the city wears the distinction with a mixture of rueful pride and genuine scientific curiosity.

Beyond the meteor, Chelyabinsk rewards visitors with a strong hockey culture (the Traktor HC is followed with religious intensity), the vast Shershni reservoir on the city's edge, and easy access to the Ural mountain scenery that begins within an hour's drive.
