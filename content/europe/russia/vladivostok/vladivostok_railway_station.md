  The station was built in 1912 in a style that consciously echoed Moscow's Yaroslavsky Terminal — the starting point of the Trans-Siberian Railway 9,300 kilometres to the west. The symmetry was intentional: the two terminals frame the world's longest railway line, and standing on Vladivostok's platform, you are as far from Moscow as you can get while remaining on Russian soil.

Vladivostok's railway terminal sits right at the waterfront, and arriving here by train is one of the classic experiences in Russian travel. The building is an elegant late 19th-century structure in cream and terracotta with Renaissance-revival details — ornate enough to announce a city of importance, and just old enough to feel like the genuine article.

The platform at the rear faces the bay. A monument marks kilometre 9,288 of the Trans-Siberian Railway, with kilometre zero at Moscow's Yaroslavsky station. Many travellers make the journey specifically to stand at this marker. There is something genuinely moving about it — the sense of distance, and of arrival. The interior of the station was restored and contains murals depicting Siberian landscapes and historical scenes.
