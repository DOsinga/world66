Kosovo is a partially recognised independent state in the western Balkans that declared independence from Serbia in 2008 — one of the youngest countries in the world and still a disputed territory. Around 100 countries recognise it; Serbia and Russia do not. For travellers, the political status matters less than the practical reality: Kosovo has its own government, currency (the euro), border controls, and tourism infrastructure. It is safe, inexpensive, and almost entirely off the standard tourist circuit.

[Pristina](/europe/serbia/kosovoprovince/pristina) is the capital and the centre of the country's youthful energy — Ottoman mosques alongside brutalist Yugoslav apartment blocks alongside glass EU-funded buildings, all inhabited by a population that is among the youngest in Europe. The cafe culture is serious, English is widely spoken, and the nightlife punches well above its weight. [Prizren](/europe/serbia/kosovoprovince/prizren) is the more picturesque old city, a compact Ottoman town in the mountains near the Albanian border. [Peja](/europe/serbia/kosovoprovince/peja) (Peć in Serbian) is the gateway to the Rugova Canyon and Accursed Mountains, some of the finest hiking in the Balkans. [Ferizaj](/europe/serbia/kosovoprovince/ferizaj) and [Skenderaj](/europe/serbia/kosovoprovince/skenderaj) are smaller towns with significance to the country's recent history.

Kosovo's history is densely layered. The 1389 Battle of Kosovo — fought on the Kosovo Polje plain outside Pristina — is one of the defining moments of Serbian national mythology. The medieval Serbian Orthodox monasteries of Gračanica and the Patriarchate of Peć are among the finest Byzantine monuments in the Balkans and are UNESCO-listed. The country is overwhelmingly Albanian Muslim in population, and the contrast between the older Orthodox heritage and the majority culture is one of Kosovo's defining qualities.
