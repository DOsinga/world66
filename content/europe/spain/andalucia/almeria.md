Almería is the driest city in Europe, sitting in a landscape that looks more like North Africa than Spain. The province around it contains the only true desert on the European continent — the Tabernas, where Sergio Leone filmed his Spaghetti Westerns and where the bare, eroded hills give the impression of being somewhere between Morocco and Arizona. The city itself has the feel of a place that knows it is not on the main tourist circuit, and that suits it: prices are lower than elsewhere in Andalusia, the old town is lived-in and genuine, and the port has a working character rarely found on this stretch of coast.

The dominant sight is the [Alcazaba](/europe/spain/andalucia/almeria/alcazaba_almeria), the massive Moorish fortress that rises on the hill above the city. Built in the tenth century for the Caliph Abd al-Rahman III, it is one of the largest Muslim fortifications remaining in Spain, larger than the Alhambra complex and considerably less crowded. The views over the city and the bay from its upper terraces are genuinely dramatic. Beside it, the [Cathedral](/europe/spain/andalucia/almeria/almeria_cathedral) tells a different story: built in the sixteenth century during a period of Berber pirate raids, it was designed as a fortress as well as a place of worship, its buttressed walls giving it a military seriousness unusual in Spanish ecclesiastical architecture.

The city's Civil War history deserves more attention than it usually gets. During the war, Almería's air raid shelters were dug under the city to protect civilians from bombing — they were largely unknown until their rediscovery in the 1990s, and today the [Refugios de la Guerra Civil](/europe/spain/andalucia/almeria/air_raid_shelters) offer a startling and sobering underground tour. The port, busy with ferries to Morocco and Melilla, and the long Paseo Marítimo make for good evening walking. The beaches at Almería city are decent but those along the surrounding coast — Cabo de Gata to the east — are spectacular.
