El Rocío is one of the strangest and most compelling villages in Spain. For most of the year it is almost a ghost town — wide sandy streets, rows of whitewashed hermandad houses locked up and waiting, and only a few hundred permanent residents. Then, at Pentecost, a million pilgrims arrive in covered wagons and on horseback for the [Romería de El Rocío](/europe/spain/andalucia/el_rocio/romeria_de_el_rocio), one of the largest religious pilgrimages in the world. The village becomes a city overnight, then empties again within days.

The focus of all this devotion is the [Hermitage of El Rocío](/europe/spain/andalucia/el_rocio/hermitage_of_el_rocio), a whitewashed baroque church at the edge of the marshes housing the Virgen del Rocío — Our Lady of the Dew. The image, a small medieval figure, has been venerated here since the thirteenth century.

Even outside the romería, El Rocío is worth visiting for the setting alone. The village sits directly beside a permanent lagoon at the edge of the [Doñana National Park](/europe/spain/andalucia/doana_national_p), and flamingos, spoonbills and herons feed in the water just beyond the wooden boardwalk at the end of the village. Semi-wild marsh horses wander the sandy lanes as if the village were their paddock — which, in many ways, it is. The roads are deliberately kept unpaved, and the whole place has a quality that is hard to name: sacred, wild, and slightly suspended in time.
