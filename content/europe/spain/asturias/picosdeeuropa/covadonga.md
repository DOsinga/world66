  Covadonga is where, by tradition, the Reconquista began. In 722, a Visigothic nobleman named Pelayo is said to have defeated a Muslim army at this spot — the victory that started eight centuries of Christian reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula. Historians argue about the scale and significance of the actual battle, but the mythology is firmly established. Spain's first national park was created here in 1918, and the site remains a major Catholic pilgrimage destination. The combination of mountain scenery, medieval legend, and 19th-century neo-Romanesque basilica makes for an unexpectedly powerful visit.

Covadonga is both a pilgrimage site and one of the most visited spots in the Picos de Europa. The complex centres on the Basílica de Santa María la Real, a dramatic neo-Romanesque church built in the late 19th century in pale stone against a forested hillside. Beside it, the Santa Cueva (Holy Cave) is built into the cliff face, sheltering a venerated statue of the Virgin and the tombs of the Asturian kings, including Pelayo himself.

The village of Covadonga is tiny — fewer than 100 permanent residents — but receives enormous numbers of visitors, particularly on pilgrimage days in September. The setting is genuinely beautiful: a narrow valley enclosed by forested limestone ridges, with the church towers and the cave opening catching the morning light. From Covadonga, the road continues 8 km uphill to the Lagos de Covadonga, two glacial lakes at 1,100 metres that are the starting point for several high-altitude walks. In summer, access to the lakes by private car is restricted and shuttles run from a car park below.
