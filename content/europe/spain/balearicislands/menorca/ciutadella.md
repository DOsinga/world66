Ciutadella is the old capital of Menorca, and it shows. The British, who controlled the island for much of the 18th century, moved the capital to Maó on the east coast with its magnificent harbour. Ciutadella has never quite forgiven them. The city retains the grandeur of its former status: Baroque palaces, Gothic churches, and a medieval street plan that crowds into the western end of the island with real dignity. The harbour is small but beautiful, a narrow channel lined with restaurants and fishing boats, overlooked by the old city walls.

The [Plaça des Born](ciutadella/placa_des_born) is the centrepiece — a formal square with an obelisk commemorating the city's defence against a Turkish raid in 1558, surrounded by the old town hall, the former governor's palace, and the facade of the Teatre Victòria. From here, the main pedestrian street, Carrer Quadrado, runs down to the [Cathedral](ciutadella/ciutadella_cathedral) at its lower end. The cathedral is Gothic in its bones, begun after the Christian conquest of 1287, though a great deal of what you see today is Baroque overlay.

Beyond the city itself, Ciutadella is a good base for the prehistoric monuments that make Menorca unusual among Mediterranean islands. The [Naveta des Tudons](ciutadella/naveta_des_tudons), a few kilometres east on the road toward Maó, is one of the best-preserved Bronze Age funerary monuments in the western Mediterranean — a boat-shaped stone chamber used for collective burial that dates to around 1000 BC. Several other Talayotic sites are scattered across the western end of the island.

The [Pedreres de s'Hostal](ciutadella/pedreres_de_shostal), an old sandstone quarry just outside town, has been transformed into a cultural space with sculptures set among the vertical quarry walls and gardens that descend into the rock. It is one of those genuinely unexpected places that justify taking the slower road across Menorca.
