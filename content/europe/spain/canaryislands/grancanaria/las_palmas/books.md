**Fortunata and Jacinta** by Benito Pérez Galdós. Galdós was born in Las Palmas in 1843 and became the greatest Spanish novelist of the 19th century — his country's answer to Balzac or Dickens. This vast double novel, set in Madrid, follows two women in love with the same man across decades of middle-class Spanish life. Galdós saw society with a clarity and sympathy that his contemporaries found uncomfortable. Starting with this one is the right call; you can visit his birthplace, the Casa-Museo Pérez Galdós in Vegueta, before or after reading it.

**The Canary Islands** by Noel Rochford. A thorough introduction to the archipelago's history from the first European contacts in the 14th century through conquest, colonisation, and the role the islands played as a waystation between Europe and the Americas. Covers Gran Canaria in detail, including the fate of the Guanche people whose remains you will see in the Museo Canario.

**The Guanches: Survivors and their Descendants** by Rafael Muñoz Jiménez and José Luis Concepción. The pre-Hispanic inhabitants of the Canaries — the Guanches on Tenerife, but also the Canarii of Gran Canaria — remain poorly understood outside academic circles. This introduction to their archaeology, burial customs, and genetic legacy gives the Museo Canario's collection real context. Not a light read, but the right preparation for the island's archaeology.

**South from Granada** by Gerald Brenan. Brenan's classic is set in Andalusia rather than the Canaries, but his eye for Spanish provincial life — the pace, the politics, the pleasures — translates directly to the feel of Las Palmas. Read it for the same reason you'd read Laurie Lee: for a tone of voice about Spain that still rings true.
