Set Portes — Seven Doors — opened in 1836 on Passeig d'Isabel II, at the edge of what was then the working waterfront. The seven arched entrances facing the arcade are still exactly as they were; the name is architectural fact, not metaphor. The building itself predates the restaurant, and the kitchen has operated in near-continuous service through the Carlist wars, the Civil War, and the transition to democracy. Hemingway had a regular table. Miró, Dalí, and Orson Welles all ate here.

The kitchen is built around rice. The paella and arrossejat (noodles cooked in fish broth) are the dishes the restaurant is known for, and they have changed little in substance over 180 years. The waiters wear white jackets; the tables use the same linens visible in photographs from the 1930s. This is not nostalgia as a marketing tool — it is what the place simply looks like because nothing has been substantially renovated.

Reservations are expected, and the restaurant fills with a mix of visitors and Barcelona residents who treat it as a reliable occasion restaurant. Prices reflect the setting. What you are paying for, beyond the food, is a room that looked essentially the same when your great-grandparents were alive.
