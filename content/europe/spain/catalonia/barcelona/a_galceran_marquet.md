In the fourteenth century the Crown of Aragon was the dominant sea power of the western Mediterranean, and Galceran Marquet was the man most responsible for that supremacy. As Admiral of the Fleet under King James II, he reorganised the shipbuilding infrastructure, standardised the design of war galleys, and created a naval command structure that allowed Aragon to project power from Sicily to Cyprus. His reforms at the Drassanes — the great royal shipyards just inland from this fountain — turned Barcelona into the premier maritime capital of its age.

What makes Marquet historically fascinating is that his greatness was logistical rather than spectacular. He won battles not through personal heroism but through superior preparation: better-provisioned ships, better-trained oarsmen, better charts. A medieval technocrat, in effect, at a time when technology was inseparable from artisanal knowledge passed hand to hand in the workshops of the Ribera district that surrounds this spot.

The choice to honour him with a fountain rather than a statue is quietly fitting. Water was the medium of his genius — he understood its currents, its moods, its commercial possibilities — and the neighbourhood he helped make prosperous still carries his memory in stone and water.
