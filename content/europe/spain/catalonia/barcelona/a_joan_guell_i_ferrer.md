Joan Güell i Ferrer (1800–1872) belongs to a familiar historical category: the self-made father whose success enables the son's greatness. Born into modest circumstances in Torredembarra, he made his fortune in Cuba before returning to Catalonia and channelling his capital into textile manufacturing at a moment when industrialisation was reshaping the region. His mill at Sants — now a cultural centre — was one of the most advanced in Iberia.

What sets Güell apart from other nineteenth-century industrialists is the quality of his economic thinking. He wrote extensively on free trade and protectionism, arguing in detailed, sophisticated terms for policies that would shelter nascent Catalan industry while gradually opening markets. His essay on economic nationalism made him a prominent public intellectual, not just a factory owner. The Ateneu Barcelonès, the city's great cultural club, owes much of its intellectual character to men like him.

His son Eusebi inherited both the money and the cultural ambition — and used them to commission Gaudí's most extravagant projects, from Parc Güell to the Palau Güell. Every time you admire those works, you are admiring, indirectly, the industrial discipline of this man whose monument stands quietly in Eixample.
