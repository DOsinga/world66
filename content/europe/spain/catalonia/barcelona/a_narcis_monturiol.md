In 1859, on the waters of Barcelona's port, a fish-shaped vessel called the Ictíneo I slipped beneath the surface with Narcís Monturiol at the helm — the first crewed submarine to make a controlled dive anywhere in the world. Monturiol was not a naval engineer by training but a utopian socialist who had originally conceived the submarine as a safer way to harvest coral, sparing the lives of divers who regularly died doing it by hand. The humanitarian impulse led, unexpectedly, to a military technology.

What is even more remarkable is what came next: the Ictíneo II, launched in 1867, was powered by an anaerobic combustion engine — a steam engine that consumed its own oxygen from a chemical reaction, making it the world's first air-independent submarine propulsion system. This preceded the internal combustion engine by nearly two decades. Monturiol described the principle clearly in his published writings, but the Spanish state showed no interest, and he died bankrupt in 1885 while Robert Whitehead's torpedo boats captured the naval imagination elsewhere.

The French submarine pioneer Gustave Zédé later acknowledged that Monturiol had solved problems the French navy was still wrestling with thirty years after the Ictíneo. The monument stands as a reminder that the history of invention is not always a story of recognition.
