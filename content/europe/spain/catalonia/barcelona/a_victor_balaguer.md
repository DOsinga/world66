For roughly three centuries after the Catalan defeat at the siege of Barcelona in 1714, the Castilian Crown systematically suppressed Catalan as an official and literary language. By 1800 it survived mainly in speech; written Catalan had no canon, no publishing infrastructure, no prestige. Víctor Balaguer (1824–1901) was central to changing that — not only as a poet who wrote in Catalan when doing so was a political act, but as the founding intellectual of the Renaixença, the cultural movement that reclaimed medieval Catalan literature and made it the basis for a modern literary revival.

Balaguer's methods were as political as they were cultural. He helped found the Jocs Florals in 1859 — the literary competition that became the showcase for revived Catalan writing — and used his position as a Spanish government minister under the liberal Sexenio Democràtic to argue for federal arrangements that would restore Catalan autonomy. He was exiled twice, wrote in prison, and spent years in France and Portugal continuing his advocacy. His street in Barcelona's Born neighbourhood carries his name partly because he was the man who named many of its streets during his time as a city councillor.

That circularity is very Balaguer: the man who shaped the landscape also recorded it, and the landscape now commemorates him.
