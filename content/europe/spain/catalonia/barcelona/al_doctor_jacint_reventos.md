At the turn of the twentieth century, tuberculosis was killing more Barcelonans than any other single disease, and the city's overcrowded working-class neighbourhoods — the Raval, the Barceloneta, the old town — were its most fertile ground. Jacint Reventós (1882–1952) responded by establishing dispensaries that brought diagnosis, isolation advice, and basic treatment to patients who could not afford private physicians. His approach, which combined medical treatment with public health education and social support, was decades ahead of mainstream Spanish practice.

Reventós worked within the framework of Catalan nationalist medicine — a movement of doctors who saw public health as inseparable from Catalan social identity. His institutional affiliations ran through the Hospital Clínic and the Generalitat's public health infrastructure, which meant that the Civil War and Franco's victory were not merely political disasters for him but professional ones: much of what he had built was dismantled by the regime that viewed Catalan institutions with deep suspicion.

He kept working regardless, in a diminished capacity, through the long grey years of the dictatorship. The monument near the Barceloneta, close to the population he served most, marks a doctor who measured success not in prestigious patients but in the survival rates of the poor.
