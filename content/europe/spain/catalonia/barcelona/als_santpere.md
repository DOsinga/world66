The Santpere name is to Barcelona comedy roughly what the Barrymore name is to American drama — a theatrical dynasty so embedded in popular culture that individual achievements blur into a collective legend. The family's association with the Teatre Grec, the Paral·lel variety theatres, and the working-class audiences of the Barceloneta spans from the mid-nineteenth century to the late twentieth, encompassing everything from nineteenth-century sainetes (short comic sketches) to television comedy in the Franco era.

What made the Santperes culturally significant was their insistence on performing in Catalan even when doing so carried political risk. During the dictatorship, performance in Catalan was restricted and surveilled; the Paral·lel theatre district, where much of the family's work took place, became a subtle site of cultural resistance, with audiences understanding the political valence of simply hearing their language from a stage.

The monument near the Barceloneta market, close to the neighbourhood that provided the family's most loyal audiences, is a reminder that popular culture — the kind made for working people who pay modest prices for an evening's entertainment — is also part of a city's identity, and that preserving a language in comedy is as important as preserving it in poetry.
