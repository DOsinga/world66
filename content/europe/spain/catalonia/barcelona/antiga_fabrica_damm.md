Joseph Damm was Alsatian, not Catalan. He arrived in Barcelona in 1876 with a recipe, a small amount of capital, and a calculation that a city of 250,000 people growing rapidly through industrial immigration needed a locally brewed lager. He was correct. The brewery he established on what is now Carrer del Rosselló — then the outer edge of the new Eixample grid, where land was still cheap enough for industrial use — became the foundation of the Damm company, which a century and a half later still holds around half the Catalan beer market.

The red-brick building reads immediately as industrial Eixample: the same vocabulary of cast-iron columns, brick vaulting, and large factory windows that the expanding city used for everything from printing works to textile mills in the 1870s and 1880s. Damm moved production out of the city decades ago, and the original factory has been reconfigured as an events and cultural space — concerts, brand activations, a rooftop terrace — while keeping the architecture legible. The brewing equipment is gone, but the bones of the building are intact.

The Damm brand's integration into Catalan cultural life runs deep enough that it sponsors FC Barcelona, the Primavera Sound music festival, and a roster of Catalan film productions. The original factory is the physical root of that presence: a building where an Alsatian immigrant's industrial gamble became, over generations, a piece of local identity.
