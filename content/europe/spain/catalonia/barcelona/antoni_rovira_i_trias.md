In 1859 the Spanish government held a competition to design Barcelona's expansion beyond the medieval walls, and Antoni Rovira i Trias won it decisively. The Barcelona city council unanimously endorsed his plan: a radial design centred on the old city, with broad avenues fanning outward like spokes from a hub. Then Madrid overruled the result and imposed Ildefons Cerdà's grid instead — a plan the Barcelona council had rejected as too alien to the existing urban fabric.

What makes the monument in the Gràcia neighbourhood quietly poignant is the bronze reproduction of Rovira's plan set into the pavement nearby. You can crouch down and study the city that might have been: more organic, more hierarchical, less relentlessly egalitarian than Cerdà's endlessly repeated octagonal blocks. Urbanists still debate which plan would have aged better. Cerdà's grid has proven extraordinarily adaptable; Rovira's radial plan might have created the kind of concentrated commercial corridors that Barcelona still lacks.

Rovira went on to design many buildings in Gràcia — including the neighbourhood's market — and the district came to feel like his, shaped by the sensibility of the man the central government had cheated. His statue looks across the plaça he helped create with an expression that resists easy interpretation.
