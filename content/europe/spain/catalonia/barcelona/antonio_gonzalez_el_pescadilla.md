Rumba catalana is one of Barcelona's most distinctive musical inventions: a fusion of Andalusian flamenco, Cuban son, and Catalan street culture that emerged in the Gypsy community of the Raval and Gràcia in the 1950s and 60s. El Pescadilla — the name means 'the little fish,' a nickname from his youth in the Barceloneta market — was one of the genre's key architects alongside his more famous contemporary Peret. His rasping voice and percussive guitar style became immediately recognisable on Barcelona's radio stations.

What makes his story interesting beyond music is the social context: El Pescadilla was part of the wave of Andalusian Gypsy families who migrated to Barcelona in the postwar decades, settling in the city's most overcrowded working-class districts and creating, in conditions of poverty and discrimination, a musical synthesis that the city later claimed with enormous pride. The rumba catalana was simultaneously invisible to bourgeois Barcelona and commercially dominant in the bars and street parties of the popular quarters.

His daughter Rosario became a major pop star in her own right in the 1990s, carrying the family musical sensibility into a very different commercial landscape. The pavement memorial near the Barceloneta — fish market territory — keeps his original, street-level world in view.
