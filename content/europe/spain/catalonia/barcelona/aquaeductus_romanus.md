Roman Barcino needed water from the hills to the north, and the engineers who built the colony's infrastructure solved the problem with a two-channel aqueduct system drawing from the rivers Besós and Llobregat. The sections still visible in the old city — fragments embedded in later medieval construction — are among the most quietly impressive Roman survivals in Catalonia, not because they are monumental but because they show how completely the Romans integrated hydraulic infrastructure into urban planning.

The aqueduct supplied not just drinking water but the public baths and the productive facilities — fullers, dyers, tanneries — that required steady water supply. Roman colonial cities were, among other things, water management systems: the ability to guarantee clean water at reliable pressure was what distinguished a proper Roman city from a native settlement, and investing in that infrastructure was how Rome expressed its presence.

What you are looking at, in the fragments preserved in the medieval walls, is a palimpsest of two ambitious urban projects separated by a thousand years: the Roman engineers who built the original system and the medieval builders who, finding solid Roman masonry, incorporated it wholesale rather than demolishing it. That pragmatism is why it survives.
