Poblenou's identity was forged in water before it was forged in steam. The neighbourhood that became Barcelona's industrial heartland in the nineteenth century — the 'Catalan Manchester' as it was called — depended on access to groundwater and the channel infrastructure that preceded it for the bleaching, dyeing, and finishing processes central to the textile industry. The sculpture Aquari plays on this layered history: the astrological sign of the water-bearer, the industrial water supply, and the ongoing urban metamorphosis of a district now rebranding itself as Barcelona's technology quarter.

The title's double meaning (Aquarius as zodiac sign and aquarius as water-carrier in Latin) is typical of the conceptual games that Barcelona's urban art programme has favoured since the 1980s, when the city began systematically commissioning public artworks as part of its post-Franco and post-Olympic urban renewal. Rather than straightforward commemoration, many of these works ask the viewer to make connections between different registers of meaning.

Poblenou has been reinvented so many times — fishing village, industrial suburb, Olympic village, innovation district — that a work invoking water feels apt: water takes the shape of its container, and the neighbourhood has always adapted to whatever container the city imposed on it.
