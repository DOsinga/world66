The Roman city of Barcino required a water supply more substantial than the local wells could provide. An aqueduct was built, probably in the 1st century CE, drawing water from sources in the hills to the north and bringing it into the city through a covered channel that ran along the line of what is now the Passeig de Sant Joan. The aqueduct entered the city through the northeast gate and fed the public fountains, baths, and some private houses.

Sections of the aqueduct survive, incorporated into the fabric of medieval and later buildings. Two short stretches are visible from public streets: one on the Carrer del Bisbe, where three arches of the original Roman arcade can be seen incorporated into a building facade, and one visible from the Plaça de la Vila de Madrid, where a section of the channel is preserved as part of an archaeological display. There are also sections preserved in private buildings and in the underground museum of the Museu d'Història de Barcelona.

The aqueduct is not dramatic in its surviving form — what you see is a few stone arches embedded in medieval stonework, without the grandeur of the aqueducts at Pont du Gard or Segovia. But its survival in the fabric of a continuously occupied city is historically significant. Buildings were constructed around and over it; it was incorporated rather than demolished because the stone was valuable and the arches were structurally useful. The Roman water infrastructure became part of the medieval building material, which is now part of the contemporary building fabric.
