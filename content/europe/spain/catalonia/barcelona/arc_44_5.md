Barcelona is oriented at roughly 44.5 degrees to the compass — almost exactly northeast-southwest along its seafront, rather than the cardinal directions most cities use as an orienting framework. This is not accidental: the city grew along the coastal plain between the Besós and Llobregat rivers, following the natural geography. But the consequence is that the light strikes Barcelona's facades at an angle that produces the distinctive golden-hour quality — the warm lateral illumination — that photographers and painters have celebrated for centuries.

The sculpture Arc 44.5° makes this invisible geometric fact visible, turning a piece of land-survey mathematics into an object of contemplation. It belongs to a strand of public art that treats urban infrastructure — the grid, the angle, the measuring system — as worthy of aesthetic attention, inviting walkers to notice the frameworks that structure experience without usually becoming conscious objects of attention.

The Ciutadella park, where this sculpture stands, has its own geometry: laid out after the demolition of the hated Bourbon citadel in the 1860s, its paths and plantings follow a formal design that Cerdà's Eixample grid does not. The arc of the sculpture echoes, in miniature, the arc of the park's own formal ambitions.
