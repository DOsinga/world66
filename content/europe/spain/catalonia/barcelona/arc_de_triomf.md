Every capital in nineteenth-century Europe had its triumphal arch, and most of them commemorated battles: Napoleon's Arc de Triomphe, Berlin's Brandenburger Tor, London's Marble Arch. Barcelona's version, completed in 1888, celebrates something entirely different — commerce, industry, and the ambition of a city that had been excluded from Spain's imperial economy for two centuries and was now demanding recognition on its own terms. The Universal Exhibition it announced drew two million visitors and put Barcelona on the international map as a modern industrial city.

The arch's architect, Josep Vilaseca i Casanovas, chose a Moorish-influenced Neo-Mudéjar style — brick rather than the marble or ashlar of most triumphal arches — which was simultaneously a statement of Iberian identity and a practical response to Catalonia's brick-building tradition. The decorative friezes by Josep Reynés show the nations of the world bringing tribute to Barcelona, a piece of civic propaganda that has aged more gracefully than most.

The Passeig de Lluís Companys that leads from the arch to the Ciutadella park was the ceremonial axis of the 1888 exhibition, and walking it still carries a faint trace of that ambition — the vision of a city that had decided, after long humiliation, to announce itself to the world.
