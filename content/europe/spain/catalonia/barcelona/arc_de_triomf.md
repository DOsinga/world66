The Arc de Triomf at the northern end of Passeig de Lluís Companys is more unusual than it looks. Triumphal arches were a Roman tradition adopted by Napoleon for military victories, and copied by European cities throughout the 19th century to celebrate their own self-importance. Barcelona's version, built in 1888, was designed by Josep Vilaseca i Casanovas for the Universal Exhibition held that year — not to commemorate a battle but to serve as the exhibition's main entrance, opening onto the fairground in the Parc de la Ciutadella.

The arch is built entirely in Mudejar-revival style, from the reddish brick that characterises much of Barcelona's 19th-century public architecture. Mudejar — the style developed by Moorish craftsmen working in Christian Spain — was chosen to evoke both Spanish heritage and the orientalism fashionable in 1880s European design. The arch's faces are decorated with terracotta reliefs: on the main face, the inscription Arxiu de la Corona d'Aragó appears, and the bas-reliefs show Barcelona welcoming the nations of the world. On the reverse, a scene of prize-giving at the exhibition.

The promenade that leads from the arch to the Parc de la Ciutadella — Passeig de Lluís Companys, named for the Catalan president executed by Franco in 1940 — is wide enough for an army to march through, which was presumably part of the original programme. Now it is lined with iron lamp posts, courts for petanque, and the lawyers' college in a curious cluster of modernista buildings. On Sundays, families walk the length of it slowly. The arch stands at the far end of the boulevard, very red in the afternoon light.
