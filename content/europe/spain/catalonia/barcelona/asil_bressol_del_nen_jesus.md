The late nineteenth century in Barcelona saw the emergence of an extensive network of charitable institutions run by Catholic orders and lay associations, aimed at addressing the social dislocations of rapid industrialisation. Women entering the textile mills and domestic service had nowhere to leave young children; the Asil Bressol del Nen Jesús (Creche of the Child Jesus) was founded to fill that gap, providing supervised care for infants whose mothers had no alternative. The initiative came from lay Catholic associations rather than the state, reflecting the Catalan bourgeoisie's view that social welfare was the Church's domain.

The institution's survival through the Civil War, the Franco period, and the post-dictatorship secularisation of social services is remarkable. Many similar institutions were dissolved, secularised, or nationalised; this one adapted. Its location in the Sant Pere neighbourhood, close to the working-class population it originally served, is itself a form of historical continuity.

The building reflects the architectural sensibility of Catalan religious welfare institutions — modest in ornament, serious in construction, designed to communicate stability and permanence to a community that had little of either. Walking past it, the visitor encounters an institution that has outlasted several different visions of what social welfare should look like.
