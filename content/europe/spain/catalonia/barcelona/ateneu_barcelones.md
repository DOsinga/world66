Pompeu Fabra, who standardised modern written Catalan in the early 20th century, worked within a world shaped partly by the Ateneu Barcelonès. The club, founded in 1860 as a literary and scientific society, was the room where the Renaixença — the Catalan cultural renaissance — was debated, where its canon was assembled, and where the political arguments about Catalan nationhood were conducted in Catalan at a time when that was not the default in any other institution. Its private library became one of the most significant in Catalonia.

Under Franco, the Ateneu's status as a private club gave it a degree of protection that public institutions did not have. Catalan could be spoken here; Catalan books could be held in its library; the cultural infrastructure that the regime was suppressing in public continued in a semi-private form. It was not resistance — it was accommodation — but it preserved things that might otherwise have been lost.

The building is a 17th-century palace with a courtyard garden. Non-members can visit the library and attend public events. The reading rooms, with their accumulated portraits and heavy wooden furniture, are one of the few places in Barcelona where the physical environment has not been renovated to match a more recent idea of what a cultural institution should look like.
