Barcelona's relationship with the car has been one of the great urban policy dramas of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. The city built motorways through its waterfront, allowed the Eixample's interior courtyards to become parking lots, and designed the Ronda ring roads in the 1980s to funnel Olympic traffic. Then, almost immediately, it began reversing course: removing seafront highways, creating pedestrian superblocks, banning cars from entire districts. The sculpture Autovia sits in this contradictory history.

The work's title invites the viewer to ask what a motorway means in an urban context — whether the logic of high-speed throughput is compatible with the texture of city life that Barcelona has always valued. The answer the city's urban planners have given since the 1990s is increasingly negative: the removal of the Ronda del Litoral's grip on the seafront, and the ongoing superblock programme, represent a systematic disavowal of mid-century car-centric planning.

Near the Ciutadella park, where the sculpture stands, this tension is particularly legible: the park itself was created by demolishing a military installation, and the streets around it have been repeatedly redesigned in response to changing transport philosophies. The Autovia is part of that conversation.
