Gaudí began the Sagrada Família in 1883. Domènech i Montaner designed the Hospital de Sant Pau — formally the Recinte Modernista — starting in 1902, siting it two blocks north of the Eixample's diagonal axis. When the two buildings were placed on Cerdà's grid, their alignments happened to coincide: the main facade of the Sagrada Família faces directly up the axis toward the hospital's central pavilion. Nobody planned this. Cerdà's grid was already there; the architects chose their orientations independently. The avenue that now runs between them, opening a diagonal slash through several Eixample blocks, was cut later to formalise what the geometry implied.

The effect is theatrical. Standing at the hospital end and looking south, the Sagrada Família fills the end of the avenue with a precision that suggests deliberate axial planning — the kind of perspective manipulation that Haussmann used in Paris. Standing at the Sagrada Família end, the hospital's Art Nouveau towers are framed in the distance. The two buildings, now both UNESCO World Heritage Sites, were designed by architects who were contemporaries and rivals rather than collaborators. Gaudí and Domènech i Montaner moved in overlapping worlds but were not close; the dialogue between their buildings is spatial rather than personal.

The avenue is pedestrianised and planted with trees, making it one of the more agreeable walks between two tourist sites in Barcelona — wide enough to be unhurried, short enough to do without planning.
