The name has nothing to do with anything you can see. The avenue runs almost exactly along latitude 41 degrees 22 minutes north, and when the street was formally named in 1894, someone noticed that geographical fact and used it. No other major city named a boulevard after a line of latitude. The coincidence has defined the street ever since: its proper Catalan name is *Avinguda del Paral·lel*, and the parallel in question passes through Sapporo, Vladivostok, and northern Turkey before it gets to this stretch of southwestern Barcelona.

From the 1890s onward the avenue became the city's entertainment district for everyone who could not afford the Liceu. The venues opened in rapid succession: the Edèn Concert in 1895, the Apolo, the Arnau, the Pompeia, and eventually El Molino, a music hall still operating today. The programming was variety theatre, popular music, flamenco, comic acts, and erotic revue. The audiences were factory workers, port workers, the inhabitants of the Raval and Poble Sec — not the bourgeoisie of the Eixample, who had their own stages. The Paral·lel was loud, cheap, crowded, and continuous from opening to well past midnight.

The anarchist movement was woven into the same fabric. The CNT's cultural infrastructure — meeting halls, ateneus, the offices of newspapers like *Solidaridad Obrera* — overlapped with the theatre district, not accidentally. The working class that filled the Arnau on Saturday night was the same working class that went on general strike on Monday morning. The avenue is quieter now; several of the old music halls have become nightclubs or been demolished, but the Apolo and the Molino are still running, improbably intact.
