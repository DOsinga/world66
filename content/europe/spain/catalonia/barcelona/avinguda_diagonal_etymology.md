The Avinguda Diagonal is the most distinctive element of Ildefons Cerdà's Eixample grid. The grid is otherwise relentlessly orthogonal — all streets are parallel or perpendicular to each other — but the Diagonal cuts through it at 45 degrees, from the southwest (Plaça de l'Espanya) to the northeast (Plaça de les Glòries). The avenue was planned from the beginning as this diagonal element; it appears in Cerdà's 1860 design alongside the cross-street Gran Via.

Cerdà's reasoning was practical: a diagonal avenue allowed direct connections between distant parts of the city that the grid would otherwise connect only through right-angle routes. It also broke the visual monotony of the grid and created points of interest at every intersection — the oblique crossing of the diagonal with the grid streets creates distinctive corners and irregular plots that were more interesting architecturally than the grid's standard right-angle intersections.

The Diagonal became one of the main arteries of Barcelona's 20th-century development. The university district, the trade fair complex, the financial district, and the residential zones of the upper city all developed along or near it. After the 1992 Olympics, the avenue was extended to the sea — a project that required the demolition of the industrial zone of Poblenou and produced the new Diagonal Mar neighbourhood. The avenue now runs from the mountains to the sea, connecting 140 years of Barcelona's urban growth in a single straight line.
