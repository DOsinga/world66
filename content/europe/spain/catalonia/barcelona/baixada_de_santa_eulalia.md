Santa Eulàlia is Barcelona's co-patron saint alongside the Verge de la Mercè, and her legend is extraordinarily violent. According to hagiographic tradition, she was a Christian girl of thirteen who refused to renounce her faith before the Roman governor Dacian around 303 AD, and was subjected to thirteen tortures — one for each year of her life. The torture attributed to this hill: sealed inside a barrel packed with shards of glass and knife blades, then rolled down the slope into the Forum (now the Plaça de Sant Miquel, below). Afterward, snow reportedly fell to cover her body. The thirteen pointed spikes of the cross in the cathedral cloister refer to the thirteen tortures.

Historians regard the entire story as pious invention with no contemporary documentation, and there is serious scholarly debate about whether Eulàlia is a duplicate of a Mérida martyr of the same name, whose story was transplanted to Barcelona when the city needed its own early Christian identity. None of this affects the descent's character: the Baixada de Santa Eulàlia is one of the Gothic Quarter's most atmospheric lanes, narrow, cobbled, and old enough to have been here when the legend was still believed without question.

It drops steeply from the Carrer dels Banys Nous area toward the plaça below, passing buildings that have been rebuilt multiple times over the centuries while maintaining the medieval street line. Walk it going downhill in the late afternoon, when the light at the bottom is amber and the stone is warm, and the name becomes easy to carry.
