The Roman balance — two pans suspended from a horizontal beam — was the universal instrument of commerce across the ancient and medieval Mediterranean. In the warehouses and merchant halls of El Born, which was Barcelona's commercial centre from the thirteenth to the seventeenth century, such scales were used to weigh everything that moved through the port: pepper, saffron, cinnamon, raw wool, finished cloth, and the silver coins that paid for all of it. The weights were regulated by the city's Consolat de Mar, the commercial court whose statutes were copied by trading cities across the Mediterranean.

The Balança Romana (Roman Scale) sculpture in El Born is part of a broader programme of public art that treats the neighbourhood's mercantile history as worth aesthetic attention — not in the heroic mode of statues and arches, but through conceptual references that reward the viewer who knows the history. El Born today, with its boutiques, cocktail bars, and tourist-oriented tapas restaurants, is a radically transformed version of the neighbourhood; the scales reference what it used to weigh.

The medieval Consolat de Mar was so influential that its code of maritime commercial law, the Llibre del Consolat de Mar, was translated into Italian, Greek, Castilian, and French, and remained a standard reference for maritime courts well into the eighteenth century. Barcelona's merchants weighed goods here that moved across the known world.
