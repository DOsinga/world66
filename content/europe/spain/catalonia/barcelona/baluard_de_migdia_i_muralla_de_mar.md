The fortifications whose remains are exposed here were not built to defend Barcelona from external enemies. They were built to defend the Bourbon monarchy from its own subjects. After the fall of Barcelona in September 1714 — the event now commemorated as Catalonia's national day — Philip V ordered the construction of a new citadel and sea wall system whose primary purpose was to keep the population under surveillance and make future resistance impossible. The Ribera neighbourhood was demolished to clear the building site; thousands of residents were displaced without compensation.

The Muralla de Mar (sea wall) extended along the waterfront, cutting the city off from direct access to its own port. The Baluard de Migdia was one of the defensive bastions in this system — a geometry of military engineering designed to give cannon clear lines of fire in multiple directions. The wall represented not just military power but an assertion of sovereignty: Barcelona's relationship with the sea, the source of its historical prosperity, was now mediated by the state.

The wall was demolished in the nineteenth century as part of Barcelona's urban expansion and as a gesture of political reconciliation. What remains, visible in these excavations near the Barceloneta, is a fragment of one of the most consequential acts of political architecture in Catalan history — the physical expression of a defeat that shaped everything that followed.
