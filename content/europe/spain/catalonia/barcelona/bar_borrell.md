Sant Antoni was, through most of the 20th century, a working district of artisans, small traders, and market vendors organised around the Mercat de Sant Antoni. Bar Borrell opened in the early 20th century at the corner of Carrer del Comte d'Urgell as a neighbourhood bar for that neighbourhood — workers, market sellers, tradespeople. It has not changed its function. The Civil War came and went; the Franco years came and went; the Eixample around it was gradually renovated into design studios, wine bars, and concept restaurants. Bar Borrell remained what it was.

This is notable because Sant Antoni has become one of the most-discussed barris in Barcelona's recent gentrification literature. The Mercat de Sant Antoni's renovation and its Sunday book market have made the neighbourhood a destination for the city's design-conscious class. Bars around it have repositioned accordingly. Bar Borrell has not repositioned. The prices are what the original clientele would have paid (adjusted for inflation, not for brand positioning), and the customers are still largely locals.

There is nothing Instagram-ready about the interior. That is the point. If you want to understand what Barcelona's neighbourhood bars looked like before the city's self-conscious reinvention, this is one of the last functioning examples in the Eixample.
