Santiago Rusiñol (1861–1931) organised the Festes Modernistes at Sitges — the series of gatherings, from 1892 to 1899, that effectively launched Catalan Modernisme as a self-conscious movement. He arrived for the second festival carrying two El Greco paintings he had purchased in Paris, processing through Sitges with them on a bier in mock-religious ceremony. The gesture announced exactly the sensibility he was promoting: art as religion, beauty as the highest value, and the artist as priest of a secular aesthetic faith.

Rusiñol was rich enough to be a full-time bohemian — his family's textile fortune funded his Paris years, his Sitges garden-museum (the Cau Ferrat, still one of the finest small museums in Catalonia), his morphine habit, and his theatrical productions. His plays, particularly L'alegria que passa and El místic, were popular successes that brought Modernista ideas to audiences who might not otherwise have encountered them. He was, in effect, the movement's publicist as well as one of its most gifted painters.

The tribute near the Citadella park honours a man whose public persona was carefully constructed but whose work was genuinely good — the garden paintings, the iron collection, the atmospheric Parisian interiors — and who understood, better than most of his contemporaries, that a cultural movement needs both aesthetic seriousness and theatrical flair.
