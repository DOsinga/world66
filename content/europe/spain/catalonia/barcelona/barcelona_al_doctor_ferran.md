In the summer of 1885, cholera was sweeping through Valencia and threatening to move north. Jaume Ferran (1851–1929) had been working in his private laboratory for two years on a cholera vaccine, inspired by the germ theory of disease developed by Koch and Pasteur. Without waiting for official approval, he organised a mass vaccination campaign in the Valencia region, inoculating over 50,000 people in a matter of weeks. The death rate in vaccinated communities was dramatically lower than in unvaccinated ones.

The controversy that followed was immediate and ferocious. A commission of French scientists, sent by Pasteur (who was developing a competing vaccine), declared Ferran's preparation unreliable. Spanish authorities withdrew their support. The scientific establishment, unwilling to credit a provincial Catalan doctor working without institutional affiliation, closed ranks against him. He died without the international recognition that comparable achievements — Pasteur's rabies vaccine, Koch's tuberculosis work — routinely received.

The rehabilitation of Ferran's reputation has been slow and incomplete. Modern epidemiological analysis of the 1885 data supports his original claims: the vaccinated populations really did show significantly lower mortality. The monument in Gràcia honours a man who did something genuinely historic and paid for it with decades of professional isolation. The story of who gets credit for scientific discoveries is rarely as clean as the textbooks suggest.
