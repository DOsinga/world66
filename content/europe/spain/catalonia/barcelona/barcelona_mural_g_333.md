When Barcelona won the 1992 Olympic bid in 1986, the city's leadership made a decision that distinguished it from most Olympic hosts: public art would not be an afterthought but a central component of urban renewal. The programme, coordinated by the city's Urban Art Service, commissioned dozens of permanent works from international and Catalan artists, designed to remain as a lasting cultural legacy after the athletes and television cameras had gone.

The Mural G-333 belongs to this moment of systematic aesthetic ambition. The designation 'G-333' is part of the city's internal cataloguing system for public art — a database that now contains over 1,000 works. The coding reflects the bureaucratic seriousness with which Barcelona approached public art: not as occasional adornment but as a managed, inventoried collection spread across the urban fabric.

The Raval location of this mural places it in one of the districts most intensively transformed by the Olympic-era regeneration. The opening of the MACBA, the CCCB, and the Rambla del Raval all happened within a few years of the mural's installation, reshaping a neighbourhood that had been characterised primarily by poverty and social exclusion. Whether the transformation benefited the original residents is a question Barcelona's urbanists have been debating ever since.
