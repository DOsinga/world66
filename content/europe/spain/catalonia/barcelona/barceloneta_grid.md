Walk into Barceloneta from the inland edge and you immediately enter a precise orthogonal grid of streets at 45 degrees to the main grid of the city. This is not an accident and not an aesthetic choice: it is a military solution to a social problem created by a military action. After Barcelona fell to the Bourbon forces of Philip V in 1714, one of the new regime's first acts was to demolish the entire Ribera neighbourhood — the most commercial and politically assertive district in Barcelona — and replace it with a star-shaped fortress, the Ciutadella. The four thousand people who had lived in the Ribera were given no new homes.

They waited. For four decades after 1714, the displaced residents of the Ribera neighbourhood camped on the spit of sand that had accumulated outside the port — the Barceloneta. They built shacks. They fished. They had nowhere else to go because the city had given them nothing. In 1753, the military engineer Juan Martín Cermeño was commissioned to regularize the situation. He designed a grid on the sandbar: long, thin blocks, 10 metres wide, running perpendicular to the shore, with streets at 45 degrees to catch the sea breeze. The streets were numbered, not named: 1st Street, 2nd Street, and so on. The blocks were designed for single-storey construction to preserve sightlines for the harbour cannons.

The single-storey rule was abandoned within a generation as residents added floors, and Barceloneta is now a dense urban neighbourhood. But the original Cermeño grid is completely intact: every street in Barceloneta follows his plan from 1753, which followed the shape of the sandbar, which followed the position of the waves. The neighbourhood is built on what was almost open sea. Stand at any corner and look along the street: the view terminates at the beach or at the mountain, depending on which way you face.
