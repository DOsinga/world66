The Romans named their colonial settlement Colonia Iulia Augusta Paterna Faventia Barcino — a mouthful usually shortened to Barcino. The name's etymology is debated: some scholars derive it from a pre-Roman Iberian or Phoenician toponym; others see Roman naming conventions at work. What is certain is that the name stuck, in modified forms, through Visigoth, Moorish, and medieval Catalan occupation, eventually producing 'Barcelona' — a name that has been in continuous use for over two thousand years.

The Poema visual Bàrcino, installed in the Gothic Quarter, engages with this linguistic sediment using the techniques of concrete poetry: visual arrangement of letters and words on a surface, so that the form carries as much meaning as the content. The work asks the viewer to see the word 'Barcino' not just as a label but as an object with its own history, a fragment of Latin that survived because the city it named also survived.

The Gothic Quarter is itself a kind of visual poem — layers of Roman walls, medieval palaces, Baroque churches, and nineteenth-century reconstructions compressed into a few blocks. Walking through it, you are reading a text written in stone over twenty centuries. The Poema visual Bàrcino makes this palimpsest explicit, inviting you to see the ancient name in the contemporary city around it.
