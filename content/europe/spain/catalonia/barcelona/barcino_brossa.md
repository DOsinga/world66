You are standing on what was once the main gate of a Roman colony. The aqueduct towers rise behind you, still holding the weight of two thousand years. And at your feet, spread across the paving stones in a diagonal line, seven letters spell out B-A-R-C-I-N-O — the city's original name, as the Romans who founded it around 10 BCE would have written it.

Joan Brossa, the Catalan poet and visual artist who became Barcelona's great street typographer, designed this in 1994. Each letter is its own invention. The B is a Brossa classic. The A rises like a pyramid with a grave accent, the Catalan way. The R borrows from a typewriter. The C curves like a crescent moon. The I sits in bas-relief inside a rectangle. The N is shaped like a sailboat, pointing north. The O carries a sun, referencing the Mediterranean just a few kilometres away. Six letters are bronze; the final N is aluminium — a material shift that makes you look twice.

Six of the seven letters sit flush in the pavement; the seventh rises slightly. Walk through them, step over them, photograph them from the steps — but stop a moment and consider that this exact spot was where the city began, where the Roman road entered, where the gate stood. Brossa was not making decoration. He was marking the ground.
