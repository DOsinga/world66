During the Spanish Civil War, the hill known as Turó de la Rovira became one of Barcelona's main anti-aircraft defence positions. Four 75mm guns were emplaced here in 1937, chosen for the hill's clear sightlines over the city and the sea. The bombardments they were meant to intercept — carried out by Italian planes flying from Mallorca in support of Franco — killed over a thousand Barcelona civilians and turned the aerial attack on civilian populations into a grim laboratory that informed German and British planners before the Second World War.

After the war the guns fell silent but the hill found a grimmer second life. From the 1940s onward, migrants from Andalusia, Murcia, and other impoverished regions poured into Barcelona faster than the city could house them. Thousands built barraques — illegal shanty structures — on the slopes of Carmel and other peripheral hills. At its peak the Turó de la Rovira settlement housed hundreds of families in conditions of extreme poverty, invisible to the tourist city below. The last barraques were not cleared until the 1990s.

The foundations you can see today are the traces of both histories: the concrete gun platforms of the Civil War and the floor plans of the shantytown built over them. The view from the top is among the finest in Barcelona, taking in the city from Montjuïc to Tibidabo — exactly the panorama that made the hill valuable to artillerymen and attractive, later, to photographers and walkers who know what they are standing on.
