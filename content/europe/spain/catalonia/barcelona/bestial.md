Before 1992, the stretch of waterfront between the old city and the Olympic Village was largely inaccessible to the public — a tangle of industrial facilities, railway lines, and military installations that had grown up over two centuries between the city and its sea. The Olympic project cleared all of this, built the new Barceloneta beach, extended the promenade, and commissioned a series of large-scale public sculptures to articulate the new public space. Bestial was one of these.

The name — Catalan for 'beastly' or 'brutish,' but also used colloquially to mean 'fantastic' or 'wild' — plays on this ambiguity between the rough and the wonderful. The seafront transformation was both: it created a magnificent public amenity while displacing the working-class communities and industries that had occupied the zone. The fishermen's cooperatives, the industrial workers, the Barceloneta residents who had long maintained an adversarial but intimate relationship with the port — their world was replaced by what you see today.

Walking the promenade now, past the sculpture and out toward the Hotel Arts and the Olympic Harbour, it is worth knowing that this polished urban surface is relatively recent, and that the 'bestial' ambivalence of the artwork's name captures something real about how transformations of public space work: simultaneously generous and violent, beautiful and displacing.
