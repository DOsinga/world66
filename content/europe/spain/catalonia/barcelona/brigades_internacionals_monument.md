The International Brigades were composed of approximately 35,000 volunteers from 53 countries who came to Spain between 1936 and 1938 to fight for the Republican government against Franco's Nationalist forces, supported by Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. Of those 35,000, around 3,000 were killed. Their departure, when the Republic dissolved them in 1938 in a vain attempt to persuade the non-intervention powers to withdraw the German and Italian forces, was marked by one of the most filmed farewell scenes in modern history.

Most of the volunteers arrived through Barcelona. They landed at the port, were transported through the city by truck, and were cheered by crowds who lined the streets. Barcelona's reception of the Brigades in 1936 reflected the city's political character at the moment of the war's beginning: the workers' organisations had defeated Franco's coup in the city and were running it on collectivist lines. The foreign volunteers arriving to defend the Republic were received as comrades.

The memorial in the Horta neighbourhood — a more modest part of Barcelona than the waterfront or the city centre — was installed in 1996 on the sixtieth anniversary of the Brigades' arrival. The location, away from the tourist circuits, means the memorial is visited primarily by people who specifically seek it out: descendants of volunteers, left-wing pilgrims, historians. It is a quieter memorial for that, and perhaps more honest about the historical relationship between the Brigades and a Barcelona that has since become something rather different from the city that welcomed them.
