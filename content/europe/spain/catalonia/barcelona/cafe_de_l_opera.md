The Cafè de l'Òpera has occupied the corner of La Rambla and Carrer de Sant Pau since 1929, though the building is older, dating to the 1870s when the Liceu opera house across the street was the centre of Barcelona's bourgeois social life. For over a century, the café's function has been the same: a place to arrive before the curtain and to argue about the performance after. The interior — dark wood panelling, marble-topped tables, ceiling mirrors, wrought-iron fittings — has not been substantially altered since the 1920s fit-out.

The Liceu was bombed in 1893 by an anarchist who threw two Orsini bombs into the stalls, killing 22. The subsequent police crackdown, show trials, and executions defined Barcelona's political atmosphere for a generation. None of this touched the café's architecture, but it saturated the street. The same La Rambla that the opera crowd walked was the axis of the working-class Raval directly behind it — a geography that made the café a junction between two cities that officially did not acknowledge each other.

Today the Cafè de l'Òpera is busy and unapologetic about its position on the most tourist-dense street in Barcelona. The coffee is competent, the prices are La Rambla prices, and the interior remains one of the few genuine 1920s café spaces left in the city. Sit at the bar if you want the room without a long wait for a table.
