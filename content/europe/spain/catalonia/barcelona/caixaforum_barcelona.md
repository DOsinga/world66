When the textile manufacturer Casimir Casaramona commissioned Josep Puig i Cadafalch to design his new factory at the foot of Montjuïc in 1911, he got something considerably more ambitious than a functional industrial building. Puig i Cadafalch, who was simultaneously designing large houses in the Eixample and had just completed the Casa Amatller on the Manzana de la Discordia, brought the full repertoire of modernista industrial architecture to bear: brick towers designed to evoke medieval Catalan castles, ceramic details by Lluís Bru, an iron structural system disguised behind expressive ornamental facades.

The building's subsequent history is a compressed version of Catalonia's twentieth century. After Casaramona's business failed, the Guardia Civil converted it into a mounted police barracks in the 1940s, covering its ornamental surfaces, installing stables, and using it for fifty years as a functional if entirely inappropriate police facility. The building survived largely because the military and police were good at maintaining structures they occupied; they were terrible at appreciating what they had.

The conversion to a cultural centre by the Fundació La Caixa in the early 2000s, designed by Arata Isozaki and Robert Brufau with a spectacular underground entrance pavilion, is one of Barcelona's most successful adaptive reuse projects. The original factory floor is now an exhibition space, the towers are accessible, and the building reads again as what Puig i Cadafalch intended: a demonstration that industrial architecture could be as beautiful as anything built for bourgeois domestic comfort.
