The tower was built in 1862 as part of the church of Sant Joan de Gràcia, when Gràcia was not a Barcelona neighbourhood but an independent municipality of some 40,000 people — a population that made it larger than most Spanish provincial cities at the time. Gràcia had its own city hall, its own markets, its own political culture, and a strong working-class and republican identity that made it distinct from the conservative bourgeois city expanding around it. The bell tower was the municipality's most visible marker, visible from the plain below.

The Spanish central government forced Gràcia's annexation to Barcelona in 1897 over the sustained objection of its residents. The town lost its political independence, its institutions, and its status as an autonomous entity. According to local record, the annexation was mourned by ringing the bells of this tower continuously — not as celebration but as lament, a sound that lasted until the ringing became protest itself. The annexation is still remembered in Gràcia's cultural memory: the neighbourhood retains a stronger sense of local identity than almost anywhere else in Barcelona, and its *festa major* in August is treated as a civic assertion as much as a street party.

The tower stands free of the church on what is now Plaça de la Vila de Gràcia, surrounded by the ordinary life of a Barcelona neighbourhood. It is an inadvertent monument to municipal extinction — a bell tower that outlasted the municipality it was built to serve.
