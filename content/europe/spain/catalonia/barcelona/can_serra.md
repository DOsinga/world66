The building never returned to private hands. When the Serra family sold Can Serra to the Diputació de Barcelona in the early twentieth century, the provincial government embedded itself so thoroughly in Puig's design that the mansion's civic identity eventually eclipsed its domestic origins. The ground-floor rooms were adapted for administration; the interiors were altered. But the facade, which is what you are here for, remained untouched.

Puig i Cadafalch was the most politically engaged of the Modernisme architects — a Catalan nationalist who served in parliament and later led the Commonwealth of Catalonia. His buildings are rarely neutral. Can Serra quotes the vocabulary of Catalan Gothic — pointed lancet windows, corner towers with conical roofs, carved stone tracery — but organises it into a street facade that reads more as manifesto than residence. The stone is pale and finely worked; the tower anchors the corner with a medieval authority that makes the Eixample grid feel provisional by comparison.

Puig designed the building at the height of his powers, in the same decade he produced Casa Amatller (1900) and Casa de les Punxes (1905). Can Serra is smaller and less visited than either, which is part of its appeal: the facade receives far fewer phones pointed at it than the better-known Modernisme addresses nearby, and the Diputació's continued occupation means the building has been maintained with institutional seriousness rather than left to the tourist economy.
