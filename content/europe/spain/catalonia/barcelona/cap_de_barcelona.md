Roy Lichtenstein created this towering ceramic column-head for the 1992 Olympics at the request of the Barcelona city government. Standing 15 metres tall at the edge of the old port, it is one of his largest public works and almost certainly his most exposed: visible from the water, from the Rambla, and from the windows of the Barceloneta neighbourhood above. The figure — a stylised female face in Lichtenstein's signature bold outlines and Ben-Day dots, executed in glazed ceramic — deliberately plays with the tradition of the Roman column topped with a portrait.

The work cost around 250 million pesetas (roughly 1.5 million euros in today's values), a figure that became the centre of intense public debate during Barcelona's Olympic preparation. Critics argued the money should have gone to social housing or public infrastructure. Supporters countered that placing a world-class artist's work at the gateway to the city was exactly what the Olympics were for. The debate was never fully resolved, and the sculpture still attracts ambivalent reactions.

What the critics could not have anticipated was how well the work would age. In an era of corporate public art and bland urban design, the Cap de Barcelona's unapologetic scale and Pop Art vocabulary have come to look almost audacious. Lichtenstein died in 1997, just five years after it was unveiled. The sculpture is now one of a handful of permanent outdoor works by him anywhere in the world, and the only one on a Mediterranean waterfront.
