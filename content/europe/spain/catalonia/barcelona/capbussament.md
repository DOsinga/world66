The word *capbussament* in Catalan means "submersion" or "a dive headfirst into water," and that is exactly what this bronze figure appears to be doing — except the water has been replaced by stone. The piece sits at the edge of Barceloneta, the old fishermen's quarter that was built in the eighteenth century to rehouse residents displaced by the construction of the Ciutadella military fortress, and whose identity has always been inseparable from the Mediterranean.

The sculpture plays on that identity with dry wit. Barceloneta's relationship with the sea is simultaneously its greatest asset and its most contested resource: the beach was industrialised, then cleaned up, then turned over to mass tourism, then fought over by residents who simply want somewhere to swim. The diver catches that ambivalence — plunging toward something just out of reach, suspended between yearning and arrival.

Placed at street level where pedestrians can stumble upon it without ceremony, *Capbussament* belongs to a tradition of Barcelona public art that refuses to announce itself grandly. No plinth, no explanatory panel at eye level, just a figure committed to a gesture the city itself has been performing for centuries.
