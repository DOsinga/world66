When Philip V ordered the construction of the Ciutadella citadel after the Siege of Barcelona in 1714 — punishing the city for backing the losing side in the War of Spanish Succession — he also razed the Ribera neighbourhood to clear space. The Capella Castrense, or military chapel, was part of the fortress complex that rose in its place, a symbol of Bourbon authority over a defeated city.

The irony is that the citadel was demolished in 1869 precisely because Barcelonans hated it so deeply, and the cleared land was donated to the city for the park and 1888 World's Fair. Almost everything military vanished — barracks, ramparts, gates — but the chapel survived, absorbed into the new civic landscape as a curious remnant. It now sits quietly inside a park that was itself an act of anti-militarist urban renewal.

The building's persistence is a reminder that history rarely erases itself cleanly. The same stones that once served a garrison chapel today stand inside a park where families picnic, couples row on the lake, and the Barcelona Zoo keeps its animals. The punitive military architecture that was meant to overawe a city ended up becoming part of that city's playground.
