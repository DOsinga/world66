In medieval Barcelona, the road northeast out of the city gates — toward France and the rest of Christian Europe — began where the Capella d'en Marcús now stands. The wealthy cloth merchant Bernat Marcús had it built around 1160 as a wayfarers' chapel: a place where travellers could pray before a long journey and postal couriers could take an oath of honest delivery. It was essentially a civic trust infrastructure, using religion as the binding mechanism.

The *confraria dels correus*, the guild of postal messengers, was officially attached to this chapel, making it the spiritual headquarters of Barcelona's communication network. Messengers swore their oaths here before heading north with correspondence for merchants and nobles — an early post office with liturgical obligations baked in.

The chapel is one of the very few Romanesque structures left in the old city. Barcelona's Gothic quarter is mostly Gothic, and the Romanesque was largely swept away by fourteenth-century expansion or nineteenth-century "restoration" that reimagined medieval buildings according to Romantic fantasies. Capella d'en Marcús escaped all of that, sitting on its corner of Carrer dels Carders quietly waiting for travellers who no longer need to swear an oath before they leave.
