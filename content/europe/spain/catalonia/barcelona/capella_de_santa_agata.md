The Capella de Santa Àgata was commissioned by King James II of Aragon in 1302 as the private chapel of the royal palace, and its builders made an unusual structural choice: rather than demolishing the Roman walls of the ancient city of Barcino, they built the chapel on top of them. You can still stand at the base of the bell tower and see Roman stonework beneath the Gothic additions — nearly two thousand years of construction in a single glance.

Inside, the altarpiece — the *Retaule del Conestable* painted by Jaume Huguet in 1465 — is considered a masterpiece of Catalan Gothic painting. It was commissioned by the Constable of Portugal, who briefly held Barcelona as king during the Catalan Civil War, a period of extraordinary political turbulence. Huguet took nine years to complete it, and the result is one of the most detailed and emotionally sophisticated panel paintings of the fifteenth century in Iberia.

The chapel is part of the Museu d'Història de Barcelona complex, which means entry requires a ticket — but the route through the underground Roman city below makes the admission worthwhile on purely archaeological grounds before you even reach the chapel itself.
