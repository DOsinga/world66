The chapel appears to grow out of the Roman wall itself. Built between 1302 and 1314 as the private chapel of the royal palace of the counts-kings of Barcelona, the Capella de Santa Àgata sits above the Roman defensive wall at the highest point of the ancient city, its bell tower — the Torre Mirador del Rei Martí — rising above everything around it. From outside, in Plaça del Rei, the chapel is the structure directly ahead as you enter the square: the tall pointed window above the staircase, the slim tower to the left.

Inside is the Conestable altarpiece, painted by Jaume Huguet in 1465. Huguet was the leading Catalan painter of the 15th century, and this is his signature work: an elaborate polyptych showing scenes from the Epiphany, the Three Magi approaching the Virgin, the figures rendered with the psychological specificity and luminous colour of the Late Gothic at its height. The altarpiece was commissioned by the Constable of Portugal, who briefly held the Catalan throne during the civil wars of the 1460s.

The chapel's floor is built over the Roman wall, which is why the floor level is elevated above the rest of the palace. Sections of the ancient masonry are visible in the chapel's lower walls — stones set without mortar in the Roman manner, incorporated into a building constructed more than a thousand years later. The chapel is administered as part of the Museu d'Història de Barcelona and requires an entry ticket. The altarpiece rewards close examination: the faces of the Magi, especially, have a quality of individual attention that makes you feel they were painted from life.
