Carmela commemorates the thousands of donkeys that were essential to Barcelona's construction economy well into the twentieth century. The city's quarries on Montjuïc produced the stone for countless buildings, and it was working animals — donkeys above all — that transported the heavy loads up and down the hill and through the city streets before mechanised transport made them redundant.

The sculpture was placed here in the Born neighbourhood, close to the old artisan quarter where animals and carts moved constantly through narrow lanes. The donkey is rendered with affectionate realism rather than monumental grandeur: this is a working animal at rest, not a heroic symbol. Barcelona has a tradition of small, unexpected tributes to the unglamorous labour that built the city — street sweepers, fishwives, day labourers — and Carmela belongs to that populist counter-canon of public monuments.

The name itself is telling. Rather than naming the sculpture after a saint or a historical figure, the city gave it a common Catalan woman's name, anthropomorphising the animal in a way that insists on its individuality and dignity. Carmela is somebody, not just something.
