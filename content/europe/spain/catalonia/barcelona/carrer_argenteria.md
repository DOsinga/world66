Walk down Carrer de l'Argenteria from the Via Laietana end and you are on the route that medieval Barcelonians called the street of the silver-workers. Argenteria comes from argent, silver — the same root as the word for Argentina, named by Spanish colonists for the silver they expected to find there. On this street in the 13th and 14th centuries, the jewellers, silversmiths, and goldsmiths of Barcelona worked, sold their wares, and lived above their workshops.

The location was deliberate. This street was the main axis connecting the commercial heart of the old city — the Plaça del Blat (Wheat Market, now roughly Plaça de l'Àngel) — to the waterfront and to Santa Maria del Mar, the parish church built by and for the people of the La Ribera commercial district. The silver and gold trade needed to be near both: near the wealthy merchants who were the customers, and near the port through which the raw materials arrived. Barcelona's medieval silver trade was connected to the mining production of Catalonia and Aragon, and to the trading networks that stretched as far as the Levant.

Nothing of the medieval craft trade survives in the buildings. The street was rebuilt in various phases, and most of the current structures date from the 18th and 19th centuries. What survives is the line of the street, unchanged since the medieval layout, and the name pressed into the tile plaque on the corner — Argenteria, telling you what happened here before telling you where you are.
