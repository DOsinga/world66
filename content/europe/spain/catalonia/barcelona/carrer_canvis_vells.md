The name Canvis Vells means Old Exchanges — and there was a Carrer dels Canvis Nous (New Exchanges) nearby, which tells you that the money-changing trade expanded at some point and needed a new street. The canvis — exchange booths or tables — were where medieval merchants could convert between the currencies of different kingdoms and city-states that flowed through Barcelona's port in the 13th and 14th centuries. A ship from Genoa arrived with Genoese pounds; the goods it carried needed to be paid for in Barcelona lliures or Aragonese sueldos; the canviadors sat at their tables and made the conversion, keeping their percentage.

The financial infrastructure of medieval Barcelona was concentrated in the streets around Santa Maria del Mar, the merchant church. The Carrer dels Canvis Vells ran parallel to the church's south face, and the exchange booths here were close enough to the waterfront that a merchant could walk from the port to the exchange and back in under ten minutes. The proximity was the whole point: medieval commerce ran on trust and liquidity, and the exchange tables needed to be where the trade was.

There was a formal exchange building in the neighbourhood too — the Llotja, the merchants' exchange, built in the late 14th century on the waterfront (the building survives, now used by the art school and chamber of commerce). But before the Llotja existed and alongside it, the informal exchange network of the street persisted. Carrer dels Canvis Vells carries the name of that older, less formal trade: the tables set up under the open sky where medieval Barcelona's international commerce was converted into something people could spend.
