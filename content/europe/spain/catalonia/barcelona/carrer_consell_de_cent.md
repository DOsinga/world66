The Consell de Cent (Council of the Hundred) was established in 1265 as the principal governing body of Barcelona. Its name was slightly misleading from the start: the membership fluctuated between 100 and 200 citizens depending on the period, and the actual decision-making was concentrated in a smaller executive committee. But the name stuck, and the institution with it.

The Consell de Cent was responsible for the day-to-day governance of Barcelona: collecting taxes, maintaining public works, negotiating with the Crown, managing the city's debt, and representing the interests of the merchant and artisan classes against the nobility. It operated continuously for almost four centuries, until the Bourbon conquest of 1714, when Philip V abolished it by decree as part of his programme of centralising power in Madrid. The Decret de Nova Planta of 1716 replaced all Catalan self-governing institutions with royal administration.

The street in the Eixample that bears the Consell's name is an 1880s addition to the city, named when the liberal politics of the Restoration period made it possible to commemorate medieval Catalan institutions without offending the government. It is one of the Eixample's major cross-streets, running from the Diagonal to the Ronda de Sant Pere. The Galeria Maeght, which showed Miró, Tàpies, and Chillida from the 1960s onward, was located here; the street became associated with contemporary art galleries in the post-Franco period, a connection that continues.
