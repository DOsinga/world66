The guild of sword- and armour-makers did not scatter across the city — they concentrated. Medieval Barcelona organised its trades spatially, and the espasers (sword-smiths) anchored themselves here, near the Mercat del Born and the sea routes that brought in Spanish steel and Flemish iron. A swordsmith's workshop required a forge, grinding stones, and skilled hands for tempering and finishing; clusters made it easier to share materials, apprentices, and customers.

The Catalan word espaseria derives from espasa (sword), itself from Vulgar Latin spatha, the broad-bladed Roman cavalry sword. By the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, when Barcelona was the military and commercial capital of the Crown of Aragon, this street supplied weapons to soldiers, merchants, and the nobility across the western Mediterranean. The Crown's campaigns in Sardinia, Sicily, and Naples created steady demand.

The trade died out here long before the Born was restored, but the name stayed. Barcelona kept hundreds of these guild-street names as the city grew and the crafts dispersed — a street-level index of what the medieval economy actually looked like, trade by trade.
