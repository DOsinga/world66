The Carrer de Blai in Poble Sec is now known throughout Barcelona as the "pintxos street" — a dense concentration of bars serving the Basque-style tapas-on-bread that became fashionable across Spain from the 1980s onward. The street's transformation from an ordinary working-class residential lane into a gastronomic destination is a story about how Barcelona neighbourhoods reinvent themselves commercially while maintaining certain social continuities.

Poble Sec (Dry Village) is a working-class neighbourhood on the slopes below Montjuïc, separated from the Raval by the Avinguda del Paral·lel. Its population through the 20th century was largely Andalusian and Murcian immigrants who came to Barcelona for factory work. The neighbourhood retains a high proportion of older residents and a commercial character dominated by small family businesses.

The pintxos bars began opening on the Carrer de Blai in the early 1990s, when the Basque food tradition was being adopted across Catalonia as a social format: cheap, varied, informal eating that could support a two-hour social occasion on a small budget. The format was well-suited to a working-class street: lower price points than restaurant dining, a social atmosphere that matched the neighbourhood culture, and an appeal to both locals and the students and artists who were beginning to move into the affordable Poble Sec rental market. The street now serves as a kind of compressed neighbourhood entertainment strip, most busy in the early evening when the pintxos-and-cava economy operates at full capacity.
