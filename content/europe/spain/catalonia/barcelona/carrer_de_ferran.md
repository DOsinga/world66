Carrer de Ferran connects La Rambla to the Plaça de Sant Jaume in a straight line, passing through the heart of the Gòtic quarter. The straightness is artificial and relatively recent: the street was cut through the existing medieval fabric in the 1820s as part of Barcelona's first deliberate street-widening project. The city had been studying Napoleonic Paris, where Haussmann-style interventions were already being planned, and Carrer de Ferran was an early local experiment in the same logic — create a wide, straight commercial street by demolishing whatever stood in its path.

The street was named for Fernando VII, the Bourbon king who ruled Spain from 1814 to 1833, alternating between constitutionalism and absolute monarchy according to the political winds. Fernando was not loved in Catalonia, and the naming was an act of royal loyalty by a city that was already chafing under Bourbon centralism. The street was built during his reign, the name stuck, and it has never been changed despite the various renaming campaigns that swept Barcelona during the Republic, the Franco period, and the democratic restoration.

In the spring of 1937, when factional fighting broke out between anarchists and Stalinist-aligned forces in Barcelona, Carrer de Ferran became one of the front lines. George Orwell was in Barcelona at the time, attached to a POUM militia, and describes the street in Homage to Catalonia as a territory that shifted between factions as the days of fighting wore on. The Plaça de Sant Jaume at one end and the Rambla at the other were both contested; the straight street between them was the kind of space that becomes a killing ground when people are shooting from doorways and balconies.
