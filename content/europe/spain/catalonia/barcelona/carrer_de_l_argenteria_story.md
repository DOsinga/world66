The Carrer de l'Argenteria (Silversmith Street) has been the main east-west artery connecting the Gothic Quarter to the port since the medieval period. The name records the guild of argenter s — silversmiths — who occupied workshops along this street from at least the 13th century. The silver trade was among the most regulated and prestigious of the medieval craft guilds: precious metals required special oversight to prevent fraud, and the guild maintained strict standards of purity and marking.

Barcelona's silversmithing tradition was closely tied to the city's role as the commercial capital of the Crown of Aragon. The wealth generated by Mediterranean trade required financial instruments, and financial instruments required silver — for coins, for weights, for the verification tools used in money-changing. The argent ers who worked in this street served both the luxury trade (ecclesiastical silver, domestic plate for wealthy families) and the commercial economy (weights and measures, currency testing).

The street today connects the Plaça de l'Àngel — where the gate in the Roman and medieval wall stood — to the church of Santa Maria del Mar, whose construction between 1329 and 1383 was funded by the parishioners of the La Ribera neighbourhood, many of them the very merchants and craftspeople whose guilds named the surrounding streets. The silversmith who worked at the Carrer de l'Argenteria in 1350 would have watched the walls of Santa Maria del Mar rising at the end of his street.
