The Carrer de la Barra de Ferro (Street of the Iron Bar) has a name that is easier to understand once you know what a barra meant in the context of medieval commerce. The word referred not to a bar as a drinking establishment, but to the counter or table used by money-changers and assayers — the iron bar was the physical instrument over which transactions took place. A barra de canvi (exchange bar) was a money-changer's table; a barra de ferro was one made of iron.

The street was the location of money-changing operations associated with the medieval trading activity of the Born neighbourhood. The merchants who used the nearby Rec Comtal, who traded through the Llotja, and who processed goods in the surrounding workshops needed to exchange different currencies. Barcelona in the medieval period circulated several currencies simultaneously — Aragonese, Castilian, Genoese, Venetian, Florentine — and specialists who could value and exchange them were essential to commerce.

The name survived long after the money-changers had moved, consolidated, or been absorbed into the formal banking system. It is now simply the address of a few restaurants and a passage leading to the Mercat de Santa Caterina. But the iron bar that hung outside a 14th-century money-changer's shop is more precisely commemorated in this street name than any number of deliberately installed heritage plaques.
