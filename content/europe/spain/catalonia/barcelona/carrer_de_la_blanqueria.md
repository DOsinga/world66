Tanning was universally pushed to the edges of medieval cities because of the stench: hides had to be soaked for months in solutions of urine, animal fat, and bark-derived tannin before they became usable leather. That the blanquers' guild maintained a presence this close to the city center tells you how indispensable leather was — for shoes, saddles, harness, bookbinding, and military equipment — and how much economic leverage that gave the guild.

The Rec Comtal, Barcelona's main water channel diverted from the Besòs river, ran just north of this street and supplied the tanneries with the constant flow they required. The canal was the industrial spine of the Sant Pere district, powering mills and tanneries along its course before it was progressively covered and forgotten over the 19th century. Carrer de la Blanqueria was positioned to draw from it directly.

The street today forms part of the arc of medieval trade-name streets curving through Sant Pere — Blanqueria, Cotoners, Flassaders (blanket-makers), Assaonadors (leather-finishers) — a surviving map of an economy organized entirely around cloth and hide.
