The etymology is disputed and more interesting for it. "Boqueria" may derive from "boc" — Catalan for goat, suggesting a livestock or meat market — or from the Arabic "borch," meaning market stall, a piece of trading vocabulary that filtered into Catalan commercial language through centuries of Mediterranean exchange. Either way, the street sat just inside the old Roman wall's gate, the natural chokepoint where sellers could set up outside the stricter jurisdiction of the city's regulated indoor markets.

When the Convent de Sant Josep was suppressed and demolished in 1835, following the liberal confiscation of church properties, the city had its chance to formalize what had long been happening informally. The iron-and-glass market hall that replaced the convent simply codified and roofed over a commercial tradition already centuries old on this street.

The covered market that grew up here eventually became so dominant that the street itself became little more than a ramp leading to the market entrance, its original identity as a commercial artery absorbed into the institution it named.
