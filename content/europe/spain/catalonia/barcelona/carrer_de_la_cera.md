The Carrer de la Cera (Wax Street) in the Raval records the location of the cerers — the wax-chandlers who made candles for Barcelona's churches and for domestic lighting. Candle-making was one of the essential trades of the pre-modern city: before gas lighting arrived in the 1840s, every public building, church, and domestic interior that required light after dark required candles, and the quality and quantity of candles in a building was a direct measure of its wealth.

The cerers occupied a position between manufacturers and retailers: they purchased raw wax (beeswax for the highest quality, tallow for the ordinary grades) and processed it into candles of various sizes, grades, and decorative complexity. Church candles required specific sizes and purity standards mandated by the ecclesiastical authorities. The guild of cerers regulated both the quality of the product and the conditions of production.

The Carrer de la Cera runs through the Raval parallel to the hospital complex. The street is now entirely residential and commercial, with no visible evidence of the industry that gave it its name. The neighbouring streets — Carrer del Carme (dedicated to the Carmelite convent at its end), Carrer de la Riera Alta (following a seasonal stream) — together tell the story of a neighbourhood that was, for most of its history, a zone of craft production and religious institutions, outside the city wall and outside the city's normal regulatory framework.
