In the dense fabric of El Born, street names are often the only surviving evidence of what once stood or grew here before the medieval city filled in every patch of open ground. Carrer de la Cirera — Cherry Street — preserves the memory of a time when this lane ran past gardens or orchards rather than the stone-faced palaces and workshops that came later. The name appears in documents from the fourteenth century, when the neighbourhood was expanding rapidly on the wealth generated by the Crown of Aragon's Mediterranean trading empire.

The street is short, running between Carrer de Montcada and the surrounding lanes, and in its current form it is emphatically urban — medieval stone on both sides, the buildings pressed close together as is standard in the Ribera quarter. But the name gestures toward an earlier landscape, when Barcelona's aristocratic and merchant families were still choosing their building plots and the spaces between were gardens, orchards, or simply undeveloped land. Cherry trees, grown for their fruit, were common enough in the city's margins to name streets and squares after them.

Walking the street now, between the Gothic palaces of Montcada and the quieter blocks to the north, the horticultural origin is entirely invisible. The city has been built solid for six centuries. But the name is a small act of remembrance, the medieval city's habit of recording what it displaced by naming the replacement after it — water, trees, trades, and landmarks that no longer exist except in the street signs.
