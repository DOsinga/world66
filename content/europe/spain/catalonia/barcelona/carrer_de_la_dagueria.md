The daga was the everyday sidearm of medieval urban life. Swords were for soldiers and nobles; the dagger was democratic — worn openly by merchants, craftsmen, pilgrims, and anyone else who traveled through a city where street violence was common and law enforcement was thin and slow. Demand was constant and spread across every social class, making the daguers' trade more economically stable than that of the sword-makers who served a narrower military elite.

The craftsmen who made daggers occupied a precise niche in the metalworking hierarchy — above the blacksmith who worked heavier, cruder iron, below the espaser (sword-maker) in prestige, but filling a volume of orders neither of the others could match. Their location in the Gothic Quarter placed them at the center of the commercial district, within easy reach of the merchant class that bought their wares daily and the guild system that regulated their prices and quality standards.

As the medieval economy shifted and personal weapons became less universal — partly through changing social norms, partly through the consolidation of crafts — the daguers were absorbed into broader cutlery and metalwork guilds. The street name is the only evidence left that this was once a specialized street of weapons workshops.
