Timber was a strategic commodity in medieval Barcelona. The city built ships, houses, furniture, and barrels — all of it consuming wood that Catalonia's immediate hinterland could not supply in sufficient volume. Timber floated down rivers from the Pyrenees and arrived by sea from Provence and beyond. Woodworkers concentrated near the port precisely to intercept that supply: carpenters, joiners, barrel-makers (bota is barrel; the nearby Carrer dels Boters remembers them), and timber merchants all worked within a few blocks of the waterfront.

Fuster in Catalan comes from Latin fustis, a staff or log, which evolved through Old French into a broader word for carpenter. The street's position close to the Llotja — Barcelona's medieval commodity exchange, built in the 1380s — was not accidental. Timber was traded there alongside wool, grain, and spices; the craftsmen who worked it needed to be nearby to inspect shipments and negotiate prices.

The fuster trade gave Catalonia one of its most common surnames: Fuster, the equivalent of the English name Carpenter or the German Zimmermann. It survives as a family name across Catalonia and Valencia, a reminder of how closely medieval identity tied to craft and quarter.
