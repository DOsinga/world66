Barcelona's Roman walls were rebuilt and extended repeatedly through the medieval period, and the gates through them were named for their construction or their destination. Porta Ferrissa — the iron-fitted gate — stood at the point where this street met the second ring of medieval walls, constructed in the late thirteenth century as the city expanded beyond its Roman footprint. An iron portcullis dropped through a slot in the gatehouse; when it fell, the city was sealed.

Ferrissa in Catalan comes from ferro (iron), with a suffix suggesting something heavily iron-clad or iron-tipped. The gate was demolished long ago as Barcelona outgrew its walls, but a ceramic azulejo panel mounted on the wall at the street's upper end records what it looked like and shows a detail that most visitors miss: the gate also housed a public fountain fed by the city's water system, making it a social gathering point as well as a defensive structure. The image is one of the most informative pieces of street-level urban history in the city.

The street runs between what was the gate site and the Rambla, connecting the medieval city to its main processional route. That position made it a natural location for market stalls and small commerce from early on — the traffic passing in and out of the gate guaranteed a captive audience for traders who set up on either side.
