The Carrer de la Princesa was cut through the dense medieval fabric of the La Ribera neighbourhood in 1853, creating a wide straight street connecting the Plaça de l'Àngel to the Ciutadella park. The project required the demolition of multiple medieval buildings and the displacement of their residents — an operation similar in character to the Haussmann projects in Paris, which were happening simultaneously and were explicitly a model for Barcelona's urban reformers.

The name honours Princess Isabella, daughter of Queen Isabella II — the naming of a new street after the princess was a standard form of political flattery in the period. The street's width (it is noticeably broader than the surrounding medieval streets) reflects the 19th-century priority of traffic movement: the old city's narrow streets were considered obstacles to commerce and to military control, and the new straight boulevards cut through them addressed both concerns.

The 1853 demolitions removed buildings that had stood since the medieval period and altered the street plan of the Born in ways that are still visible on a map: the straight line of the Carrer de la Princesa interrupts the organic medieval grid at a right angle, creating the kind of geometric disruption that characterises 19th-century urban surgery. The Born neighbourhood's current character — a mix of medieval, 19th-century, and later fabric — reflects this intervention as much as it reflects the medieval origins of the district.
