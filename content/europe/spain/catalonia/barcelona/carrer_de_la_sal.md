The Carrer de la Sal (Salt Street) in Barceloneta records the central role of salt in the neighbourhood's fishing economy. Before refrigeration, salt was the only practical preservative for fish on a commercial scale. The salt cellars (salines) that stored supplies for the fishing industry were located in and around this street, close to the beach where the boats came in.

The salt trade was heavily controlled by the state throughout the early modern period. The Spanish Crown maintained a salt monopoly from the 16th century onward, and the management of salt distribution was a significant source of both revenue and corruption. In the fishing communities of the Mediterranean coast, access to affordable salt was a constant political issue: if the price of salt was too high, preservation became impossible and the fishing economy collapsed. The salt cellars of Barceloneta were one node in a supply chain that ran from the salt pans of the Camargue and the Balearic islands through official distribution channels to the neighbourhood.

The street is now undistinguished — a residential lane between the main Barceloneta arteries. But its name is a precise record of an industrial supply chain that no longer exists: salt is cheap, fish is refrigerated, and the cellar buildings were demolished long ago. The neighbourhood's economic identity has shifted so completely — from fishing community to tourist beach resort — that the salt street is now simply an address.
