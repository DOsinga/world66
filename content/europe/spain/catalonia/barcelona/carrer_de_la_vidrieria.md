Medieval Barcelona had a glass industry that was significant enough to give a street its name. The *vidriers* — glassmakers — produced both functional wares and decorative pieces for a city that, by the 13th and 14th centuries, was one of the wealthiest trading ports in the western Mediterranean. The street sits in what is now the Born district, close to the old commercial waterfront, which is where you would expect luxury craft trades to concentrate: near money, near merchants, near the ships that brought in the raw materials.

Glass production in medieval Catalonia drew on a long tradition that mixed Islamic and Venetian techniques. Sand, ash, and mineral colorants were fused in wood-fired furnaces at temperatures that required constant skilled attention. The finished goods — cups, bottles, window glass for wealthy patrons, pharmacy vessels — were fragile cargo that needed to be sold close to where they were made. The street accommodated both the workshops and the retail trade.

The distinction between production and sales on a street like this would have been blurry. A master glassmaker might blow glass in the back of the same building where his wife sold the finished pieces from a counter facing the street. The guild system that governed the trade also regulated this integration: glassmakers were not supposed to undercut each other, and they were not supposed to sell glass they had not made themselves. Whether these rules were observed is a separate question. What the street name records is the fact of concentration — this is where the glass trade was.
