Carrer de les Caputxes — Hoods Street — is one of El Born's medieval lanes whose name records a craft that no longer exists but once was essential. The hood-makers who gave the street its name produced a specific garment: the pointed capuchon or caputxa worn by members of the religious confraternities in public processions. The design, with its pointed top and the holes for the eyes, is familiar today primarily from Holy Week processions in Seville and other southern Spanish cities, but in medieval Barcelona it was worn across dozens of confraternities throughout the year.

The confraternities were lay religious brotherhoods organized around patron saints, neighbourhoods, or trades. They funded masses for their dead members, ran charitable works, maintained chapels, and staged elaborate public processions that were the major civic spectacle of medieval urban life. Membership in a confraternity was both a religious obligation and a social marker; the wealthier confraternities competed in the elaborateness of their processional displays, which meant costumes, music, and the distinctive hoods that identified each brotherhood's members while concealing their individual identities. The demand for high-quality hoods in a city with dozens of active confraternities was enough to support a specialist street of craftsmen.

The trade disappeared as the confraternities declined and processional culture changed after the Reformation and Counter-Reformation reshuffled European religious practice. The street kept its name. The El Born neighbourhood around it went through the trauma of 1714 — the siege, the demolition of hundreds of buildings, the military occupation — and was rebuilt in a form that preserved many of the old street lines, including this one, even as the social fabric was entirely remade.
