The Carrer de les Freixures takes its name from freixura — a term for the lights, liver, and other organ meats taken from slaughtered animals. These cuts were the cheapest and least valued parts of the carcass and were sold separately from the prime cuts of meat, at prices the working poor could afford. The street records the location where this trade took place.

The freixures trade existed at the margins of the formal butchery guild. Guild regulations concerned themselves primarily with the handling of prime cuts; the organ meats were more loosely regulated and could be sold by people who were not full guild members. This created an entry point into the food trade for the economically marginal — women, migrants, and non-guild members who could not access the more regulated markets.

The freixures of the medieval period were sold raw and cooked. Some vendors boiled or fried the organs before selling them — essentially running a cheap food stall using the least expensive meat available. The food stalls of the medieval city, of which the freixures vendors were one type, occupied a social function broadly comparable to the current street food economy: quick, cheap food for people who did not have the time or facilities to cook. The Carrer de les Freixures in the Born is a street whose name records the lowest economic register of the medieval food trade, in a neighbourhood now associated with the highest price points of contemporary Barcelona dining.
