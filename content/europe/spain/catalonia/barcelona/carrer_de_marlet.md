The lamp at number 1 Carrer de Marlet is one of the oldest surviving street fixtures in Barcelona. The iron bracket and stone surround date to the medieval period, and the Latin inscription beneath it reads: *Sia lum d'esta llanterna / per los pobres que ploren* — "May the light of this lantern / be for the poor who weep." The inscription is actually in medieval Catalan, not Latin, and it dates the lamp to at least the 14th or 15th century. For centuries it was re-lit by the pious residents of the street; today a replica burns there, the original having been moved to the Museu d'Història de Barcelona.

The street itself takes its name from the Marlet family, documented property owners in this part of the Barri Gòtic from the medieval period. Such naming was common: streets in a city without formal addresses were identified by whoever owned or occupied the prominent building at their head or corner. The Marlets were not nobles or great merchants — they were the kind of prosperous urban family whose main historical significance turned out to be the lamp they or their neighbours maintained.

What the lamp reveals is the texture of medieval urban piety. Barcelona's streets were full of small devotional objects — shrines, lamps, carved Madonnas in wall niches — that were maintained by the collective effort of the people who lived and worked on them. Most have disappeared. The Marlet lamp survived because the inscription gave it a narrative, a reason to be remembered and tended. It is now one of the most photographed objects in the Gothic Quarter, visited by people who do not necessarily know why it is there.
