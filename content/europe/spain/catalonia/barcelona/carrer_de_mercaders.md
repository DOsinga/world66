The mercaders who gave this street its name were not small traders. In medieval Catalan law, a mercader was a wholesale merchant — someone who moved goods in bulk across sea routes, who had agents in Genoa or Alexandria, who insured cargoes and extended credit. The Consolat de Mar, Barcelona's commercial court founded in the thirteenth century and one of the earliest in Europe, administered the contracts these men made. Carrer de Mercaders ran through the district where they lived and worked.

Mercader derives from Latin mercator (from merx, goods), the same root that gives English merchant and market. The Catalan form has changed little in eight centuries. The street's position in the Born district, between the port and the Mercat de Santa Caterina, placed it at the junction of sea trade and local distribution — exactly where a merchant class would naturally anchor.

The palaces on nearby Carrer de Montcada, several of which are now museums, were built by the same class of people who did business on this street. Barcelona's fourteenth-century commercial empire stretched from the Black Sea to the Atlantic; this street was part of the infrastructure that made it run.
