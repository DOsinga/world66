The Barceloneta district was planned and built between 1753 and 1755 as a military engineering project, intended to house the sailors, fishermen, and labourers who had been displaced when the Bourbon forces demolished the Ribera neighbourhood in 1714 to build the Ciutadella fortress. The engineer Juan Martín Cermeño designed the grid: a series of identical blocks, each two stories high, each divided into identical dwellings of a single room depth, so that every apartment opened onto a street on one side and a back lane on the other. Carrer de Sant Carles is the main axis of this grid, running through the centre of the neighbourhood from north to south, and it was named for King Carlos III, in whose reign the district was completed.

The street still runs the length of the peninsula, and the grid around it is still recognisable as Cermeño's plan, although the buildings have grown — nineteenth-century additions pushed the houses from two stories to five or six, compressing the interior courtyards and light wells that the original plan assumed. The street is domestic and commercial in the way that Barceloneta has always been: fish restaurants, small shops, neighbourhood bars whose regulars are not tourists. The church of Sant Miquel del Port, built in 1755 and opening onto the Plaça de la Barceloneta, stands at the end of the main axis as Cermeño intended.

The 1754 plan was unusual in European terms: a purpose-built workers' housing district, rationally laid out, with uniform provision and public space incorporated from the start. It was also a military calculation — the district controlled access to the port and could be sealed off from the city proper by a gate. The grid was an instrument of order as much as welfare, and the inhabitants knew it. Carrer de Sant Carles runs through the middle of this ambiguity, a pleasant street in a neighbourhood that began as a managed enclosure.
