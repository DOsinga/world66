The street is narrow even by Gothic Quarter standards. The buildings lean slightly toward each other overhead. There are no market stalls, no tourist attractions announced. But the name on the wall — Carrer del Call — is one of the most historically weighted street names in Barcelona.

El Call was Barcelona's Jewish quarter from at least the 11th century. The word call itself comes from the Hebrew qahal, meaning community or assembly — the same root as the synagogue as a communal institution. The quarter occupied a roughly triangular area between the Cathedral, La Rambla, and the Roman wall, with Carrer del Call as its main axis. At its height in the 13th century, it was one of the most significant Jewish communities in the Iberian Peninsula, producing scholars, doctors, philosophers, and financiers who served the Aragonese crown.

In August 1391, the community was destroyed. A wave of anti-Jewish violence that had begun in Seville spread to Barcelona, and a mob attacked the Call. Most of the Jewish population of the city was killed, expelled, or forced to convert. The few survivors were gone within decades. The synagogues were converted to churches. The property was seized. Within a generation, there was no Jewish community left in Barcelona, but the name of the street remained, its etymology still carrying the word for community long after the community itself was gone. Walk the length of the street slowly; the ancient synagogue (on Carrer de Marlet, nearby) has been partially excavated and is open to visitors as a small museum.
