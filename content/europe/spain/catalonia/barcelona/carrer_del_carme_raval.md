Carrer del Carme is one of the main arteries of the Raval, running from the Rambla westward to the Hospital de la Santa Creu, Barcelona's great medieval hospital. The street takes its name from the Carmelite convent — the Convent del Carme — which from the 13th century occupied the substantial block on its northern side. The convent was one of the larger religious establishments in the Raval, and the street that ran beside it acquired both its name and its character from the institution.

When the Liberal government of Spain suppressed religious orders in 1835, the Carmelite convent was dissolved and the buildings seized. The convent church became a school — it is now the Escola Massana, Barcelona's art and design school. The rest of the convent complex was eventually demolished and developed, though the cloister garden survived and can still be entered. The suppression of 1835 was the event that remade the Raval's physical geography: five major convents disappeared within a decade, freeing up enormous tracts of land in what had been one of the most densely institutionalized quarters of any European city.

The street's straightness was not accidental. The Hospital de la Santa Creu, which has operated on this site since 1401 and is one of the oldest hospital complexes in Europe, required clear access for the stretchers and carts that brought in the sick and injured from across the city. Carrer del Carme was maintained as a corridor wide enough to allow this — an ambulance route, in the vocabulary of a city before ambulances existed. The hospital has since moved its clinical operations to the Hospital de Sant Pau, but the Gothic complex at the western end of Carrer del Carme now houses the Biblioteca de Catalunya.
