The Forn de la Calç (Lime Kiln) is a surviving industrial structure in the Les Corts neighbourhood, dating from the 19th century when the area was primarily an agricultural and light industrial zone on the outskirts of Barcelona. Lime kilns burned limestone to produce quicklime, which was an essential building material — used in plaster, mortar, and whitewash — for the expanding city nearby.

The Les Corts lime kiln operated during the period when the neighbourhood was producing the raw materials for Barcelona's construction boom. The Eixample expansion, which began in the 1860s, required enormous quantities of building materials: stone, brick, lime, and sand. The limestone deposits in the Collserola hills were processed in kilns like this one, in villages and suburban zones that are now entirely absorbed into the city.

The kiln is now a protected industrial heritage structure in a neighbourhood otherwise defined by the Camp Nou stadium and the commercial development that has grown around it. The relationship between a 19th-century lime kiln and the world's largest football stadium, 500 metres apart in the same neighbourhood, is one of the stranger juxtapositions of industrial and post-industrial Barcelona. The kiln represents the building industry that made the city; the stadium represents the entertainment economy that replaced it as the dominant feature of the neighbourhood's identity.
