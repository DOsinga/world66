The name Poble Sec (Dry Village) describes a practical geographic fact: the southern slope of Montjuïc, where the neighbourhood sits, had no natural water source. The springs that fed the rest of Barcelona's surrounding villages were on the northern and eastern faces of the Collserola and Montjuïc hills; the south-facing slope below Montjuïc was dry. When workers began settling here in the mid-19th century to be near the factories and port facilities along the Paral·lel, they built a neighbourhood where water had to be carried up from below.

Piped water eventually reached Poble Sec in the late 19th century, ending the practical problem that gave the neighbourhood its name. But the name persisted, as neighbourhood names do. The hillside character of the district — streets that rise steeply from the Paral·lel toward the Montjuïc cliff face — is still its defining physical feature. Some streets are too steep for cars; others end in staircases climbing directly up the hillside.

The neighbourhood's working-class identity survived longer than in some comparable Barcelona districts. The Avinguda del Paral·lel's entertainment economy and the factories of the Zona Franca kept the social composition relatively stable through the mid-20th century. The recent gentrification of Poble Sec — driven partly by the Mercat de les Flors dance venue, the Paral·lel theatre revivals, and proximity to Montjuïc — has changed the commercial character of the lower streets without yet transforming the upper part of the neighbourhood, which retains the steep, residential character of a working-class hillside settlement.
