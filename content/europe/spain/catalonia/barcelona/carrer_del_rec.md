The Rec Comtal was one of the great engineering achievements of medieval Barcelona. Constructed in the 10th or 11th century (sources differ on the exact date), it carried water from a weir on the River Besòs, some 7 kilometres to the northeast, into the city. The canal was not merely for drinking water: it powered the grain mills that fed Barcelona, the tanning workshops that produced leather, the paper mills, and the dyeing vats that processed cloth. The entire industrial economy of medieval Barcelona ran on the Rec.

The canal entered the city through what is now the Sant Pere neighbourhood and ran along the line of the present Carrer del Rec through what was then the garden district of La Ribera, before turning south toward the port. It remained in use, in diminishing form, until the late 19th century, when the city's water system was finally modernised.

The street that replaced it follows the canal's exact curve — a gentle bend that makes no sense in terms of the surrounding medieval grid but is entirely logical once you know you are walking along the ghost of a watercourse. Some of the buildings along the street still have the slightly raised threshold levels that reflect the canal bank. The name Rec (canal, in Catalan) is a reminder that the fashionable restaurant strip of the Born neighbourhood was once the industrial waterfront of a medieval city.
