Walk along Carrer del Rec Comtal and you are tracing a waterway that is a thousand years old. The canal — *rec* is Catalan for channel — was engineered around 977 AD under Count Mir of Barcelona to divert water from the Besòs river into the city. For five centuries it was the economic spine of the Raval and the Born: it drove flour mills, fulling mills for wool, and tanneries, and it fed the market gardens that provisioned a medieval city of tens of thousands.

The Rec Comtal ran for roughly eleven kilometres from the intake at Montcada to the sea, dropping enough elevation to power the mills by gravity alone. It was a feat of hydraulic engineering that predates the great cathedrals of the city. Medieval Barcelona's guilds — dyers, tanners, fullers — located themselves along its banks precisely because they needed running water. When the canal was finally covered and filled in the 18th and 19th centuries as the city modernised, the street above it inherited the name.

Fragments of the original stone channel have surfaced during building works in the Born district and are sometimes visible in basement excavations. The street today is quiet and residential, easy to miss among Barcelona's more spectacular medieval thoroughfares — which is part of what makes it worth noticing. The name alone is a compressed infrastructure history: *rec* (channel) + *comtal* (of the count).
