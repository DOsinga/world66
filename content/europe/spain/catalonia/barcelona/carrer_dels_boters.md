Cooper work was skilled and indispensable in a way that is easy to underestimate. A wine barrel had to be watertight, resilient enough to survive sea transport, and sized to standard measures for tax and customs purposes. The boters shaped oak staves, steamed them over fire until they curved, bound them with iron hoops, and sealed the joints — a craft that took years to master and days to complete per barrel. A city trading wine, olive oil, and salted fish across the Mediterranean needed hundreds of barrels a week.

The street sits in the Gothic Quarter near the old merchant district, close to the warehouses where goods were stored before shipment from the port. Proximity to the waterfront was practical: barrels were heavy and barrel-making produced enormous quantities of waste wood, so minimizing the distance between workshop and dock saved labor.

The guild system meant coopers lived, worked, and trained their apprentices in the same streets, forming dense trade communities whose collective identity now survives only in a street name and the faint outline of a neighborhood economy that has entirely disappeared.
