The flassaders were weavers of coarse woollen blankets, a staple trade in the medieval city. Their guild was established in this street in the 13th century, when the La Ribera neighbourhood was being developed as the commercial district of a rapidly expanding city. The street name records their presence even though no textile production has taken place here for several centuries.

What the blanket-weavers could not have anticipated is that their street would later contain the Casa de la Seca — the Royal Mint. The Seca (from the Arabic sikka, meaning the stamp used to mint coins) was the official mint of the Crown of Aragon and later of the Spanish monarchy in Catalonia. The building, constructed in the 16th century and substantially rebuilt in the 17th, produced the coins of several different monetary authorities over three centuries. It operated until 1851, when Barcelona's mint functions were consolidated in Madrid.

The Casa de la Seca was recently restored and is now a cultural centre and performance space. The restoration revealed remarkably intact remains of the mint's production facilities, including furnace bases and drainage channels for the metal-working processes. The street thus contains, in compressed form, two of the central activities of the pre-industrial economy: textile production and monetary circulation. Neither the blankets nor the coins are made here any longer, but the names persist.
