A medieval mirror was nothing like a modern one. Before the Venetians perfected the tin-mercury amalgam process in the sixteenth century, mirrors were polished metal — bronze, silver, or tin — ground to a reflective surface by hand. The mirallers who worked this street near the Born were specialist craftsmen producing luxury objects for wealthy households and ecclesiastical use. A good mirror cost more than most people earned in a month.

The Catalan word miraller derives from mirall (mirror), which comes from Vulgar Latin mirare, to look at. The same root gives English mirror, French miroir, and the verb admire — all connected to the act of gazing. That the street sits in the Ribera district, close to the port and the merchants who could afford such objects, is consistent with how medieval luxury trades positioned themselves: near their customers and near the supply chains that brought in raw materials.

The guild disappeared as mirror production industrialised and Venice monopolised the high-end market, then later as cheaper production spread across Europe. The street name remained as the only evidence that Barcelona once had a community of craftsmen skilled enough to turn sand into a surface that showed you yourself.
