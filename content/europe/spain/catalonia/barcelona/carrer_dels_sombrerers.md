In medieval Barcelona, the hat was not an accessory — it was a statement of rank, profession, and civic identity. The *sombrerers* (hat-makers) formed one of the city's recognised guilds, with their own ordinances, inspectors, and feast days. They concentrated here, near the waterfront and the great market church of Santa Maria del Mar, in the 13th and 14th centuries, when the neighbourhood was the commercial and artisanal heart of the city.

Hat-making in this period was skilled work. The best hats were felted from beaver or rabbit fur — materials that had to be imported — and the finishing required waterproofing, blocking, and dyeing. Guild regulations specified minimum standards and prohibited the sale of substandard goods; a hat-maker found selling mislabelled fur faced confiscation and fines. The guild also controlled who could practice the trade in the city, a form of monopoly that kept prices up and quality nominally consistent.

The street is short, quiet, and largely residential today, overshadowed by the splendour of Santa Maria del Mar a few streets away. But the specificity of the name is the point: medieval Barcelona organised its commercial life by trade, and the street names are the residue of that organisation. Walk through the Born and you will find the same pattern repeated — *Espaseria* (sword-makers), *Assaonadors* (leather-dressers), *Argenteria* (silversmiths). The city's medieval economy is mapped in the street signs.
