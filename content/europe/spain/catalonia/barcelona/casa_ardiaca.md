The building that houses Barcelona's City Archive is one of the most archaeologically dense in the old city. The foundations incorporate a section of the Roman wall that once enclosed Barcino — you can see the ancient masonry from inside the courtyard. A medieval Gothic structure was built on and around those walls in the 15th century for the Archdeacon of the Cathedral. Then, in 1902, the building was renovated and extended for the 1929 International Exposition, and the architect Lluís Domènech i Montaner added a detail so unexpected that it stops every visitor who notices it.

Look at the letterbox slot on the Carrer de la Pietat side of the building. Domènech carved it in Modernista style: three swallows swooping downward and, below them, a tortoise walking very slowly across the marble. The combination was not decorative whimsy. It was a comment on the legal profession — the swallows symbolizing the swift flight of justice, the tortoise the actual pace at which the courts operated. The building at the time housed offices of the Bar Association, and Domènech apparently felt strongly enough about lawyers' working methods to encode a joke about them in stone at the entrance.

Inside, the Gothic courtyard has a small fountain and an orange tree. The building is open during archive hours and entry is free. In the courtyard, the Roman wall section is visible through a glass panel in the floor — you can look down and see the stone courses of the 4th-century defensive wall that once protected the entire city.
