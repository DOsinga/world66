The building at La Rambla 82 stops people mid-stride. The upper floors are decorated with open Chinese parasols in cast iron, painted in red, yellow and green. On the corner, where the building meets Carrer de la Boqueria, a gilded Chinese dragon projects from the wall, one arm extended, holding a gas lamp. The whole thing is a piece of 19th-century commercial theatre: this was the shop of Bruno Cuadros, Barcelona's leading umbrella and parasol merchant, and the building was its own advertisement.

The architect was Josep Vilaseca, who redesigned the building in 1883, the year of Barcelona's first major international exhibition. The Japanese aesthetic was fashionable across Europe at the time — Japonisme had reached Barcelona just as it was reaching Paris and Vienna — and Cuadros seized on it for his trade. The parasols are not decorative; they are the product, displayed on the façade at a scale that made the building visible from a hundred metres away on the busiest street in the city. The dragon, added as the corner accent, became the detail that everyone remembered.

The building now houses a bank on the ground floor and various offices above. The parasols are still there; the dragon still holds its lamp, though the lamp is now electric. Look up from the pavement at the junction of La Rambla and Carrer de la Boqueria and you can see the whole programme: the Oriental fantasy pressed onto a Catalan corner building, the dragon watching the crowd with what passes for a smile, the parasols frozen at the moment of opening. Bruno Cuadros sold umbrellas here until the business moved on. The dragon stayed.
