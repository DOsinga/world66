In 1883, the umbrella merchant Bruno Cuadros hired the architect Josep Vilaseca to redesign his shop on the corner of Las Ramblas and Carrer de la Boqueria. Vilaseca was an eclectic architect working at the moment when Catalan culture was obsessed with Orientalism — Japanese art in particular had become fashionable throughout Europe following the opening of Japan in the 1850s, and the Barcelona *Modernisme* movement absorbed those influences enthusiastically.

The result was a building clad in decorative tiles and ironwork dragons, Japanese fans, and parasols — all logical advertising for an umbrella shop, but also a full-throated celebration of the ornamental excess that characterised commercial architecture of the period. The dragon gripping a lantern on the corner became one of the most photographed details on the entire length of Las Ramblas.

Cuadros's umbrella business eventually gave way to other tenants, and the ground floor is now occupied by a bank — a transition that has happened to a surprising number of Barcelona's most theatrically decorated commercial buildings. The irony of a building designed to sell objects for keeping dry now being used to keep money dry is one of those accidents of commerce that the city never bothered to correct.
