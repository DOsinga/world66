Francesc Cambó was the leader of the Lliga Regionalista, the conservative Catalan nationalist party, and for two decades in the early twentieth century he was one of the most influential politicians in Spain — serving as a minister in Madrid while simultaneously championing Catalan autonomy. His Gothic Quarter townhouse was renovated in the 1920s to serve as both a residence and a statement of cultural ambition: Cambó was an obsessive art collector who spent lavishly on Flemish primitives, Italian Renaissance panels, and Spanish Golden Age masters.

The collection he assembled is now split between the Museu Nacional d'Art de Catalunya and a foundation in Buenos Aires, following his somewhat complicated decision to support Franco during the Civil War — a choice that destroyed his political legacy and forced him into exile. The building itself, stripped of its collection, became an institutional space.

The house sits on Carrer de la Pietat, steps from the cathedral, in a neighbourhood dense with the layered political and architectural history of Catalan bourgeois power. Walking past it now, you are passing through the ghost of a political project that almost succeeded — Catalanism as a governing ideology within a pluralist Spain — before the Civil War foreclosed every moderate option.
