Before the municipality of Gràcia was absorbed into Barcelona in 1897, the area now covered by dense residential streets was a mixture of artisan workshops, market gardens, and masies — the traditional Catalan farmhouses that had worked the land above the city for centuries. Can Ferrer was one of these, a working agricultural property that found itself surrounded by urbanisation as the Eixample and Gràcia grew together.

The building survived not because anyone decided to preserve it but because the cost and complexity of demolition in a rapidly changing neighbourhood made it easier to leave. By the time the surrounding streets were fully built out, the farmhouse had acquired a kind of historical gravity through mere persistence. Its ruined state reflects decades of neglect by owners who neither developed nor maintained it.

The *can* in the name is a Catalan contraction of *ca en*, meaning "the house of" — the standard prefix for a family farmstead. Throughout the area that is now Barcelona's upper districts, dozens of *cans* once occupied the hillsides; Can Ferrer is among the very few that left any physical trace. The name is preserved in street names and references long after the building itself ceased to function.
