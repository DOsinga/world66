The Casa de la Misericòrdia was founded in 1583 as a charitable institution for the city's most vulnerable: orphaned children, abandoned women, and the elderly poor. It occupied a large block in what was then a dense, difficult quarter of El Raval, close to the Hospital de la Santa Creu and the other institutions the city placed on its margins — the mad, the sick, the destitute, gathered together away from the prosperous centre. The building was substantially rebuilt in the seventeenth century and expanded over the following two hundred years as the city's population grew and the institution's functions multiplied.

In the late twentieth century, the complex became the site of the Centre de Cultura Contemporània de Barcelona (CCCB), which opened in 1994 in a conversion that retained the original courtyard while adding a bold glass and steel façade along one side. The courtyard — long, colonnaded, with a formal dignity typical of Counter-Reformation institutional architecture — is now an outdoor event space, used for cinema screenings, concerts, and festivals. The contrast between the seventeenth-century stonework and the reflective glass wall, which mirrors the courtyard back at itself while opening views to the city beyond, became one of the defining images of Barcelona's 1990s urban reinvention.

The CCCB sits alongside the Museu d'Art Contemporani de Barcelona (MACBA), on a square that did not exist before the institution's buildings were cleared. Together they form the cultural district that replaced the worst of Raval's overcrowded housing — a transformation that was also a displacement, the poor moved on to make room for the arts. The Casa de la Misericòrdia's history, caring for those displaced by poverty, gives its transformation a particular edge that the institution's brochures tend not to dwell on.
