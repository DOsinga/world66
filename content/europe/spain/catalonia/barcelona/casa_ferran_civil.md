Casa Ferran-Civil stands at a point in the Gothic Quarter where you can notice — if you pay attention to the angles — that the streets do not line up cleanly. The Roman city of Barcino laid out its grid on one orientation; the medieval city that grew up around and over it had a slightly different north. Where the two systems met, the streets kink and the building plots take on irregular shapes.

The building itself dates from the late Gothic and early Renaissance period, with subsequent modifications that added the kind of layered, patchwork character common to properties that have served dozens of functions over five centuries. The Ferran and Civil families who gave it its name were among the merchant elite who occupied this quarter when it was still the administrative and commercial heart of the city.

What makes the location remarkable is how much of that collision of civilisations is still readable at street level. Walk along the wall of Casa Ferran-Civil and then look at the building beside it: the angle changes. The Romans are still arguing with the medieval mapmakers, and the building sits at the fault line.
