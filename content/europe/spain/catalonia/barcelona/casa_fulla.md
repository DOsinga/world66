To understand what Clotet and Tusquets were doing in 1966, you have to know what the available positions were. Official architecture under Franco favoured a stripped vernacular — regional materials, pitched roofs, the visual vocabulary of an imagined rural Spain. The progressive counter-position was orthodox functionalism: the International Style, rationalist grids, no ornament. Studio PER rejected both. Casa Fullà mixes brick with white render, applies decorative elements that reference historical architecture without quoting it directly, and treats the street facade as a deliberate composition — which was, in the mid-1960s, a mildly subversive act.

The building predates the international postmodern turn by nearly a decade. When Robert Venturi published Complexity and Contradiction in 1966 — the same year Casa Fullà was completed — he was articulating a critique that Clotet and Tusquets had already built. The Barcelona school's countercultural architecture developed in relative isolation from the American and Italian debates, shaped more by its fraught political context than by theoretical manifestos. Casa Fullà belongs to that tradition: architecture as argument conducted through materials and form.

Clotet and Tusquets would become central figures in Barcelona's design culture over the following decades — Tusquets as architect, product designer, and writer; Clotet as a rigorous and somewhat quieter presence. Casa Fullà is an early statement from a partnership that would define Catalan architecture's transition out of the Franco period and into the democratic city that hosted the 1992 Olympics.
