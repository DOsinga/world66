Josep Martí i Fàbregas commissioned this building in the first years of the twentieth century, at the height of Barcelona's *Modernisme* boom, when the city's prosperous middle class was competing to outdo each other in architectural ambition. The architect Jeroni Granell i Manresa was a specialist in precisely this kind of work: elaborate decorative facades for clients who wanted to announce their arrival in the new bourgeois Barcelona without necessarily being famous enough to be remembered by history.

Granell worked extensively in the Eixample and the adjacent transitional zones between the old city and the new, producing buildings that synthesised the Modernista vocabulary — curved iron, ceramic tile, floral ornament — into a reliable commercial formula. Casa Josep Martí i Fàbregas is among his more successful examples, with ironwork balconies that show genuine care for the detail work.

Almost nothing is recorded about the client himself, which is historically common for the owners of mid-tier Modernista buildings. The great houses — Batlló, Milà, Lleó Morera — survive in history because their owners were powerful enough to leave records. The many hundreds of Barcelona buildings built to the same cultural programme by less prominent families have been preserved largely by chance and the city's general commitment to its architectural heritage.
