The detective Pepe Carvalho is fictional; the restaurant where he ate was real. Manuel Vázquez Montalbán, who wrote twenty-odd Carvalho novels between 1972 and his death in 2003, ate at Casa Leopoldo himself, and the restaurant at Carrer de Sant Rafael 24 appears in the novels not as a thinly veiled composite but as the actual place, with its actual menu. The Carvalho series is one of the most sustained literary portraits of Barcelona's underworld and working-class culture in the 20th century, and Casa Leopoldo is at the centre of it.

The restaurant opened in 1929 under the Gil family and served Catalan cooking — heavy on seafood, traditional preparations, nothing fashionable — for three generations. The Raval of those decades was the neighbourhood Vázquez Montalbán was mapping: dense, poor, multilingual, dangerous in parts, and entirely unlike the tourist circuit running parallel to it along the Rambla. Casa Leopoldo was where that neighbourhood's working-class and bohemian cultures met over a set lunch.

It closed in 2013. The building remains, and the address is noted by anyone who has read the Carvalho novels, but the kitchen is not operating. The closure was reported internationally as a cultural loss, which is accurate as far as it goes — but the neighbourhood the restaurant represented had already been substantially replaced by the time the kitchen went cold.
