Josep Puig i Cadafalch was the third figure in the triumvirate of Catalan Modernisme, alongside Domènech i Montaner and Gaudí. The Casa Macaya, built in 1901 for the industrialist Joan Macaya, represents his most developed domestic statement. Where Domènech i Montaner used brick and ceramics, and Gaudí used stone and parabolic arches, Puig i Cadafalch turned to the white stucco of Catalan Gothic secular architecture — the palaces and guildhalls of the late medieval period — inflected with Northern European Gothic and Renaissance details learned from his study of Dutch and Belgian architecture.

The façade is arresting: white stucco over a brick structure, with Gothic pointed arches, Renaissance medallions, and floral sculpture by Eusebi Arnau (whose chisels were seemingly everywhere in Barcelona at the turn of the century) deployed across the surface. Bicycles appear among the carved ornaments — the client was an enthusiast, and the architect was happy to oblige. It is a building that wears its moment very precisely.

The courtyard, reached through the entrance gate, is the real achievement: a Gothic arcade at ground level supporting an open gallery above, with a staircase of considerable elegance connecting them. The building now houses the Social Obra Catalunya programme of the La Caixa Foundation; the courtyard is accessible during office hours. Puig i Cadafalch later had a political career — he was President of the Mancomunitat de Catalunya, the first Catalan regional government, from 1917 to 1924 — and was forced into exile after Franco's victory.
