Gaudí moved into this house in 1906, when he was already one of the most famous architects in Barcelona, and he lived here until 1925 — the last year of his life, when he relocated to sleep in his workshop at the Sagrada Família, apparently too absorbed in the cathedral to bother with a home. The house was not designed by Gaudí; it was built in 1904 by his collaborator Francesc Berenguer as a show home for the Park Güell development, which Eusebi Güell had hoped would attract wealthy residents. Almost none came.

The museum inside contains furniture, personal objects, and devotional items that give a rare glimpse of Gaudí's private life — a life that was, by most accounts, austere and intensely religious. He was a Franciscan tertiary, fasted frequently, and increasingly dressed with a simplicity that struck contemporaries as eccentric given his professional fame. The contrast between the flamboyant architectural ego visible in his buildings and the ascetic private man is one of the unresolved tensions in his biography.

The house also functions as a strategic viewpoint within Park Güell, offering views down toward the city and up toward the unfinished development's terraces. From here you can understand what Güell and Gaudí were attempting: a garden city for the Barcelona elite, conceived just as that elite was deciding it preferred the Eixample grid.
