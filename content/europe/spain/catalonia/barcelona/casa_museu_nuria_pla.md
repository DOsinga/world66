The building was constructed in 1910 as the Institut Ravetllat-Pla, named for the veterinarian Jaume Ravetllat and the physician Ramon Pla i Armengol, who collaborated on heterodox tuberculosis research at a time when the disease was killing vast numbers of Barcelonans. Their work — involving theories about the relationship between tuberculosis and blood chemistry — was controversial within the medical establishment and ultimately not validated, but the institute they established occupied this building for decades.

The name Pla creates a superficial connection to the artist Núria Pla, whose work is now displayed here — but the physicist and the painter share only a surname. The transformation of a scientific institute into an art museum is itself a form of institutional archaeology: the building was repurposed because it was available and historically significant, not because of any thematic link between its former and current occupants.

The Gràcia neighbourhood in which it sits was an independent municipality until 1897, with its own political culture, its own festivals (the Festa Major de Gràcia is one of the city's great street celebrations), and its own architectural character distinct from the Eixample grid pressed against it. The institute's location reflects Gràcia's tradition of housing progressive intellectual and scientific projects on the city's edge.
