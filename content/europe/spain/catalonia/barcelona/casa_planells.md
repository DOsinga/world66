Most visitors to Barcelona know the bench at Parc Güell without knowing who made it. The sinuous, tile-encrusted serpentine terrace is universally attributed to Gaudí but was designed almost entirely by Josep Maria Jujol, who also executed the swirling polychrome ceiling of Casa Batlló's main hall. Jujol was Gaudí's most gifted collaborator, and for much of his career his own work was obscured by the association. Casa Planells, built three years after Gaudí's death, is his attempt to carry the organic impulse forward independently.

The resemblance is unmistakable. The facade curves at the corners, the balconies ripple rather than project in straight lines, and the building's outline from across the street has the same restless silhouette as Gaudí's apartment blocks. But the structural ambition is different — Jujol worked with conventional masonry rather than parabolic arches and hyperbolic vaults, and the effect is more decorative than tectonic. The building does not engineer its way out of Euclidean geometry; it simply refuses to acknowledge right angles on its public face.

The address adds a layer that no architect could have planned. Avinguda de Gaudí was renamed in honour of the master after his death, and Jujol's building now stands on a street dedicated to the man he spent his career alongside. Whether that reads as tribute or as the long shadow of a collaborator finally working in his own name is a question the building does not answer.
