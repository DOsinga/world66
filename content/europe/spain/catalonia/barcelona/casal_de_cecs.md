The Carrer de l'Hospital (Hospital Street) in the Raval takes its name from the Hospital de la Santa Creu, the main charitable medical institution of medieval Barcelona, which has occupied the block on the north side of the street since 1401. But the hospital was not the only welfare institution in the area. A cluster of related establishments occupied the surrounding streets: a hospice for pilgrims, a home for the elderly poor, an asylum for the mentally ill (the Casa dels Folls, one of the first psychiatric hospitals in Europe, founded 1401), and the Casa dels Cecs — the house for the blind.

The Casa dels Cecs provided accommodation and basic vocational training for people who had lost their sight, often through the industrial and military accidents that were common in the pre-modern city. Blindness was not rare in an era when eye infections went untreated and metalworking produced flying fragments without protective equipment. The casa taught blind residents to make rope, baskets, and simple textiles — trades that could be practised without sight.

The building that housed the Casa dels Cecs has been rebuilt several times, and no medieval fabric survives in its current form. What survives is the institutional memory: the street pattern around the Hospital de la Santa Creu reflects the layout of a medieval welfare complex that was one of the most comprehensive in Europe. Walking the Carrer de l'Hospital and its adjacent lanes means walking through the footprint of a system designed to manage the bodies that the city's economy had broken.
