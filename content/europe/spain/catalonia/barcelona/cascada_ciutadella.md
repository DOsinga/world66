The Cascada — the great waterfall and fountain at the far end of the Parc de la Ciutadella — was designed for the Universal Exhibition of 1888. It is a theatrical piece of landscape architecture: a massive rocky artificial cliff drops from a Baroque-inspired upper terrace, water tumbles down in several falls, and at the centre stands a bronze group of Aurora's chariot rising from the sea. The scale is romantic rather than rational, borrowed from French and Italian garden traditions and rendered in Spanish stone.

The lead architect was Josep Fontseré, who won the design competition for the park layout in 1872. He hired several young architecture students to assist with the project. One of them was Antoni Gaudí, then completing his degree at the Barcelona School of Architecture. The exact extent of Gaudí's contribution to the Cascada is debated — some historians attribute the rocky artificial cliff and the formal composition to him; others limit his role to technical assistance and draughtsmanship. What is documented is that he worked on it in 1876, when he was 24 years old.

If any of the Cascada's sculptural intuition — the naturalistic piled rocks, the sense of geological formation — comes from the young Gaudí, then this is the earliest surviving example of his influence on Barcelona's public space. The fountain has been restored several times and now operates during park hours. The lake in front of it can be crossed by rented rowboats. Go on a weekday morning when the park is quiet and the water is running; the theatrical intent of the whole composition reads better without a crowd.
