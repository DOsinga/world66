The name is borrowed from a comedy — a zarzuela called Les tres bessones (The Three Twins) that was running in Barcelona at the time of the exhibition — but the architecture is entirely serious. Lluís Domènech i Montaner designed the building in 1888 as a deliberate manifesto against academic historicism: the iron frame is left exposed inside, the brick is left unplastered, and the decorative programme uses medieval Catalan motifs rather than the Renaissance and classical vocabulary then fashionable for prestige buildings. It was among the first statements of what would become the Modernisme movement.

After the exhibition closed, the building's fate took an unexpected turn. The naturalists of the Natural Sciences Museum moved in, using its spacious iron-framed interiors as workshops for taxidermy, specimen preparation, and scientific illustration. For decades it was less a showpiece than a workshop — filled with animal skins, geological samples, and classification tables — before eventually becoming the museum's main public building. The scientific collections have since moved to the Museu Blau at the Forum site, leaving the Castell dels Tres Dragons largely empty and awaiting a new use.

From the outside, the crenellated roofline and circular corner towers read as a toy fortress from a distance, particularly from across the Ciutadella lake. Up close, the structural logic becomes clear: every decorative element is a rationalised version of something structural, and the ceramic panels and iron grilles of the façade were produced by craftsmen whom Domènech had brought to work on site specifically to revive techniques that had lapsed in Catalonia.
