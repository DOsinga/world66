The geese are the first thing most people ask about. They live in the cloister of the Cathedral of the Holy Cross and Saint Eulàlia, tended by cathedral staff, and their number is fixed at thirteen — one for each year of Eulàlia's life. According to tradition, the young girl was subjected to thirteen tortures by the Roman governor Dacianus before she died, sometime around 303 AD. Whether Eulàlia was a real historical person or a pious legend assembled from fragments of other martyrologies is debated by hagiographers; what is not debated is that her cult was established in Barcelona by at least the 7th century, and that the cathedral built in her honour became one of the great Gothic churches of the Iberian Peninsula.

Construction of the present cathedral began in 1298 on the site of a Romanesque church, which itself stood over a Visigothic basilica and, before that, a Roman temple. The Gothic structure took until 1450 to complete in its essential form — nave, transept, crypt — making it a century and a half of continuous building. The elaborate façade visible today, with its spires and rose window, is not medieval at all: it was designed by Carlès Galtés in 1408 but not built until 1882 to 1913, funded by a banker named Manuel Girona. Medieval visitors would have seen a plain stone front where tourists now photograph pinnacles.

Eulàlia's remains are kept in an alabaster sarcophagus in the crypt, carved in 1327 by Lupo di Francesco, a Pisan sculptor. The crypt is accessible from the main nave and is one of the most visited spaces in the cathedral. The cloister, built between the 13th and 15th centuries, is among the best-preserved in Catalonia: its garden of magnolias and palm trees, surrounded by Gothic arches, functions as a quiet counterweight to the noise of the Barri Gòtic outside. The geese have been there — or their predecessors have — since at least the medieval period.
