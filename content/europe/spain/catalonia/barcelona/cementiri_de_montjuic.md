Opened in 1883 on the slopes below the Montjuïc fortress, the Montjuïc cemetery became the principal burial ground for Barcelona's industrial bourgeoisie at the height of their wealth and ambition. The mausoleums they commissioned are on a different scale from northern European cemetery architecture: entire neoclassical temples with Ionic columns, neo-Gothic chapels in dressed stone, Art Nouveau vaults with stained glass and ceramic ornament. The families of textile manufacturers, bankers, and merchants who funded the Eixample and the World Exhibitions are commemorated here in stone that was clearly intended to outlast memory itself.

The same hillside contains mass graves from the Civil War and the postwar repression. The Barcelona working class and the political left that the industrial families had feared and occasionally employed are buried here without markers, in the same cemetery where the bourgeoisie built temples. The Fossar de la Pedrera, a section of the cemetery, holds thousands of victims of Francoist executions, including Lluís Companys, president of the Generalitat, who was shot at the fortress above in 1940. The topography of the cemetery is a compressed map of Barcelona's class and political history.

Among the named tombs is that of Francisco Franco's family — an arrangement that generates periodic political controversy and has prompted calls for exhumation and relocation that have not so far been acted upon. The cemetery is fully open to visitors and is one of the more architecturally serious open-air collections of 19th-century funerary art in southern Europe.
