The cemetery was opened in 1775 outside the city walls, on ground that was then well beyond the eastern edge of the built city. Known officially as the Cementiri de l'Est, it is Barcelona's oldest functioning cemetery and was established partly for sanitary reasons — the authorities wanted burials removed from the churchyards inside the city walls. In the 19th century, as Poblenou industrialised around it, the cemetery became the burial ground for the same working population that staffed the surrounding factories, while also attracting the patronage of families wealthy enough to commission elaborate monuments.

The funerary sculpture here is among the finest of its period in Spain: marble weeping angels with realistically carved drapery, broken columns symbolising a life cut short, draped urns in the neoclassical manner, and portrait medallions set into tomb facades. The quality reflects both the skill of Barcelona's 19th-century stonemasons and the competitive anxiety of the families who commissioned the work. Joan Maragall, the Catalan poet, set a well-known short story (*La fi d'en Patufet* is sometimes cited, though the most associated piece is his prose on the cemetery itself) in this place, and the cemetery has a literary presence in Catalan culture that the Montjuïc cemetery, despite its grander monuments, does not quite match.

The *aplec dels morts* — the gathering of the dead — is a local custom observed on All Saints' Day (1 November) in which families bring food and spend time at the gravesides of their relatives. The practice is neither solemn nor macabre in its local execution: it is closer to a picnic with a specific guest list, an annual acknowledgement of continuity between the living and the dead that has persisted through changes in religious practice and urban character. The cemetery is open daily and receives visitors throughout the year.
