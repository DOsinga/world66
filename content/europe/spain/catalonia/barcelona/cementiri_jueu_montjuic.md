The hill of Montjuïc takes its name from the Jews who buried their dead here: "Mons Judaic," the Hill of the Jews. From at least the tenth century until the expulsion of 1492, Barcelona's Jewish community maintained its cemetery on the southern slope of the hill, accessible from the city below by a track that is now approximately the route of Carrer de la Font Honrada. The cemetery was by far the largest Jewish burial ground in the Iberian Peninsula, corresponding to a community that numbered several thousand at its height.

After the expulsion, the site was progressively built over, though the depth of accumulated burials — several metres in places — meant that construction work consistently encountered tombstones and skeletal remains. In the twentieth century, road construction on the Montjuïc slope repeatedly exposed additional graves. Some of the recovered tombstones, with their Hebrew inscriptions, are preserved in the Museu d'Història de Barcelona's lapidary collection; others were scattered or lost. Estimates of the cemetery's extent suggest it covered several hectares of the hillside.

No marked cemetery survives; the site is now a slope of road infrastructure, gardens, and the grounds of the castle above. The Associació Call de Barcelona has advocated for a permanent memorial marker on the site, and a small commemorative plaque was installed in the early 2000s, but the scale of the cemetery and the significance of the site are not fully reflected in what is visible there today.
