Ildefons Cerdà i Sunyer died in 1876 in Caldas de Besaya, Spain, aged 64, virtually bankrupt. The plan he had created in 1859 for the expansion of Barcelona — the Eixample grid that would come to define the city — had been accepted by the Spanish central government over the objections of the Barcelona city council, which had preferred a different design. But accepting the plan had not meant rewarding its author. The construction of the Eixample proceeded according to Cerdà's grid but deviated from his social programme: the interior block gardens he had intended as communal space were built over by private developers, and the mixed-use, mixed-class neighbourhood he had planned became a bourgeois enclave.

Cerdà had invented the word urbanisme (urbanism) and written the first systematic theoretical treatise on urban planning — the General Theory of Urbanisation (1867) — but the commercial development of his plan had left him without the resources to maintain his own household. He died away from Barcelona, in a town in the Cantabrian mountains where he had gone for his health.

His grave in the Cementiri de Montjuïc is marked with a monument that acknowledges the scale of his achievement — a recognition that came after his death, as the Eixample he designed became one of the most celebrated urban plans in European history. The grid he drew covers approximately 7.5 square kilometres and houses several hundred thousand people. His grave is in the cemetery on the hill that would be developed for the 1929 Exposition — another posthumous transformation of a Barcelona landscape he did not live to see.
