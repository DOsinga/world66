The Cementiri de Poblenou — often called the Cementiri del Poblenou or the Cementiri Vell (Old Cemetery) — was opened in 1775 and is among the oldest purpose-built cemeteries in Spain. Before its creation, the dead of Barcelona were buried in church floors and the small yards attached to parish churches, a practice that had become a public health problem in a city with a rapidly growing population and a tradition of opening graves before the previous occupant had fully decomposed.

The Enlightenment brought a new view of burial: the dead should be separated from the living, placed outside the city in dedicated spaces where decomposition could take place without contaminating the urban water supply or the air. The Cementiri de Poblenou was the direct result of this medical and philosophical shift. It was built outside the city limits, in the then-rural zone east of Barcelona, in a Neoclassical style that treated death as a civic rather than purely religious matter.

The cemetery's tombs include some of the finest Neoclassical and 19th-century funerary sculpture in Catalonia. Several of the mausoleums are elaborate Modernista structures built for wealthy industrial families in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The kiss of death — a white marble young man being embraced by a skeletal figure — is the most photographed tomb, carved in 1930 by the sculptor Jaume Barba for the Llorens family. Barcelona's contemporary art scene discovered the cemetery in the 1990s as a venue for summer concerts, a decision that sits uncomfortably with the solemnity of the subject but has brought new audiences.
