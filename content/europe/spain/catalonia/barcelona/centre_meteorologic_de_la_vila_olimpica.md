Siza was invited to Barcelona as one of four internationally prominent architects — alongside Rafael Moneo, Vittorio Gregotti, and Oriol Bohigas — to design public buildings in the Vila Olímpica, the new district built on former industrial land between the old city and the sea for the 1992 Olympics. Most visitors who walk through the area are looking at the beach, the port, Frank Gehry's copper fish, or the towers. Almost none stop at the weather station.

The building is small, its programme modest — meteorological monitoring equipment and associated offices — but Siza treated it with the same formal rigour he brought to the Galician Centre of Contemporary Art in Santiago de Compostela or the Faculty of Architecture in Porto. The plan is organised around a curved white-render wall that wraps and compresses the interior volumes, the curved edge contrasting with the orthogonal geometry of the surrounding Olympic street grid. Every corner and threshold is resolved with the kind of precision that only makes sense once you've seen Siza's drawings.

Siza won the Pritzker in 1992, the same year the building opened. Portugal's position as a relatively small and architecturally peripheral country means his work is less routinely cited than comparably recognised figures; this building compounds that obscurity by having a function that generates no visitors of its own. It sits on the Carrer de la Marina, white and quietly insistent, doing its job.
