The Cercle Artistic de Sant Lluc was born in 1893 from a split in Barcelona's art world. When the progressive Cercle de Sant Joan Baptista leaned too secular for some tastes, a group of devout artists broke away and established a new society under the patronage of Saint Luke — the evangelist tradition credited as the first Christian painter. The founding members wanted to prove that Catholic faith and serious artistic ambition were not mutually exclusive.

What makes the Cercle surprising is who actually joined. Antoni Gaudí was a devoted early member, as was the sculptor Josep Llimona. Later generations brought in Salvador Dalí and Joan Miró — artists whose work became anything but conventionally pious. The Cercle's drawing classes were famously rigorous, and for decades the building on Carrer dels Àngels served as one of Barcelona's most important spaces for artistic education and debate.

The plaque on the building marks a piece of the cultural geography that the Eixample's grid tends to obscure: that the city's modernista ferment happened in overlapping, often competing circles, and that the tension between religious tradition and avant-garde experiment was never simply resolved — it was productive.
