The Parc de Cervantes in the Pedralbes district is planted with one of Europe's largest rose collections, and visitors who come for the flowers sometimes overlook the pair of bronze deer that stand near the entrance. The Catalan word *cèrvols* means deer, and the park's name carries a double meaning — Cervantes (the author of Don Quixote) and *cervos* (the Latin root for deer) converge in a piece of toponymic coincidence the sculptor seems to have enjoyed.

The figures were placed here as part of Barcelona's ongoing programme of putting public sculpture into its parks, a tradition that stretches back to the 19th century and intensified dramatically in the run-up to the 1992 Olympics. Deer had appeared in Catalan heraldry and civic imagery for centuries, lending the piece a rooted quality even as the surrounding park was relatively recent.

What makes *Cèrvols* worth pausing for is less the sculpture itself than the relationship between name, animal, and place. The deer stand in a park that might as well have been named after them — and in a city that has always been attentive to the layering of meanings in public space, that kind of convergence is never entirely accidental.
