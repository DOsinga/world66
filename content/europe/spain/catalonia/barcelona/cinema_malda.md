Spain's post-war dubbing culture was not purely a commercial decision. The Franco regime imposed dubbing partly to suppress regional languages, since a film shown in the original version could contain Catalan or Basque dialogue that the censors could not easily control. The Maldà's insistence on original-version screenings has roots in that history, and the practice became part of its identity even after the political stakes diminished.

The cinema survived the wave of closures that followed television in the 1960s and 1970s and the multiplex expansion of the 1980s and 1990s by having low overheads, an unusual specialisation, and a loyal audience that valued its stubbornness. It has never been extensively renovated; the interior remains cramped, the projection equipment is updated by necessity rather than ambition, and the location inside the Galeries Maldà — a small arcade running between Carrer del Pi and the lanes of the Gothic Quarter — ensures it is never quite on the way to anywhere.

The programming is independent and international, weighted toward films that would otherwise reach Barcelona only through occasional festival screenings. Audiences who find it tend to become regulars; audiences who expect stadium seating and cup holders do not return. The Maldà is not interested in either outcome.
