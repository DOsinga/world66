The Monestir de Jonqueres was one of medieval Barcelona's great convents, home to a community of nuns from the 13th century until Spain's Liberal Triennium and subsequent church suppressions made monastic life untenable in the 1870s. When the nuns finally departed, the question of what to do with an ancient Gothic cloister in the middle of a city undergoing radical modernisation had no obvious answer. Demolition was the default.

Instead, the cloister was taken apart and moved. Each carved capital, each stone arch was numbered and transported across the city to be incorporated into the newly built Church of the Conception on the Eixample grid. The exercise required a kind of institutional patience that is rare in any era: the Eixample was being built at speed, driven by the logic of the modern city, yet here architects paused to carry the medieval past with them.

Walking into the cloister today, the transplant is seamless enough to fool you into thinking it always belonged here. The Gothic arcades sit around a small garden in the church's interior, entirely calm. The story is only visible if you know to look for it — which is precisely the point of stopping.
