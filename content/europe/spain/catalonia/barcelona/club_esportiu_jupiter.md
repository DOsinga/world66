Club Esportiu Júpiter emerged from the factories and workshops of Poblenou, Barcelona's industrial quarter, at a moment when football was spreading through Spain's working class with extraordinary speed. Founded in 1909, the club drew its membership from the neighbourhood's labourers and artisans, and that identity — proudly proletarian, fiercely Catalan — would define it through some of the most violent decades of the 20th century.

During the Spanish Civil War, Júpiter's facilities were requisitioned by Republican forces. After Franco's victory, the club faced the same pressures as every Catalan institution: name changes, language suppression, surveillance. That it survived at all, let alone maintained its neighbourhood roots and Catalan identity through four decades of dictatorship, is the real story the memorial marks.

The plaque doesn't celebrate trophies or famous players. It honours the fact of persistence — the obstinate decision of a working-class sports club to keep meeting, keep playing, and keep existing as a specifically Catalan institution when the political cost of doing so was real.
