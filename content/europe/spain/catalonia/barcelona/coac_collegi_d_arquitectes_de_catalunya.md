The building itself is unremarkable — a clean rationalist grid of the kind that professional bodies were commissioning across Europe in the early 1960s. What makes it one of the most photographed facades in the Gothic Quarter is its skin: three horizontal friezes of sgrafitto depicting gegants (parade giants), sardana dancers, and children at play — images from Catalan folk culture executed at a scale that turns the building into a public artwork. Picasso drew the designs; the Norwegian artist Carl Nesjar transferred them to the concrete using a sandblasting technique he had developed, cutting through a dark outer layer to reveal a lighter substrate beneath.

Picasso's involvement was itself a political act. He had left Spain in 1934, opposed Franco publicly and permanently, and refused to allow Guernica to be returned to Spain while the dictatorship remained in power. Agreeing to contribute to a building in Barcelona — and choosing to depict specifically Catalan cultural imagery — was a statement made from exile. The designs were drawn in 1960, two years before the building opened; Picasso never returned to see the result.

The building sits across the street from the Cathedral, which means Picasso's sardana dancers occupy one of the most loaded visual confrontations in the city: medieval Catalan Gothic on one side, a modernist professional headquarters on the other, the space between them crossed by tourists who often photograph the friezes without knowing their history. The Col·legi's ground floor is open to the public; the bookshop carries architecture titles in Catalan, Spanish, and English.
