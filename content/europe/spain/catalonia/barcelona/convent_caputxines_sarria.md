In March 1966, approximately 130 intellectuals, academics, writers, and students gathered in the Convent de les Caputxines in Sarrià for what was announced as a constituent assembly for a democratic student union. The gathering was illegal under Franco's dictatorship. When police arrived to break it up, the participants refused to leave. They remained barricaded inside the convent for several days, with police surrounding it. The convent's status as a religious building created a diplomatic complication: entering by force would mean violating an ecclesiastical space.

The event — known as la Caputxinada — became the first significant collective public act of cultural resistance to Franco by Catalonia's intellectual class. Among those present were the philosopher and historian Jordi Solé Tura, the poet Salvador Espriu, the novelists Maria Aurèlia Capmany and Manuel Vázquez Montalbán, and the economists who would later design Catalonia's autonomy statute. The gathering produced a manifesto calling for democratic freedoms and Catalan cultural rights.

When the participants eventually left the convent, most were briefly arrested and fined. The event galvanised a generation of Catalan cultural and political activism. A commemorative plaque at the convent, installed in 2016 on the fiftieth anniversary, records the names of those present. The convent remains an active religious house; the plaque is on its exterior wall, visible from the street. In a dictatorship where no public assembly was permitted, a convent gate and the protection of ecclesiastical space gave Catalonia's intellectuals the one room they could speak in.
