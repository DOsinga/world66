The original Dama d'Elx is a polychrome stone bust discovered in 1897 near the town of Elche, in Valencia. Carved by Iberian craftspeople sometime in the 5th or 4th century BC, it shows a woman wearing elaborate jewellery and headdress, and it remains the most technically accomplished piece of pre-Roman Iberian sculpture ever found. Within days of its discovery, a French archaeologist purchased it and shipped it to Paris — where it entered the Louvre and stayed for over four decades.

The bust returned to Spain only in 1941, after a diplomatic exchange negotiated between Franco and Pétain's Vichy France. The swap involved Spanish manuscripts that the Spanish crown had once donated to France; the Dama came back as part of a wartime deal between two authoritarian governments, which is not the triumphant homecoming the story might otherwise suggest. It now lives in the Museo Arqueológico Nacional in Madrid.

The copy in Barcelona serves as an ambassador for the original, and for the broader Iberian culture that produced it — a civilisation that flourished across what is now Spain and Portugal before Roman conquest, leaving behind art that is neither Greek nor Roman but distinctly its own.
