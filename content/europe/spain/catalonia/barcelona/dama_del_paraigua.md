Josep Reynés created *La Dama del Paraigua* — the Lady with the Umbrella — in 1884, and the figure has occupied the same corner of the Parc de la Ciutadella ever since. She stands at the edge of a small pond, umbrella raised, and has accumulated the kind of affectionate mythology that only very old and very familiar public artworks acquire. One persistent story pairs her romantically with the male figure from the Cascada monumental fountain across the park, though the two sculptures were made by different artists a decade apart.

The umbrella is the detail that fixes her. Umbrellas in 19th-century sculpture were almost always attributes of the bourgeoisie — markers of respectability and protection from the elements — but in Reynés's hands the accessory reads as slightly comic, even whimsical. The figure doesn't pose heroically; she simply stands there, sensibly dressed for Catalan weather.

The Ciutadella park was laid out on the site of a hated military fortress demolished in 1869, and its sculptures accumulated gradually as Barcelona's artists used the new public space as an outdoor gallery. The *Dama del Paraigua* was among the first permanent residents, and she has outlasted several generations of more grandiose neighbours.
