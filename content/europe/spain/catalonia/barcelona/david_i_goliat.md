Antoni Llena is one of the central figures of Catalan conceptual art, and his reading of the David and Goliath story is characteristically perverse. The traditional interpretation celebrates the small against the large, the underdog's cunning triumph over brute force. Llena's version, installed in 1990 near the Olympic Village, refuses that comfort. His Goliath is not a monster but a figure with his own interior life; the stone mid-flight is not triumphant but terrible.

The work was created in the years immediately before the 1992 Olympics, when Barcelona was engaged in massive urban transformations that displaced communities and erased neighbourhoods to make way for the Games. Whether Llena intended the allegory to be read in that context is less important than the fact that it can be. Who is David and who is Goliath in a city being remade by public and private power for global spectacle?

The sculpture sits in the landscape of the new Olympic neighbourhood as a kind of counter-narrative — officially commissioned, publicly funded, yet asking questions that the celebration of the Games was not designed to accommodate. That is precisely the kind of ambiguity that good public art is capable of sustaining.
