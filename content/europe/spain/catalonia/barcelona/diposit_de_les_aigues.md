The Eixample expansion, designed by Ildefons Cerdà from 1860 onwards, was the largest urban engineering project in 19th-century Spain — but it required infrastructure that the old city could not supply. The Dipòsit de les Aigües was built to solve one specific part of that problem: drinking water. Josep Fontserè, the engineer who also designed the monumental cascade in the Ciutadella park, constructed a vast underground cistern here at the park's edge, capable of holding millions of litres behind its brick arched vaults.

The cistern's function was straightforward and its design austere — rows of brick columns supporting barrel vaults, repeated across a floor area roughly the size of a city block. When the water supply system was later superseded by direct piping, the cistern fell out of use and remained sealed for most of the 20th century. It was the conversion to a library in 1999, by the Universitat Pompeu Fabra, that revealed the full quality of Fontserè's brickwork: the arched ceilings are now the dominant architectural feature of the reading rooms, giving the space a gravity that most new university buildings cannot approach.

The entrance pavilion above ground, an ornate brick structure with Moorish-inflected detailing, was the public face of what was otherwise a buried piece of infrastructure. It survives intact and serves as the library's main entrance — a rare case where the decorative elements outlasted the utilitarian purpose they were built to signal.
