Barcelona's water supply was one of the defining political and engineering problems of the 19th century. The city had grown with extraordinary speed, driven by textile industry profits and population migration, but its infrastructure had not kept pace. Periodic droughts combined with a population that had outgrown its medieval water systems made access to clean water a constant source of conflict between the municipality, private companies, and an increasingly organised working class.

The deposit on Montjuïc was part of the network of cisterns, channels, and reservoirs built to stabilise supply during this period. Montjuïc's elevation made it an ideal location for gravity-fed distribution — water stored at height could reach much of the lower city without pumping. The 19th-century engineering required to cut and line these underground chambers was substantial, and the structures were built to last.

What remains today is a fragment of the hydraulic city that existed beneath and alongside the visible one — the pipes, tanks, and tunnels that made urban life possible but were never meant to be seen. Coming across the deposit is a reminder that Barcelona's surface has always been underlain by infrastructure, and that the decisions made about water in the 1800s shaped the distribution of the city in ways still legible today.
