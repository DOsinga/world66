Dolmens are among Europe's oldest human constructions — communal burial chambers assembled from massive stone slabs by Neolithic communities sometime between 4000 and 2000 BC. The Dolmen de la Vinya Monera sits in the Collserola Natural Park, which forms the green backdrop visible from almost anywhere in Barcelona. That the hills above the city were already settled, cultivated, and consecrated long before any Roman or Iberian presence is a fact the urban landscape below tends to obscure.

The dolmen's name comes from the plot of land where it was found — the *vinya* (vineyard) of a family called Monera — which is typical of how these structures survived in rural Catalonia: not through official preservation but through the slow accretion of local names and landmarks. Farmers knew where the old stones were; they ploughed around them, named fields after them, and incorporated them into the mental geography of the land.

Reaching the dolmen requires a walk into the park, which is itself part of the experience. Collserola rises sharply from the city's northern edge, and within a few minutes of leaving the streets the urban noise fades entirely. The stones sit in that silence as they have for millennia, predating not just the city below but the very concept of a city on this coastline.
