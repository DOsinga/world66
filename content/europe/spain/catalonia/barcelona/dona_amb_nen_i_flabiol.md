The *flabiol* is a small end-blown flute, typically six holes, that sits at the heart of traditional Catalan music. What distinguishes it from other folk instruments is how it has always been played: the flabiol goes in the left hand while the right hand beats a small drum called the *tamborí*, producing melody and rhythm simultaneously from a single performer. This technique demands considerable dexterity, and skilled flabiol players were once essential figures at every Catalan festival, dance, and celebration.

The sculpture — a woman, a small child, and a flabiol player — captures a domestic or communal scene, the kind of everyday cultural transmission where music is embedded in family life rather than performed on a stage. The setting in the Gothic Quarter places it in the older layers of the city, where these traditions were maintained through the worst years of Franco's suppression of Catalan cultural expression.

During the dictatorship, playing the flabiol and tamborí at sardana dances — the circular Catalan folk dance — was one of the most visible acts of quiet cultural resistance available to ordinary people. The instrument's presence in public sculpture is therefore not purely nostalgic; it carries the memory of a period when maintaining this music was a political act.
