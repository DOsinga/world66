The Parc de Joan Miró occupies the site of the old municipal slaughterhouse — the Escorxador — on the edge of the Eixample, and at its centre rises Joan Miró's last major sculpture: a 22-metre concrete tower clad entirely in broken ceramic tile, in the sgraffito and trencadís technique that Miró and Gaudí both drew from the Catalan craft tradition. The title means Woman and Bird. The form is simultaneously both and neither — a totem pole, an idol, an abstraction that resolves into something organic the more you look at it.

Miró completed the design in 1981, two years before his death at age 90. He had been working in the monumental ceramic style for decades, from the wall murals in UNESCO headquarters in Paris to the Sun and Moon walls at Harvard. Dona i Ocell was installed in 1983, the year he died, in a park that was still under construction — the former slaughterhouse buildings were being demolished, the gardens were being planted. The dedication ceremony was held that year, and Miró attended in a wheelchair, one of his last public appearances.

The sculpture stands in a shallow circular reflecting pool. In the right light — late afternoon in summer, when the low sun catches the ceramic surface — the tile colours change register and the form seems to move. The park around it is one of the few large green spaces in the Eixample grid, and it gets heavy use from the neighbourhood: dogs, children, old men playing cards on the grass. The sculpture rises above all of this, colourful and slightly mysterious, exactly as Miró seems to have intended.
