Joan Miró was 88 years old when *Dona i Ocell* — Woman and Bird — was inaugurated in 1983, just months before his death. He had been working on public sculptures for decades, but this one, rising 22 metres from a shallow pool in what had been the city's main slaughterhouse district, was understood from the beginning as a kind of testament. The park around it was named for him; the work was intended to anchor his legacy in the city that had made him.

The slaughterhouse site is a detail Miró is said to have found poetically apt. The Escorxador — the abattoir — had been one of the grim necessities of industrial Barcelona, a place of death serving the city's appetite. Transforming it into a park named after one of the 20th century's most life-affirming artists, and filling it with a tower covered in Miró's vivid biomorphic forms and ceramic colours, was the kind of urban inversion the surrealist tradition enjoyed.

The surface of the tower is encrusted with ceramic fragments — white, black, yellow, red — that pick up the sunlight differently throughout the day. *Dona i Ocell* belongs to a visual vocabulary Miró developed over seven decades: the woman, the bird, and the moon as fundamental presences, rendered in forms that are simultaneously childlike and deeply strange. The work is enormous and gentle at the same time.
