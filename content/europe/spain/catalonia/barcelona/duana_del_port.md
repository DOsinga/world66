Every ship entering Barcelona's port between 1902 and the mid-20th century passed its cargo through this building before it could enter the city. The Duana del Port — designed by Enric Sagnier, who was also responsible for the Tibidabo church and the Palace of Justice — occupies the point where the Passeig de Colom meets the port's inner basin, making it the literal hinge between the city grid and the working harbour. The customs inspectors' offices, the weighing rooms, and the bonded warehouses were arranged across its neoclassical wings in a sequence designed to process bulk goods efficiently while projecting institutional authority.

Sagnier gave the building a deliberately sober programme — a rusticated ground floor, tall arched windows, and a central pediment — that read as a statement of fiscal seriousness at a moment when Barcelona was still processing goods from Cuba and the remaining Spanish colonies. The 1898 disaster, which ended Spain's colonial empire, had already happened by the time the building opened, but its formal language was conceived in the optimism of the preceding decade.

The building now houses regional government offices, and the inspection rooms have been converted to administrative use. But its position — at the exact meeting of the Ramblas axis and the port — means it still functions architecturally as it was intended: as the formal gateway between the city's land-based commerce and the sea. The equestrian figures on the roofline, representing trade and navigation, have kept their post through every change of use.
