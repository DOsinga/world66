After the War of the Spanish Succession ended in 1714, the victorious Bourbon administration punished Barcelona's resistance by closing its university and relocating it to Cervera, a small inland town with no academic tradition and no library worth the name — a deliberate act of institutional dispossession. It remained in Cervera for 138 years. When Elies Rogent began building the Edifici Històric in 1863, he was not simply constructing a university; he was building the instrument of return.

Rogent chose Neo-Romanesque because it was neither the Baroque of the Counter-Reformation nor the Neoclassicism of Bourbon state architecture — it reached back to a medieval tradition that could be claimed as specifically Catalan and Iberian. The result is a building of exceptional civic weight: the central cloister with its arcaded galleries, the ceremonial staircase, the main hall with its coffered ceiling and portraits of Catalan scholars. It was one of the first large public buildings to occupy a full block of Ildefons Cerdà's new Eixample grid, and its presence — set back from the street, symmetrical, monumental — helped establish what civic seriousness could look like in the planned expansion.

The university now has multiple campuses across the city, but the historic building remains the seat of the rector and the principal ceremonial space. Its courtyards are open during the day and are worth entering for the quality of the stonework alone — the building is consistently undervisited relative to its architectural importance.
