Stand at any intersection in the Eixample and look at the building corners: they are not right angles. Every corner is cut at 45 degrees, creating a small flat face at each intersection where a right-angle corner would otherwise be. Seen from above — from an aircraft or from the hills — this gives the Eixample its distinctive appearance: not a pure grid but an infinite sequence of octagons, the pattern visible in any aerial photograph of Barcelona.

The chamfered corners were designed by Ildefons Cerdà, the engineer who planned the Eixample in 1859. His original plan was more radical: the corners would create small public squares at every intersection, with benches and greenery, giving the district a network of breathing spaces distributed throughout the grid. The corners themselves would be set back enough that the intersection would feel open rather than enclosed. Carriages — and later, cars — would have long sightlines in all directions and would be able to turn without risking collision.

Only the corner cut was realized; the benches and greenery mostly were not. But the chamfered corner survives as the fundamental visual character of the Eixample — a design decision made in 1859 that has defined the experience of every intersection in this part of the city for more than 160 years. It also created the wide windows and balconied corners that Modernista architects exploited: a flat corner face is easier to ornament than a right angle, and Domènech, Puig, and Gaudí all used the corner prominently. The chamfer is, in a sense, the technical reason why the Block of Discord on Passeig de Gràcia looks the way it does.
