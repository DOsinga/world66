Jaume Barba carved *El petó de la mort* — The Kiss of Death — in 1930 for the tomb of the Llaudet family in the Poblenou cemetery, and it became famous almost immediately. The image is stark and precise: a winged skeleton bends to press its lips against the forehead of a handsome young man who has collapsed or is being lowered toward death. The young man's expression is one of peace rather than terror, which is the work's central and most unsettling choice.

The Kiss of Death was a concept with deep roots in European memento mori tradition, but Barba's execution is unusually literal and unusually tender. Death does not seize or strike; Death kisses, and the gesture reads as almost maternal. The winged figure is skeletal but not grotesque; the young man is beautiful and relaxed. Together they create an image of mortality that refuses to be horrifying, which perhaps explains its enduring hold on viewers.

Poblenou cemetery — the Cementiri de l'Est — dates from 1775 and contains an extraordinary collection of 19th and 20th-century funerary art. In a city with three major cemeteries on its hills, Poblenou's is the most coastal, and walking it on a clear day you can see the sea over the cemetery walls. *El petó de la mort* is the piece that most visitors seek out specifically, treating it as a pilgrimage destination within a destination.
