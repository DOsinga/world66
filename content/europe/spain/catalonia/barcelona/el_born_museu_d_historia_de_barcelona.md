The Mercat del Born opened in 1876 as one of Europe's finest examples of iron-and-glass market architecture — a structure that would not have looked out of place in Paris or London. It functioned as a wholesale market until 1971, then sat abandoned for decades while the city argued about what to do with it. Plans to turn it into a library prompted excavation of the floor, and what emerged stopped the project entirely.

Under the market's Victorian ironwork lay a quarter of Barcelona's 1714 cityscape — streets, houses, wells, shops — buried intact when Bourbon forces of Felipe V demolished the Ribera neighbourhood to build a citadel after the War of Spanish Succession. The defeat of September 11, 1714 is the event commemorated as Catalonia's national day; excavating the physical traces of the neighbourhood destroyed in its aftermath was an act of historical recovery that carried enormous emotional weight.

The decision to leave the ruins exposed rather than fill them in and proceed with the library was taken in 2002, and the market now functions as a museum built around the archaeology. Walking through it, you pass over the 18th-century street plan under the 19th-century iron canopy inside the 21st-century museum — three layers of the city's history simultaneously present in the same space.
