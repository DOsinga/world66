The Carmel neighbourhood occupies the slopes below and around the Turó de la Rovira — the hill where Civil War anti-aircraft batteries were positioned. The neighbourhood itself was created by internal migrants arriving in Barcelona in the 1940s and 1950s: workers from Andalusia, Murcia, and Extremadura who came north for factory jobs and could not afford formal housing.

The barri was built without any official planning. Migrants bought or occupied plots on the hillside and built their own houses with whatever materials they could find or afford, often over several years, adding rooms as families grew and money allowed. The result was a dense, irregular neighbourhood with narrow streets following the slope rather than any grid, and houses of varying quality and age that reflected their owners' economic trajectories.

The Barcelona city government largely ignored the barri until the 1960s, when it was large enough that it could no longer be treated as a temporary phenomenon. Services — running water, sewage, paved streets — arrived late and unevenly. The community's informal origins gave it a social cohesion unusual in formally planned developments: people had built the neighbourhood themselves, maintained it themselves, and fought for its recognition together. The neighbourhood's Associació de Veïns (Residents' Association) was one of the most active in Barcelona during the transition to democracy, having spent decades negotiating with an indifferent administration.
