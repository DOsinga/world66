Rolling a hoop — *rutlla* in Catalan — was one of the defining street games of European urban childhood for centuries. Children guided iron or wooden hoops through city streets using a stick, and the game required exactly the kind of skill, coordination, and spatial awareness that narrow, crowded streets demanded. By the mid-20th century the game had almost entirely disappeared, displaced first by bicycles and later by cars that reclaimed the street as transport infrastructure rather than public space.

*El nen de la rutlla* — The Boy with the Hoop — catches the child in motion, the hoop tilted at the angle of active play. The sculpture is installed in the Sarrià district, one of Barcelona's quietest and most residential neighbourhoods, a former independent municipality absorbed into the city in 1921 that still has something of a village character. Sarrià's streets were exactly the kind of neighbourhood where hoop-rolling survived longest, in the quieter back streets away from the main traffic.

The sculpture belongs to a type of public art that Barcelona has placed generously throughout its neighbourhoods: small figurative pieces that celebrate the specific texture of local life rather than grand historical or allegorical themes. *El nen de la rutlla* is about nothing more and nothing less than a game that children played in these streets, and its presence in stone is a way of slowing down the city's perpetual renovation enough to remember what was here before.
