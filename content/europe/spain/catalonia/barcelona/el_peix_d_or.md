Frank Gehry built *El Peix d'Or* — the Golden Fish, sometimes called the Barcelona Fish — as a sculptural commission for the 1992 Olympic Village, installed beside the Hotel Arts at the waterfront. At roughly 35 metres long and 54 metres tall, the fish shape is unmistakable, the scales rendered in interlocking titanium panels that shift colour with the light — gold, bronze, copper depending on the angle and the hour.

What makes the fish more than a landmark is its place in Gehry's own development. The titanium cladding technique he worked out for this piece — how to engineer the curves, how to make the panels ripple organically — became the core technology of the Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao, completed in 1997. The Barcelona fish is a full-scale prototype for one of the most influential buildings of the late 20th century, and it arrived five years before Bilbao made Gehry's curved titanium surfaces famous worldwide.

Gehry has spoken about fish forms throughout his career as an obsession — the fish appears in his jewellery, his furniture, his early sculptural experiments. For the Olympic commission, the form was not decorative but structurally and conceptually essential. The piece also announced that the Barcelona waterfront, long closed off by industrial infrastructure, had definitively opened to the sea.
