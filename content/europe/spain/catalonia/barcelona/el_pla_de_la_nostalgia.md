The *Pla de la Nostàlgia* sits in the Olympic Village, the neighbourhood built on the demolished bones of Poblenou — Barcelona's great industrial district, the so-called Catalan Manchester. When the city was awarded the 1992 Olympics, the decision was made to use the event as a catalyst for clearing land that the city considered degraded and transforming it into a model residential district with direct sea access for the first time in the city's history.

What this meant in practice was the demolition of hundreds of buildings, the displacement of remaining residents and businesses, and the erasure of a specific urban landscape that had developed over 150 years. The factories, warehouses, workers' housing, and neighbourhood infrastructure of Poblenou were removed systematically. The *nostàlgia* of the title is not sentimentality; it names a real loss.

The work's presence in the new neighbourhood it was built to mark is an act of institutional self-awareness that is relatively rare in public art. Cities do not often commission art that questions the projects that commissioned it. The *Pla de la Nostàlgia* does not refuse the Olympic Village or demand Poblenou back; it asks that the transformation be remembered as transformation rather than pure improvement.
