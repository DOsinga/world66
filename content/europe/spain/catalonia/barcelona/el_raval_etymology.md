The name El Raval tells you exactly what this place was: the suburb. Raval comes from the Arabic rabad, meaning an extramural settlement — the inhabited area outside a fortified city. The word entered Catalan during the period when Arabic was the prestige language of urban administration in much of the Iberian Peninsula, even in Christian territories, because it carried practical concepts for describing urban forms that Latin and early Romance languages lacked.

Barcelona's Roman walls defined a small rectangle on the hill of Mont Tàber. As the city grew through the early medieval period, population spilled outside those walls in all directions. The settlement that accumulated to the west of La Rambla — which followed the course of a small stream, the Cagalell — became known as the raval: the place outside, the area that was inhabited but not enclosed, not protected, not quite the city. A new set of walls eventually enclosed the Raval in the late 13th and early 14th centuries, incorporating it fully into the city, but the name persisted.

The Raval has been the city's most consistently working-class and immigrant neighbourhood for most of its history. Its street grid is denser and more irregular than the Barri Gòtic, reflecting its unplanned organic growth before the enclosing walls imposed any order. The name survives as a geographical designation without the stigma it once carried — the word for what lay outside has become, simply, the name of the place. But knowing the etymology changes how you read the streets: the Raval was always the neighbourhood defined by its relationship to the walls, by being just beyond them.
