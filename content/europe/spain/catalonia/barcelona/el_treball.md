Montjuïc cemetery has been Barcelona's principal burial ground since 1883, and its monuments reflect the full social hierarchy of the city that built them — from grand bourgeois mausoleums in marble and granite to modest collective graves for the poor. *El Treball* — Labour — was commissioned by one of Barcelona's workers' mutual aid societies, the kind of institution that provided sick pay, death benefits, and collective dignity to artisans and factory workers in the era before the welfare state.

The figure of Labour as a heroic or dignified subject, rather than a pitiful or picturesque one, was a deliberate political choice. The mutual aid societies of late 19th-century Barcelona were deeply engaged in the emerging labour movement, and commissioning a major monument in a public cemetery was an assertion that workers deserved the same commemorative scale as merchants and industrialists.

The cemetery's position on Montjuïc gives the monument a particular resonance: the hill above holds the dead of every period and every faction of Barcelona's turbulent history, from Republican victims of Franco's firing squads in the *Fossar de la Pedrera* to the elaborate bourgeois tombs of the families who built the Eixample. *El Treball* stands in that company as a democratic counter-claim.
