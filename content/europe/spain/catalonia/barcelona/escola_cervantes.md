The building started life as the residence of the Dou family, one of Barcelona's prosperous Liberal dynasties of the nineteenth century. When the city's expanding school network needed premises in the Eixample, the council acquired the mansion and converted it rather than demolishing it — an early exercise in adaptive reuse that preserved an intact piece of domestic modernisme inside a working neighbourhood.

Named after Miguel de Cervantes, the school became a quiet emblem of the Republican-era belief that public education deserved beautiful buildings. During the Franco years the name survived, though the pedagogical ambitions behind the original project did not. The Catalan language was banned from the classroom for decades, a particular irony in a building standing in the heart of a neighbourhood where Catalan was the everyday language of the street.

Escola Cervantes is not a tourist site, but the facade repays attention: the ornamental ironwork and the proportions of the windows belong to a moment when a school was considered civic architecture worthy of as much care as a theatre or a bank. The children who file past its entrance each morning are heirs to a much longer argument about what cities owe their youngest inhabitants.
