The floors inside Escola Massana are not level. Pinós angled them deliberately — each studio tilts toward its glazed wall to pull in north light without glare, so painting and printmaking students work on a plane that feels slightly wrong until you realise it is optimised for the eye rather than the foot.

Pinós spent a decade as co-principal with Enric Miralles — the two designed the Igualada Cemetery together, one of the most discussed works of 1990s European architecture — before his death in 2000 sent her practice in its own direction. The Massana commission, won by competition in 2009 and completed after years of budget delays, became her largest built work in her home city. The building's concrete shell folds inward around an atrium that doubles as an exhibition and event space, so the school's student work is visible from every gallery level rather than confined to studios.

The site matters too. Massana had occupied a 1970s building on the lower Rambla del Raval, which gave it a presence in the neighbourhood during decades when the Raval was Barcelona's most contested urban redevelopment zone. The new building sits one block away, on Carrer dels Àngels facing the MACBA courtyard, which means the school and the contemporary art museum now share a public plaza — an urban adjacency that was planned but still reads, from the street, as a genuine cultural district rather than a zoning decision.
