Gaudí built the Escoles de la Sagrada Família in 1909 as a temporary school for the children of workers on the basilica site. Budget was minimal and the materials were cheap brick, yet the structure became one of his most technically adventurous works. The roof and walls ripple in a continuous sinusoidal curve — a form Gaudí derived from his studies of catenary geometry — distributing loads so efficiently that a thin brick shell could span the space without internal supports.

Le Corbusier visited in 1928 and sketched the roofline in his notebooks. Architects have since traced the undulating roof to influences as varied as Pier Luigi Nervi's concrete shells and Utzon's Sydney Opera House. The building demonstrated that a corrugated surface is structurally far stronger than a flat one, a principle engineers now take for granted but which Gaudí worked out by hanging models and inverting them to read the thrust lines.

The school was partly demolished during the Civil War and later rebuilt from photographs. It now stands in the shadow of the basilica's nave, easy to walk past while craning upward at the towers. Pausing to look at it instead is a useful corrective: Gaudí's most lasting influence on architecture may have come not from the cathedral but from this small, cheap, provisional building that nobody was meant to keep.
