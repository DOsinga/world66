The Carmelite friars arrived in what would become Gràcia in 1626, building a modest church dedicated to Our Lady of Grace — a title that would eventually lend its name to the entire neighbourhood. Saint Joseph was added to the dedication later, and the double-barrelled official name proved far too cumbersome for daily use. Locals have called it the Josepets — the little Josephs — for as long as anyone can remember, and the plaza in front of it follows the same logic: it is the Plaça dels Josepets even in speech, whatever the street signs say.

The Carmelites were expelled during the liberal suppressions of the 1830s and the convent was demolished, but the church survived and was handed to the secular clergy. The transition preserved the building while erasing most traces of its origins: the cloister, the chapter house, and the library all disappeared. What remains is the nave and facade, which date from a seventeenth-century rebuilding and show the sober Catalan baroque that was developing at the same moment as the more elaborate Spanish baroque further south.

The Josepets sits at the foot of Gràcia on the Carrer de Gràcia road that was once the main route north out of Barcelona. Travellers heading to Sarrià or the hills above would have passed it as the last church before open country. That threshold quality has faded as the city expanded, but the church still marks a perceptible shift in the grain of the streets around it.
