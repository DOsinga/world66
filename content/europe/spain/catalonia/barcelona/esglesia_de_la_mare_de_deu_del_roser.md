The Dominican order has claimed ownership of the Rosary since the thirteenth century, when their founder Saint Dominic — according to a tradition the order elaborated over generations — received the practice from the Virgin herself. Whether the story is historically credible matters less than its institutional consequence: the Dominicans became the Rosary's primary propagandists across Europe and the Americas, producing confraternities, processions, and churches dedicated to Our Lady of the Rosary in every city where they operated.

The church in Poble Sec carries that tradition into a working-class neighbourhood that developed rapidly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as migrants from across Spain settled on the slopes of Montjuïc. The Rosary dedication gave the new parish community a devotional identity tied to the universal Church rather than to local Catalan traditions, which suited a neighbourhood whose population was often non-Catalan in origin.

The building is modest in scale and decoration, appropriate to a neighbourhood that was never wealthy. Its interest lies less in architectural ambition than in the social history it represents: the Church's effort to provide pastoral infrastructure to the rapidly expanding industrial periphery, and the working families who made it their own over successive generations even as the neighbourhood around them changed.
