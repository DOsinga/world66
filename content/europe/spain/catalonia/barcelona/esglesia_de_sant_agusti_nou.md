The Convent of Sant Agustí Nou was founded in 1728 to replace a medieval Augustinian house lost to urban expansion. The 'Nou' — New — in the name distinguished it from the earlier foundation, and the adjective stuck even after the building itself aged into the fabric of the old city. By the time the liberal revolutions of the 1830s suppressed the religious orders, the complex had become an institution too embedded in the neighbourhood to disappear entirely.

In July 1835, anti-clerical mobs burned convents across Barcelona in what historians call the Burning of the Convents — a violent expression of resentment at the Church's wealth and political alignment. Sant Agustí survived when others did not, partly because local residents intervened to protect a building they thought of as theirs rather than the clergy's. The convent buildings were secularised and converted to municipal uses including a market and eventually a civic centre, while the church continued as a parish.

The building is not architecturally distinguished by Barcelona's standards, but its survival across nearly three centuries of revolution, dictatorship, and urban transformation makes it a document of the Raval's stubborn continuity. The neighbourhood around it has changed repeatedly — immigrant waves, the post-Olympic renovation, gentrification pressures — while the church has remained a fixed point on streets that are always in motion.
