Gràcia was an independent municipality until 1897, when Barcelona forcibly annexed it despite fierce local resistance — a grievance the neighbourhood has nursed ever since. The church of Sant Joan was already centuries old when annexation came, its festival of Sant Joan at midsummer one of the fixed anchors of village life. Converting a village parish into a city church required no architectural changes; the social role simply continued under new administrative circumstances.

The feast of Sant Joan — the eve of June 24th — is the night when Barcelona burns. Fireworks, bonfires, and the correfoc fill the streets city-wide, but in Gràcia the celebration has a particular intensity because it is the neighbourhood's own patron, not an imported civic occasion. The church steps become a gathering point, and the plaza in front of it fills from sunset until well after dawn.

The building itself reflects the compressed history of Catalan church architecture: a medieval foundation rebuilt in the baroque period, repaired after Civil War damage, and restored again in the democratic era. Each phase left traces without erasing the previous ones. The result is a building that reads as layered rather than unified, which is perhaps appropriate for a parish whose identity has always been defined by accumulated loyalties rather than any single founding moment.
