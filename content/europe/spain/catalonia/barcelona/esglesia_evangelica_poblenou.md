Protestantism in nineteenth-century Spain existed in a legal grey zone at best and was actively persecuted at worst. The Constitution of 1876 permitted private worship by non-Catholics but prohibited public expression of any religion other than Roman Catholicism. Protestant congregations that formed in the industrial neighbourhoods around Barcelona did so at real personal risk, meeting in apartments and workshops before daring to construct visible meeting houses.

The Poblenou evangelical church emerged from this climate of cautious persistence. Poblenou had become one of Barcelona's primary industrial districts by the 1870s — the 'Catalan Manchester' as contemporaries called it — and its working-class population included migrants who had encountered Protestant missionaries in other regions of Spain. The congregation that formed was small, working-class, and largely outside the networks of bourgeois Protestant respectability that shaped religious dissent elsewhere in Europe.

The arrival of full religious freedom after Franco's death in 1975 transformed the legal situation but not necessarily the social position of evangelical churches in Catalonia. The Poblenou congregation has continued as a minority institution, its existence a reminder that Barcelona's religious history is more plural than the city's Catholic monuments alone suggest. Walking past the building is an encounter with a tradition that survived by staying quiet for a very long time.
