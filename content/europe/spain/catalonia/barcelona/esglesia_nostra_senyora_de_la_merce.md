La Mercè — Our Lady of Mercy — became Barcelona's patron in 1868, chosen by popular vote after the city had narrowly avoided a locust plague. The vote edged out Sant Eulàlia, the older patron, and the rivalry between the two has never quite died: neighbourhoods still divide informal loyalties along ancient lines. The church itself predates the patronage by more than a century, built in the 1770s on the site of a medieval convent of the Mercedarian order, founded specifically to ransom Christians captured by North African pirates.

The Mercedarian mission is written into the church's iconography: the order's emblem — a white habit with the arms of Aragon and the cross of the Crusaders — appears repeatedly in the interior decoration. The order raised money across Catalonia, then sent its members to negotiate with Barbary captors, sometimes offering themselves as hostages when funds ran short. That history of bodily risk in exchange for another's freedom gives the church a specific moral texture that the architecture alone does not convey.

The Festes de la Mercè each September 24th transform the entire plaza into a performance space for Barcelona's most spectacular popular traditions. The castellers raise their human towers, the correfoc sends fire-breathing devils through the crowds, and the gegants — giant papier-mâché figures — process through the streets. The church itself is almost incidental during the festival; the street is the sanctuary.
