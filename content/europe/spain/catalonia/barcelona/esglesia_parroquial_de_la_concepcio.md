When Ildefons Cerdà was laying out the Eixample in the 1860s, the old city's religious orders were losing their convents to liberal secularisation laws. The convent of Jerusalem, a Gothic structure dating to the fifteenth century, was slated for demolition. A group of parishioners and clergy obtained permission to dismantle it and re-erect it on a newly allocated block in the expanding grid — a task completed between 1871 and 1888.

The transplantation was meticulous. Each stone was numbered, removed, and relaid in its original position. The cloister, the nave columns, the carved capitals — all came from the Carrer dels Àngels and were reassembled on the Carrer d'Aragó. The result is a medieval interior inside a modernista neighbourhood, a dislocation that still stops visitors in their tracks when they step through the doors expecting something neoclassical and find instead ribbed vaulting and Gothic lancets.

The campanile was added after the move and does not match the Gothic vocabulary of the rest of the building, which gives the exterior a collaged quality. But inside, the transplanted church is coherent and cool, and the cloister — easily missed from the street — preserves the atmosphere of a much older city entirely intact, as if the stone remembered where it came from.
