The Dominican convent of Santa Caterina was founded in the thirteenth century and occupied a substantial block in the old city for six hundred years. When liberal legislation dissolved the religious orders in 1835, the complex was secularised; by 1837 it had been demolished to make way for what would become Barcelona's first covered market — appropriately named the Mercat de Santa Caterina. The speed of demolition was driven partly by politics and partly by the city's urgent need for market space as the population grew.

Archaeological excavations during the market's extensive renovation — completed in 2005 with Enric Miralles's undulating ceramic roof — uncovered the foundations of the medieval cloister and church beneath the market floor. Rather than rebury the finds, the architects and archaeologists designed a display zone that allows visitors to look down into the excavated layers through a glazed floor section: the Espai Santa Caterina.

The layers visible in the excavation span multiple centuries of occupation on the same site, from the Dominican foundation back to earlier Roman-period and medieval secular structures below it. The space is a literal cross-section through Barcelona's time, where the medieval, the early modern, the nineteenth century, and the contemporary are stacked in visible sequence beneath one of the most photographed rooflines in the city.
