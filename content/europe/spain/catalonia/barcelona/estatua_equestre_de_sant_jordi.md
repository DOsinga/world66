Josep Llimona was the dominant sculptor of the Catalan modernista movement, a contemporary and collaborator of Gaudí who brought to stone the same fusion of symbolism, natural form, and spiritual intensity. His Sant Jordi, created in the early twentieth century, shows the saint mounted and triumphant but with a psychological interiority unusual for equestrian monuments: the figure seems to contemplate rather than merely display his victory.

Sant Jordi — Saint George — is Catalonia's patron saint and the occasion of its most characteristic popular festival: on April 23rd, Sant Jordi's Day, lovers exchange roses and books throughout the region. The tradition is specific to Catalonia and has deep roots; it was not invented by tourism authorities. The dragon that George slays in the legend is traditionally identified with a threat to the city, and the princess he rescues with Barcelona itself — an allegory of liberation that took on obvious political resonances during the twentieth century.

Llimona's treatment departs from the rigid medieval conventions by depicting horse and rider in motion and in psychological depth, reflecting the influence of Auguste Rodin's expressive surfaces on Catalan sculptors of the period. The dragon below the horse's hooves has an almost sympathetic quality — also characteristically modernista, ambivalent about the clean moral simplicity of the saint-and-monster narrative.
