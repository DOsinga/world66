Barceloneta was constructed between 1753 and 1755 to a grid plan by military engineer Juan Martín Cermeño — an act of urban planning that is impossible to separate from the military violence that preceded it. Philip V had demolished the Ribera neighbourhood to build the Citadel fortress after the siege of 1714, displacing thousands of residents. The new district on the sand spit was the compensation: regimented, small-scaled, built for the fishing and maritime working class.

The neighbourhood's identity as a place of sailors, fishermen, and dock workers persisted for two centuries. The distinctive narrow houses — each plot limited in width so that the grid would produce maximum density — were occupied by multiple families, and the street life of Barceloneta had a density and informality that set it apart from the rest of the city. The beach was a working space, not a leisure amenity, for most of that history.

The Evocació Marinera sculpture acknowledges that vanished identity. The maritime economy that shaped Barceloneta through the nineteenth and most of the twentieth century has largely disappeared: the industrial port moved away, the fishing fleet shrank, and the neighbourhood gentrified after the 1992 Olympics opened the beachfront. The memorial is addressed to a way of life that the district itself can only partially remember.
