Louis Moritz arrived in Barcelona from Alsace in the 1850s and established his brewery on Ronda de Sant Antoni in 1856 — before there was a unified Germany, before Spain had significant industrial infrastructure, and well before Barcelona's Eixample district had been fully laid out. Moritz was one of the first people to industrialise beer production in Spain, and the brand he created became a specifically Barcelonès working-class institution, the local beer in an era before Estrella Damm dominated the market.

The factory closed and the site sat unused until 2011, when it was reopened as a restaurant and microbrewery. Jean Nouvel — the Pritzker Prize-winning architect responsible for the Torre Glòries tower across the city — designed the conversion, preserving the 19th-century industrial brick vaults and excavating a new underground level below them. The juxtaposition of original structure and contemporary insertion is visible throughout: old brick above, Nouvel below.

What makes the Fábrica Moritz unusual among Barcelona's industrial conversions is the continuity of function: the building makes beer again. In a city where industrial buildings were routinely converted into hotels, offices, or cultural centres with the original use erased, the decision to restore a brewery's brewing function in its original building was deliberate, and has proven commercially viable.
