Pere Falqués i Urpí, Barcelona's municipal architect, designed the lampposts of the Passeig de Lluís Companys in 1905 — the same year he produced the famous lamp-benches on the Passeig de Gràcia. In both cases his approach was the same: treat street furniture as architecture, integrate multiple functions into a single object. Each lamppost on this passeig has four curved stone benches built into its base, so the column is simultaneously streetlamp and seating. The upper shaft bifurcates into two diagonal arms with sinuous vegetal forms that move between Gothic, Classical, and Modernista registers without settling in any.

The originals were badly damaged during underground car park construction and faithfully reconstructed in 1991–98 from Falqués's original designs. The reconstruction acknowledges the passeig's role as the processional approach to the Arc de Triomf — designed by Josep Vilaseca i Casanovas for the 1888 Exposition in exposed red brick, deliberately not the classical stone of a military arch, to assert Barcelona's industrial identity — and the park beyond it.

The passeig was renamed for Lluís Companys in 1979. Companys was president of the Generalitat de Catalunya, arrested by the Gestapo in France in August 1940, handed to Franco, tried by a court-martial that lasted under an hour, and shot barefoot on Montjuïc on 15 October 1940 — reportedly saying "Per Catalunya!" He is the only democratically elected head of government in European history to have been executed by a fascist regime. Walking between Falqués's 1905 lampposts under that name is one of Barcelona's more quietly charged experiences.
