The faun as a mythological figure — half man, half goat, associated with Dionysiac wildness and untamed nature — was irresistible to the artists of Catalan modernisme. A movement that wanted to recover an Arcadian vitality from beneath bourgeois convention found in the faun a ready-made symbol: the classical world's embodiment of nature asserting itself against civilised order. Placing such a figure in the Ciutadella park, itself a reclaimed military space converted to public greenery, added another layer of meaning.

The Parc de la Ciutadella was created on the site of the hated Bourbon citadel demolished in the 1860s — a military installation that had symbolised authoritarian control of the city for a century and a half. Converting the site into a park was an ideological act as much as an urban planning decision, and the park's sculptures were chosen to populate it with imagery of freedom, nature, and civic joy. A faun fits that programme precisely.

The bronze technique used in this and similar Ciutadella sculptures reflects the influence of Rodin's surfaces on Barcelona's younger sculptors, who visited Paris, absorbed the French master's approach to modelling, and brought it back to a Catalan context where it merged with Maragall's poetry and the noucentista interest in Mediterranean classical heritage. The result is a distinctly Catalan modernisme: not French, not Spanish, but something that drew on both and on older roots.
