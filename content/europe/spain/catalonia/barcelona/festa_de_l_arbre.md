The Festa de l'Arbre — Tree Festival — was inaugurated in Barcelona in 1894, modelled on similar arbour day traditions in the United States and France. The event brought together schoolchildren, civic associations, and municipal authorities to plant trees in public spaces, combining environmental consciousness with pedagogical purpose: the children who planted were meant to understand themselves as stewards of the city's future.

The campaign was part of a broader modernista-era interest in the relationship between urban life and the natural world. Barcelona's rapid industrialisation had created severe environmental pressures — air quality, water contamination, and the absence of green space in working-class districts were well-documented problems by the 1890s. The Tree Festival responded with a gesture that was partly symbolic and partly practical, planting trees while also creating a shared civic ritual around environmental care.

The sculpture memorialising the festival turns a seasonal event into permanent public art, which is itself a statement about the relationship between ephemeral civic actions and lasting cultural memory. Most of what cities do disappears without trace; the Tree Festival produced monuments. Whether the trees themselves survived matters less to the memorial's meaning than the gesture of civic care that the festival represented and that the sculpture now commemorates.
