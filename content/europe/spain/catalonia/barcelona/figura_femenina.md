Allegorical female figures — representing Justice, Nature, Abundance, the Arts — were a standard vocabulary of public sculpture throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, but Catalan modernisme gave them a specific inflection. Where French academic sculpture tended toward composed, idealized types, the Barcelona sculptors working in the shadow of Rodin and Maillol produced figures with a more immediate physical presence: weight in the contrapposto, texture in the surface, tension in the pose.

The figure belongs to a park that was deliberately populated with sculpture as part of its conversion from military fortress to public garden. The Ciutadella was supposed to express everything the citadel had not been: open, welcoming, beautiful, and devoted to pleasure and culture rather than to the suppression of the city's inhabitants. Sculpture was the most direct way to encode those values in permanent form.

Reading allegorical sculpture requires patience with a visual language that the twentieth century largely abandoned. The female nude in public space now carries meanings that the early modernista sculptors did not intend and could not have anticipated, but recovering what the figure was supposed to communicate — and to whom, in what context, for what civic purpose — remains a worthwhile exercise in understanding what modernisme was actually for, beyond its surface exuberance.
