Josep Maria Jujol is the least-known of the great Catalan Moderniste architects, which is in part because he spent much of his career working in Gaudí's shadow. He designed the sinuous ceramic bench at Park Güell, helped model the chimneys of Casa Milà, and contributed to several of Gaudí's major projects. But Jujol's independent commissions — scattered across Barcelona and the Tarragona countryside — show a surrealist inventiveness that goes beyond Gaudí's organic naturalism into something stranger and more personal.

The Finca Sansalvador in Sant Gervasi was commissioned in the 1920s as a private garden estate. Jujol designed gate towers, walls, and garden structures with his characteristic use of broken ceramic, curved forms, and ornamental improvisation. The money ran out before the project was finished. The site was never completed, never properly maintained, and never demolished. Decades of Barcelonan indifference did what neglect always does: the vegetation absorbed the structures, the ceramic work crumbled and re-adhered in new configurations, and the whole became an accidental landscape.

The result is one of the most atmospheric fragments of Modernisme in the city — not despite its ruination but because of it. Jujol's work has a quality that finished buildings sometimes conceal: the sense of something still in the process of becoming. Here, where the process stopped, that quality is undisguised. The site is not formally open to the public but is visible from the street, the gate towers legible through the overgrowth.
