Before 1897, Barcelona was surrounded by independent municipalities: Sants, Gràcia, Sant Andreu, Horta, and Sant Martí de Provençals each had their own councils, their own budgets, and their own civic identities. The boundary stones that marked the limits of each jurisdiction were practical objects — they defined tax obligations, legal jurisdictions, and rights of way — but they also carried an implicit political meaning: here one community ended and another began.

Sant Martí de Provençals, which occupied the territory of what is now the Poblenou and Eixample Est districts, resisted annexation as strenuously as it could. The town had developed rapidly as an industrial centre, and its residents feared — correctly — that absorption into Barcelona would mean higher taxes, loss of local autonomy, and the subordination of Sant Martí's interests to a city government that had its own priorities. The annexation was approved by the Spanish government over local objection in 1897.

The boundary stone predates 1897 — the inscription specifies the pre-annexation date — making it a survivor of a political geography that no longer exists. Finding it in the landscape is an encounter with a moment when the city we now take as a unified entity was still a contested frontier between competing communities. The stone did not move when the border was abolished; the border simply ceased to matter.
