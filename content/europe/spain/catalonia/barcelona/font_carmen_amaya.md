Carmen Amaya was born in 1913 in the makeshift settlement at the foot of Montjuïc known as La Perona, in the most marginal corner of a city that kept its gitano population firmly at the periphery. She began performing as a child in the taverns of Barceloneta, was discovered by impresarios who recognised something unprecedented in her technique, and by the 1930s was performing for audiences across Europe and the Americas. She spent decades in exile during the Franco years, recording and performing in Buenos Aires, New York, and Hollywood, before returning to Spain in the 1950s.

What made Amaya revolutionary in flamenco was her refusal of the gender conventions that governed the art form. Women in flamenco were expected to embody grace and restraint in the arms and torso while the footwork — the zapateado — remained a male domain. Amaya danced in trousers, executed the full percussive vocabulary that men reserved for themselves, and did so with a physical ferocity and emotional directness that left audiences without a framework for response. She had to be admired as an entirely new category.

The fountain in Barceloneta places her memorial in the neighbourhood that shaped her, a gesture of civic recognition that arrived decades after it was merited. She died in 1963 at the height of a renewed career. The area around the fountain is now gentrified almost beyond recognition; the La Perona of Amaya's childhood is entirely gone. The stone and water remain as a marker for a presence that the neighbourhood produced and then largely failed to claim.
