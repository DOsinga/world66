Hercules was a natural figure for a park that was supposed to represent the triumph of civic culture over brute force. The Ciutadella had been a military fortress for a century and a half, its very existence a reminder of the Bourbon victory of 1714 and the subjugation that followed. Demolishing the fortress and creating a park in its place was a civic act of considerable symbolic weight, and populating that park with figures of heroic labour — Hercules completing his twelve tasks — made the symbolism explicit.

The choice of Hercules also connected Barcelona's park to a long Mediterranean tradition. The hero had legendary associations with the Iberian Peninsula: ancient authors placed his pillars at the Strait of Gibraltar, and several Catalan cities claimed Herculean foundation myths. A fountain depicting his labours in a Barcelona park was both a reference to that classical heritage and a statement about the kind of civic virtue the city aspired to — strength disciplined by purpose, power in service of civilisation.

The neoclassical style of the fountain places it in the tradition of European public sculpture that dominated the period when the Ciutadella was being landscaped in the 1870s and 1880s. The style was already beginning to seem conservative by the time the park opened formally in 1888 for the Universal Exposition — modernisme was emerging as a more assertive Catalan alternative — but it suited the park's institutional ambitions.
