Snow White appeared as a fountain subject at a moment when Disney's 1937 animated film was still culturally fresh and when Spanish municipalities were seeking accessible, apolitical decorative programmes for public spaces. The Franco regime was not hostile to international popular culture when it served conservative family values — the animated fairy tale, with its emphasis on innocence, domesticity, and feminine virtue, fit the regime's ideological preferences well enough to be uncontroversial.

Placing a Disney character in a public fountain was unusual even by the standards of the period, which tended to prefer allegorical figures, civic heroes, or religious subjects for permanent street furniture. The choice reflects either a genuine affection for the story on the part of whoever commissioned it, or a practical calculation that a recognisable popular figure would attract children to the fountain and thereby animate the public space it occupied.

The fountain now reads as an artefact of a specific cultural moment — the mid-century intersection of American popular culture, European austerity, and the particular mixture of repression and escapism that characterised daily life under Francoism. Its survival as a functional fountain rather than a museum piece is partly what makes it interesting: it has outlasted the cultural context that produced it and is now simply an odd, charming object in the city.
