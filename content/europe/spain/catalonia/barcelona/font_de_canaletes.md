The legend of Canaletes is documented from the late nineteenth century: drink from the fountain and you will return to Barcelona. The origin of the belief is obscure — it may derive from earlier traditions about the spring that fed the location, or it may have been generated by the fountain's position at the top of the Rambla, where travellers departing the city would take a final drink before leaving. Whatever its source, the legend took hold and transformed a cast-iron drinking fountain into a site of genuine emotional attachment.

The name comes from the canal system that supplied water to this part of the city before the modern water network was constructed. 'Canaletes' means 'little canals' — a reference to the infrastructure that the fountain replaced, preserved in the name when the infrastructure itself disappeared. That kind of toponymic persistence is common in old cities: the name outlasts the thing named, and the fountain carries the memory of a hydraulic system that nobody alive has seen.

The football connection is modern but has layered deeply into the fountain's identity. FC Barcelona supporters began gathering at Canaletes for celebrations in the early twentieth century, and the tradition accelerated as the club's fortunes and political significance grew. For Catalans under Franco, the club was one of the few legal outlets for collective Catalan identity, and victory celebrations at Canaletes therefore carried meaning beyond sport. The fountain is now inseparable from that history of joyful resistance.
