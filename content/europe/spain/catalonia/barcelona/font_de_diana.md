Diana — Artemis to the Greeks — was the goddess of the hunt, of the moon, and of wild places: an apt presiding deity for a park that was supposed to represent nature reclaimed from military occupation. The Ciutadella park's designers in the 1870s and 1880s drew on the classical mythological repertoire to give the space an intellectual and aesthetic framework, and Diana's associations with woodland and untamed landscape made her a natural choice for a fountain in the park's green spaces.

The depiction of Diana in Western art and sculpture follows conventions established in antiquity and elaborated through the Renaissance: the goddess shown with her bow and quiver, often accompanied by a stag or a hound, always projecting an ideal of athletic feminine power that sits somewhat uneasily with the domestic allegorical nudes that populated the same parks and salons. Diana is active, independent, and dangerous — qualities that made her a favourite subject for artists who wanted to represent female energy without the conventional associations of beauty or maternity.

The Ciutadella fountain places Diana within a programme of mythological statuary that reads, across the park as a whole, as a declaration of Greco-Roman cultural heritage as the foundation of Catalan civic identity. That claim was not innocent — it was an assertion of European belonging against Spanish centralism, of Catalan distinctiveness against Castilian homogenisation — but it was made in the neutral-seeming language of classical antiquity.
