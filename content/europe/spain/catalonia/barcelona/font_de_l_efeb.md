The ephebe — ἔφηβος in Greek, the young man between puberty and full civic status — was a central category in ancient Greek thought about the body and society. The Athenian system of ephebic training produced a specific aesthetic: the youth shown between childhood's softness and the adult male's hardness, at the moment of maximal physical beauty and minimal social definition. This figure entered the Western sculptural tradition through Roman copies of Greek originals and was revived obsessively in the nineteenth century.

Catalan modernisme was drawn to the ephebe for reasons that combined aesthetic and cultural ambitions. The movement's engagement with classical Mediterranean heritage — expressed in poetry, painting, and sculpture — found in the Greek ephebe a figure that was simultaneously universal and specifically Mediterranean, belonging to the same cultural geography that the modernistes claimed as Catalonia's deepest heritage. The youth also embodied the movement's characteristic optimism about development, growth, and the perfection of human potential.

Placing an ephebe fountain in the Ciutadella park connected the work to a long tradition of classical figures in garden settings that runs from ancient Roman villa culture through Italian Renaissance gardens to the great European parks of the nineteenth century. The tradition asserted that public space was not merely recreational but educational — that walking among classical figures improved the citizens who encountered them. Whether this programme actually worked is another question, but the ambition is written in the bronze.
