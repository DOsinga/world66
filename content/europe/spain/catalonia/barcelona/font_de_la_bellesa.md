Allegorical fountains dedicated to abstract virtues and ideals — Beauty, Truth, Justice, Nature — were a standard equipment of the nineteenth-century public park throughout Europe, expressing the era's belief that aesthetic experience could improve the moral character of citizens who were exposed to it. The Ciutadella park, designed in the years after 1869 when the hated military fortress was finally demolished, was consciously programmed with this kind of didactic intent.

Beauty as a civic virtue had a specific meaning in the Catalan cultural context of the 1870s and 1880s. The modernista movement was beginning to argue that beautiful cities produced better citizens, that ornament and art were not luxuries but necessities, and that the industrial city's ugliness was a moral problem as much as an aesthetic one. A fountain dedicated to Beauty in a newly opened public park was an early expression of this argument in permanent form, a statement that the city's government accepted some responsibility for the aesthetic quality of its citizens' environment.

The Bellesa fountain has outlasted the philosophical framework that produced it. The idea that allegorical statuary improves the morals of its audience now seems quaint, and the specific visual language of nineteenth-century allegory requires a kind of cultural literacy that fewer visitors bring to the park. But the fountain continues to do what it was built to do: it marks a spot, provides water, and gives the eye something to rest on in the green space. Sometimes that is enough.
