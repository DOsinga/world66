Barcelona's late-nineteenth-century expansion required thousands of street fountains to supply fresh water to the new grid neighbourhoods, and the city used them as canvases for minor sculpture and playful naming. The Font de la Caputxeta, whose name evokes the fairy-tale girl in the red hood, belongs to a tradition of whimsical civic decoration that gave character to otherwise identical grid blocks. The name *caputxeta* is Catalan for 'little hood' or 'little cape', the same root that gives us *capuchin* and *cappuccino*.

The Eixample was designed by Ildefons Cerdà as a rational, hygienic city, but the bourgeoisie who moved into it demanded ornamentation — hence the cast-iron balconies, the mosaic pavements, and the story-named fountains that interrupted the geometry with a touch of fancy. Cast-iron drinking fountains came in catalogues from foundries in Barcelona and abroad; a municipal engineer would choose the model and the name, and the fountain would be installed and connected to the city mains.

These modest neighbourhood fountains are easy to walk past without a second glance, but together they form an open-air museum of nineteenth-century civic ironwork. Look for the drinking spout, the decorative basin, and whatever figurative detail the foundry saw fit to add — each one a small argument that a city should delight as well as function.
