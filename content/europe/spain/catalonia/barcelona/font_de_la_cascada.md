When the 1888 Universal Exhibition needed a showpiece for the repurposed Ciutadella park, the city commissioned a monumental cascade in the French style — a tiered waterfall flanked by sculpture and crowned with a triumphal arch. The project was overseen by Josep Fontserè, but a young architecture student named Antoni Gaudí worked on the hydraulic engineering of the grotto beneath. It is the earliest known physical contribution Gaudí made to the city that would later be defined by his work.

The cascade draws on the tradition of Baroque fountains from Rome and Versailles — grand, theatrical, designed to be heard as well as seen. Water tumbles down artificial rockwork into a broad pool, and the whole ensemble is populated with allegorical sculpture: Venus emerging from the waves, sea horses, tritons, and the personification of the rivers of Catalonia. It was meant to announce that Barcelona was a European capital with European ambitions.

Today the fountain is a favourite of families and weekend picnickers. The grotto underneath, where Gaudí reportedly worked on the rock formations, is worth peering into — it has the organic, cave-like quality that would later appear, magnified, in the crypt of the Colònia Güell and the naves of the Sagrada Família.
