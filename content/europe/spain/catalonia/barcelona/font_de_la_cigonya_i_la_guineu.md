The fable is simple: a fox invites a stork to dinner and serves soup in a flat dish that the stork cannot eat from; the stork retaliates by inviting the fox and serving food in a narrow-necked jar. The lesson is about the reciprocity of hospitality, the cruelty of giving help in a form the other cannot use. Placing this pairing on a street fountain is a small, pointed act of civic moralising — the kind Barcelona's nineteenth-century reformers loved.

The Barceloneta was built in the 1750s as housing for the fishermen and workers displaced when Philip V demolished the Ribera neighbourhood to build the Ciutadella citadel. It was designed as a grid of narrow streets and low houses, and it remained a working-class fishing district until very recently. A fountain with a fable about false hospitality has an edge in that context: the fishermen of Barceloneta knew about being given the wrong dish.

The Aesop tradition ran deep in civic iconography across Europe, and Barcelona's fountain-makers drew on it freely. This fountain is one of a cluster in the Barceloneta and the nearby port area that use animal figures — drawn from fable, heraldry, or natural history — to give ordinary street furniture a second layer of meaning. The stork and the fox are still there, still facing each other across the basin.
