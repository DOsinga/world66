Before running water reached apartment buildings, the neighbourhood fountain was where residents fetched drinking water, washed their hands, and sometimes their clothes. The *palangana* — a wide, shallow washbasin — was a standard feature of working-class fountains, giving this Eixample example its unpretentious, domestic name. It belongs to the category of purely functional street furniture that the city has kept in place not as monuments but as continuous civic infrastructure.

The Eixample grid was built in phases across the second half of the nineteenth century and the early twentieth, and fountain design evolved accordingly. The simpler cast-iron models, like this one, came in catalogues from iron foundries in Barcelona and abroad. Hundreds survive across the grid, each subtly different in their casting details, scattered through streets that otherwise look identical from one block to the next.

Walking the Eixample with an eye for these fountains reveals how densely the city once relied on shared public water. Today they mostly serve dog-walkers and the occasional thirsty cyclist. The *palangana* basin is usually gone — replaced by a simpler drain — but the name persists, a fossil of the fountain's original social life.
