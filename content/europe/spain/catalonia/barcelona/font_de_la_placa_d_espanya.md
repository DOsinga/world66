When Barcelona hosted the 1929 International Exhibition on Montjuïc, the city needed a monumental gateway to frame the approach. Plaça d'Espanya was that gateway, and at its centre the city placed a fountain designed by Josep Maria Jujol, one of the most gifted collaborators of Antoni Gaudí. The fountain is explicitly Roman in its references — a circular basin with triumphal figures, allegories of the three great rivers of Spain (the Ebro, the Tagus, and the Guadalquivir), and a vertical tower that echoes the obelisks of Rome and the columns of Baroque squares.

Jujol is best known for his ceramic work on Gaudí's buildings — the sinuous, mosaic-encrusted benches of Park Güell are substantially his work — but the Plaça d'Espanya fountain shows him working in a more classical register. The brief demanded something legible to an international audience, and Jujol delivered allegory in stone and water, a language every European visitor of 1929 would have understood.

The square itself is confusing to navigate today — the traffic is relentless and the scale overwhelming — but if you stand at the fountain and look up the avenue toward the Palau Nacional, you see the exhibition's original axis intact: a procession of light towers, cascading fountains on the hillside, and the palace at the summit. The 1929 Exhibition was Barcelona's coming-out party as a modern European city, and this fountain was its front door.
