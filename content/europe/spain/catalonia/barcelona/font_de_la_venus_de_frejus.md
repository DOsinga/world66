The Venus de Fréjus is a Hellenistic marble statue found in the Roman town of Forum Julii (modern Fréjus, in Provence) and now housed in the Louvre. It belongs to the tradition of Aphrodite figures derived from Praxiteles, and reproductions of it spread across Europe as neoclassicism demanded ideal female figures for gardens, fountains, and civic spaces. Barcelona's version arrived in the Gràcia neighbourhood during the period when Gràcia was still an independent town — before its annexation by Barcelona in 1897 — and its presence reflects the civic ambition of a prosperous mercantile community.

Gràcia in the nineteenth century was a town of textile workers, craftsmen, and bourgeois families who had made money in the colonies, particularly Cuba. They built their ateneus, their casinos, and their squares with genuine civic pride, commissioning sculptures and fountains that announced their town's cultural aspirations. A cast of a Louvre original was a statement: we know our classics, we are Europeans, we belong to the same world as Paris.

The fountain today sits in one of Gràcia's quieter corners, less visited than the famous Plaça del Sol or Plaça de la Vila de Gràcia. That relative obscurity makes it the better find: a piece of the ancient Mediterranean transplanted into a Barcelona neighbourhood by people who wanted their home to feel connected to the deep history of the continent.
