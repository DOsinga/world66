The Three Graces — Euphrosyne (joy), Aglaea (radiance), and Thalia (flourishing) — are the handmaidens of Venus in classical mythology, and they appear in some of the most celebrated images in Western art: Botticelli's *Primavera*, Raphael's painting in Chantilly, Canova's famous marble. Their interlinked pose symbolised the flow of generosity: giving, receiving, and returning. The Renaissance philosophers who revived the image made it a diagram of how gift exchange should work in a civilised society.

Barcelona's version, placed at the edge of the medieval quarter, belongs to the nineteenth-century habit of furnishing the city with mythological reference points. As the old walls came down and the city expanded, fountains and sculptures were installed to assert that Barcelona was a city with classical roots — heir to Rome and Greece, not merely a medieval Catalan town. The Graces, with their associations with femininity, generosity, and civic virtue, were a natural choice for public water.

The fountain is modest in scale — this is not a grand piazza installation — but the subject matter is ambitious. Three linked figures, water flowing between them: it is a working diagram of civic reciprocity, the idea that the city's public goods pass from hand to hand and should be returned in kind.
