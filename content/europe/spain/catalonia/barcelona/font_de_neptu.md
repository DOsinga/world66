Barcelona's relationship with the sea is one of the most complex in Mediterranean history. The city turned its back on the beach for most of its existence — the waterfront was industrial, malodorous, and associated with the dangerous social margins of dock workers and sailors — while simultaneously building its wealth on maritime commerce. Neptune, the god who personified that ambivalence, appears repeatedly in Barcelona's public art: dominating, unpredictable, essential.

The city's first Neptune fountain dates to the eighteenth century, when the port's expansion brought renewed attention to the maritime identity of a city that had, in the previous century, seen its commercial primacy shift decisively toward Castile and the Atlantic. Invoking Neptune was a way of reasserting that the Mediterranean trade routes still mattered, that Barcelona's future remained tied to the sea, even as the global economy was reorganising itself around the Americas.

Public Neptune fountains work as civic declarations as much as water features: this city knows the sea, claims it, and is not intimidated by it. The scale of the figure and the assertiveness of the posture in most Neptune fountain programmes express a relationship of command rather than deference toward the water. How accurately that relationship described Barcelona's actual situation at any given moment is a question the fountain cannot answer.
