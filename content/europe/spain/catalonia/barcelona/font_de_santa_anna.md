Santa Anna is an extraordinary survival: a Romanesque-Gothic church and cloister from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries sitting in a small square just two blocks from the Rambla, almost unknown to the tourists who walk past its entrance. The fountain in the square in front of it shares that quality of unexpected quiet, an amenity for a neighbourhood within the neighbourhood that has existed on this spot since the medieval period.

The church gave the square and the street their name, and the street gave its name to the fountain — a chain of nomination that is typical of how old-city Barcelona layers its history. Nothing in this corner of the Barri Gòtic is exactly what it seems from the outside: the medieval church survived by becoming Anglican for a period after the liberal suppressions, the square that looks ancient was substantially modified in the nineteenth century, and the fountain that seems incidental is documented from a period when this was one of the few reliable water sources for residents of several surrounding blocks.

Drinking at a fountain near Santa Anna church in the medieval city was an experience shared by people who would be unrecognisable to each other across time — Roman legionaries, medieval merchants, early modern artisans, nineteenth-century immigrants. The continuity of the water source, if not the fountain itself, is one of those threads through urban history that the city's surface transformations cannot entirely cut.
