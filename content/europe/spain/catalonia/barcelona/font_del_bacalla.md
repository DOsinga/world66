Salt cod — *bacallà* in Catalan — was the protein of the Mediterranean poor for five centuries. Caught in the North Atlantic by Basque and later Catalan fishermen, dried and salted aboard ship, it arrived in Barcelona's port already preserved and could be stored through summer without refrigeration. It was cheap, filling, and available year-round in a city where fresh fish was expensive and seasonal. The Ribera neighbourhood, just behind the port, was where the trade was concentrated: warehouses, curing facilities, and the pungent smell that made the area unmistakable.

The market of Santa Caterina, which gave the neighbourhood its modern name, was built on the site of a Dominican convent and became one of the city's main food markets. The cod trade that filled its stalls and the surrounding streets was so dominant that the local fountain acquired the fish's name. The font del bacallà was where market traders, dockers, and neighbourhood residents drew water — a functional object labelled by the trade that surrounded it.

The current market building was redesigned by Enric Miralles in the early 2000s and topped with his famous undulating ceramic roof. The old fountain, however, connects the site to a much older economy, one in which the North Atlantic and the Mediterranean were linked by the humble, pungent, indispensable cod.
