*Castellers* — the builders of human towers — are one of the most distinctive expressions of Catalan popular culture. Teams called *colles* compete to build the tallest and most complex towers: a wide base of hundreds of tightly packed bodies, rising through layers of climbers to the *enxaneta*, the small child who crowns the tower and raises a hand to signal success. The tradition originated in the Camp de Tarragona region in the late eighteenth century and spread across Catalonia, reaching Barcelona with the waves of migration that filled the new working-class neighbourhoods.

The Poblenou was a neighbourhood of factories and workers' housing — Barcelona's industrial heartland in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — and it developed a strong popular culture that included *castellers*, *gegants* (giant carnival figures), and the dense associational life of the Catalan labour movement. A fountain celebrating *castellers* in this neighbourhood is a statement about local identity: this is a place with deep roots in Catalan popular tradition, not just an industrial suburb.

The UNESCO recognition in 2010 was a milestone in the international visibility of *castellera* culture, but the tradition had been central to Catalan life for two centuries before that. The fountain in Poblenou belongs to a network of street monuments across Barcelona that insist on the vitality of popular culture as a form of civic identity — as important as the opera house or the art museum.
