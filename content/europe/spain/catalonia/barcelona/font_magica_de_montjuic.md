The Magic Fountain was designed by Carles Buïgas and built in just under a year for the 1929 International Exposition — a feat of engineering improvisation that required installing 3,620 nozzles, 4,500 lightbulbs, and 50 km of electrical cable beneath the hill in a construction period that Buïgas himself considered impossibly short. The opening night show on May 19, 1929 reportedly brought King Alfonso XIII to tears, which was either an endorsement of the design or a reflection of the occasion's emotional weight.

The fountain had a troubled middle life. Under Franco it operated intermittently, was allowed to deteriorate, and fell into extended disuse during the austerity years. Its revival as a regular spectacle came in the 1980s and accelerated after the Olympics brought international attention to Barcelona's public spaces. The modern shows add synchronised music to Buïgas's water and light effects — a practice he would almost certainly have approved, given how theatrical his original conception was.

What Buïgas had grasped, and what has ensured the fountain's survival through a century of changing tastes and political circumstances, is that water and light together produce effects that no static object can match. The fountain is different every second, which means that no photograph exhausts it and no single visit fully captures it. That inexhaustibility is the key to its durability as a public attraction: it rewards return.
