The Passeig de Gràcia was laid out in the 1820s as a tree-lined promenade connecting Barcelona to the village of Gràcia — then a separate municipality — and from the beginning it was designed for walking and display rather than commerce or traffic. The fountains placed along its length were functional amenities in the sense that they provided drinking water, but they were also deliberate ornaments, the kind of cast-iron street furniture that Paris and London were deploying along their new boulevards and that Barcelona's civic authorities wanted to match.

The nineteenth-century fountains preceded the modernista mansions that now define the avenue's identity. When Domènech i Montaner and Gaudí and Puig i Cadafalch were building their celebrated blocks along the Passeig de Gràcia in the 1890s and 1900s, the street furniture was already in place. The fountains are therefore part of an earlier urban vision: the city of bourgeois promenading, of gas lamps and carriages and Sunday afternoon display, that modernisme would both celebrate and transform.

Most visitors to the Passeig de Gràcia now navigate it as an open-air museum of modernista architecture, moving between Casa Batlló, Casa Milà, and the Manzana de la Discordia. The fountains are walked past rather than noticed. Attending to them is a way of recovering the earlier idea of the avenue — the idea that preceded the famous facades and that the facades were built to complement.
