The fountain itself is modest — a small basin built into the wall at the corner of La Rambla and Carrer de la Portaferrissa. But look at the ceramic tiles above the spout. They were installed in 1959, designed by Joan Baptista Guivernau, and they show the Portaferrissa gate as it looked in the 18th century: merchants, water-carriers, a city going about its business through an arch that no longer stands.

The gate this fountain is named for — Porta Ferrissa, the Iron Gate — was one of the main entrances through Barcelona's medieval walls. The fountain has been here in one form or another since 1604, when it stood on Carrer del Carme; it was moved to this spot in 1680 when the Jesuits needed the original site for a new church. For centuries the basin was where residents came to fill their jugs. By the mid-1700s, vendors had set up beside it selling anise and sweetened water — a practice that spread to fountains across the city.

What you are looking at is a palimpsest: a 17th-century fountain, decorated with 20th-century tiles, showing a scene from the 18th century, on a street that was once a medieval gate. Every layer is still there if you take thirty seconds to look.
