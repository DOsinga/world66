Tanning — the process of converting raw animal hides into leather — was one of the most important and most repellent trades in medieval cities. It required large quantities of water, urine, oak bark, and animal brains, and it produced a smell that could overwhelm a neighbourhood. Medieval Barcelona, like every other city, confined the tanners to a specific district near running water, usually at the city's edge. The stream — the *rec* — that ran along what is now the Via Laietana supplied the tanneries of the Ribera and Gothic Quarter with the water they needed.

The twin fountains marked the boundary or the entrance of this district, and their pairing may have served a practical purpose: one for drinking, one for washing, or one for each side of a gateway. Twin fountain arrangements were common in Barcelona, often placed at the entrances to markets, squares, or neighbourhood boundaries. They created a sense of threshold — you were entering a distinct zone of the city.

The tanneries are long gone, replaced by the medieval buildings that tourists now photograph in the Gothic Quarter, but the fountains remain as a pair. They are the kind of city furniture that outlasts the function it was built to serve, surviving because they are useful as water sources long after the original reason for their placement has been forgotten.
