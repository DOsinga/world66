On 11 September 1714, after a fourteen-month siege, the forces of Philip V entered Barcelona and ended the War of the Spanish Succession. The Catalans had backed the losing side — the Habsburg claimant — and the price was the abolition of Catalan institutions, the imposition of Castilian law, and the demolition of the Ribera neighbourhood to build a citadel. The defenders who died in the final assault were buried in the graveyard beside Santa Maria del Mar: the Fossar de les Moreres, named for the mulberry trees that grew there.

An inscription at the site — written by the nineteenth-century poet Frederic Soler — reads: *En el Fossar de les Moreres no s'hi enterra cap traïdor* ('In the Fossar de les Moreres no traitor is buried'). It refers to a legend that the gravedigger refused to bury a man who had collaborated with the Bourbon forces. The phrase became a rallying cry of Catalan nationalism, and 11 September is now the Catalan national day, the *Diada*.

The site was redesigned in 1989 with a sunken plaza, a flame that burns continuously, and a red-painted ground that evokes blood. It is an intensely charged place — Catalans come here to lay flowers on the *Diada* and to mark other moments of political significance. For visitors, it is one of the most legible monuments in the city: the past is not past here, and the stone makes that unmistakably clear.
