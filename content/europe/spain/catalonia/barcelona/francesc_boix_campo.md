Inside Mauthausen's photographic laboratory, Boix and a small group of Republican prisoners copied SS negatives that documented camp conditions, prisoner labour, and deaths. They passed the copies to Spanish Republican women living in Mauthausen village, who concealed them through multiple Gestapo searches until Allied liberation in 1945. When Nuremberg prosecutors needed documentation of Mauthausen atrocities, those images — the camp's own record-keeping turned against it — were ready.

In 1946 Boix testified at Nuremberg in French, authenticating the photographs before the tribunal. He was 25, the only Spaniard to appear as a witness. Spain under Franco had no standing in the Allied proceedings, and Spanish Republicans existed in a diplomatic limbo the trial briefly illuminated. Boix died in Paris in 1951, aged 30, before any of the recognition that would arrive decades later.

The brass stone in the pavement here is part of Gunter Demnig's Stolpersteine project, which has installed over 100,000 such markers across Europe. The design is a deliberate inversion: instead of looking up at a monument, you look down at a name set flush in ordinary pavement at the last address where the person lived freely.
