The Colombian sculptor Fernando Botero is famous for his inflated figures — humans, horses, birds, everything rendered with the proportions of a balloon animal, somehow both comic and imposing. This bronze cat, installed in 2003 in the Rambla del Raval, is a characteristic Botero: enormous, placid, its haunches filling half the boulevard. It is also beloved. Locals call it simply El Gat — the cat.

The Rambla del Raval did not exist until 2000, when a block of densely-packed housing was demolished to create a pedestrian promenade in a neighbourhood that had almost no open public space. The Raval was historically Barcelona's most crowded and impoverished district, the place to which the city's immigrant workers, sailors, prostitutes, and artisans were confined by economics and geography. The new boulevard was part of a long urban renewal effort that produced a mixed response: some welcomed the light and the sculpture; others noted that the demolition had displaced many of the neighbourhood's remaining old residents.

Botero donated the cat to the city. A very similar Botero cat in Medellín, Colombia was destroyed in a 1995 bomb attack by the Medellín Cartel; rather than remove the fragments, the city left them in place and installed the new cat alongside them as a memorial. The Barcelona cat carries no such history of violence, but it arrived with that context already attached to its species. Children climb on it. People rub its nose for luck. The patina on the nose is bright from handling, the darkest point of a very dark sculpture.
