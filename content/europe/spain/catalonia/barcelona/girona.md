Girona and Barcelona share a relationship that is older than either of their current roles: for much of the medieval period, the two cities were the twin pillars of Catalan political life, each with a Jewish quarter, a cathedral, and a tradition of mercantile wealth that helped fund the Aragonese Crown of the Mediterranean. Girona was, in fact, the seat of the heir apparent to the Catalan crown — the Infante bore the title Prince of Girona, an honorific preserved to this day in the Spanish royal house.

This artwork in Barcelona functions as a kind of formal fraternal greeting — a reminder that Catalonia's cultural depth is not concentrated in the capital alone. Girona's old city, with its Romanesque cathedral rising above the river Onyar, its Jewish quarter among the best preserved in Europe, and its position on the Via Augusta running from Rome to Cadiz, has a claim to historical significance that Barcelona's size can sometimes obscure.

The tribute piece sits modestly in the urban fabric, not demanding attention but rewarding it. It is the kind of civic gesture that Barcelona has become skilled at making: acknowledging a wider Catalonia even from the centre of the metropolis.
