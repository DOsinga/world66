On 7 November 1893, an anarchist named Santiago Salvador threw two Orfila bombs from the upper gallery of the Liceu into the audience below during a performance of William Tell. Twenty people died. The attack was part of a wave of anarchist violence in Barcelona that also included the bombing of the Corpus Christi procession the following year, and it crystallised the class tensions that ran through the city's rapid industrialisation: the Liceu was the opera house of the bourgeoisie, and Salvador meant it that way.

The theatre survived the 1893 bombing and was rebuilt; it did not survive a fire in 1994, which gutted the auditorium completely. The reconstruction that reopened in 1999 preserved the original facade on Las Ramblas while building an entirely new stage tower and backstage complex behind it. The new auditorium replicates the original's horseshoe form and gilded decoration almost exactly, because the subscribers and patrons who funded the reconstruction wanted continuity above all.

The Liceu today presents a full season from September to July and is technically one of the best-equipped opera stages in Europe. The foyer, rebuilt to match the original, retains the slightly airless grandeur of 19th-century bourgeois culture — which, after everything, feels more honest than pretending otherwise.
