The Hivernacle's iron frame is its most honest feature. Built by Josep Amargós in 1888 as the warm greenhouse component of the exhibition's botanical display — its counterpart, the Umbracle, was an open-slatted timber structure for palms that still stands nearby — the building makes no pretence of being anything other than a light-industrial structure adapted for plants. The slender cast-iron columns, the barrel-vaulted glass roof, and the brick side walls with their arched windows are the vernacular of the Victorian greenhouse translated into a civic register.

The 1888 Universal Exhibition transformed the Ciutadella Park, which had been a military citadel demolished in 1869 after decades of popular agitation. The park itself was designed by Fontserè in the 1870s; the exhibition's architects — Domènech i Montaner for the Tres Dragons, Amargós for the Hivernacle — worked within that framework and left Barcelona with several structures that outlasted the event. The Hivernacle was retained as a municipal greenhouse after the exhibition, supplying plants to the city's parks for much of the early 20th century.

The current use as a concert venue is a reasonable adaptation — the acoustics are imperfect but the visual effect of a candlelit or spot-lit performance inside the glass vault is considerable — though it has gradually displaced the building's function as a plant house. The iron structure has been conserved rather than restored, and the patination of the columns and the original fittings repay close examination by anyone with an interest in 19th-century industrial craftsmanship.
