Every year on April 23rd, the feast day of Sant Jordi (Saint George), Catalonia celebrates with books and roses. Men give women roses; women give men books — or so the tradition goes, though in practice anyone gives anything to anyone they care for, and the city fills with stalls selling both. The date is significant: April 23rd is also the date on which Cervantes and Shakespeare both died, in 1616, which is why UNESCO chose it for World Book Day. But Catalonia was doing this already.

The mosaic on the pavement honouring this tradition is one of several pieces of public art that Barcelona has dedicated to its literary and cultural customs. The Sant Jordi association of books with the defence of culture is old — the saint who killed the dragon and rescued the princess was adopted as a patron of Catalonia in the 14th century, and the rose that sprang from the dragon's blood became the saint's emblem. The book is a more recent addition, but it has become inseparable.

On Sant Jordi's Day the Rambla de Catalunya and the Passeig de Gràcia become outdoor book fairs. Publishers launch their major titles on this date; writers sign copies; crowds move slowly between stands. It is one of the few days when a city that can feel ruthlessly commercial turns genuinely literary for a few hours.
