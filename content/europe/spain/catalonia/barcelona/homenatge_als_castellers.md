Building a human tower requires a minimum of four people and can involve several hundred. The pinya — the base — consists of the heaviest and most experienced participants, their bodies interlocked to distribute weight. Above them come successive smaller layers, called pisos, each person standing on the shoulders of those below, until the enxaneta — a child of perhaps six or seven years old — reaches the top, raises four fingers to signal a clean climb, and begins the careful descent.

The tradition originated in the late 18th century around Valls, in the Tarragona area, as a competitive display between local colles — teams that still bear the names of their towns and compete fiercely. The competition spread through Catalonia and became a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2010. The governing competition is held every two years in Tarragona's bullring, now used exclusively for this purpose.

This monument in Barcelona, separate from the Font dels Castellers, reflects how the city has adopted a tradition that originated elsewhere in Catalonia and made it part of its own civic identity. The castellers perform at major festivals; the training is year-round; the youngest members begin learning their roles almost before they can walk.
