You might walk into Plaça de Sant Miquel — the square between City Hall and the police headquarters — and think you are looking at a piece of industrial scaffolding left by someone who forgot to come back for it. Look again. Antoni Llena's sculpture, inaugurated on 12 February 2012 (Sant Eulàlia's Day, when castellers traditionally perform in the city), is 26.5 metres of interwoven stainless steel tubes rising from a concrete base you can walk beneath.

Llena took his conceptual cue from the wire mesh around wine bottles — that lattice of cohesion that holds many thin strands together into something that cannot be pulled apart easily. The same logic governs the castell: not one person, but dozens, each in a specific position in a structure that can only stand if everyone holds. The tubes rise, diverge, rejoin, and rise again, mimicking the stages of a human tower being built. It is a monument to a collective act.

Castellers — the teams who build these towers — were declared an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO in 2010, two years before this sculpture was installed. It went up in the exact square where the towers are traditionally raised during the city's festa major. Stand under it and look up: the steel tubes converge overhead into something that looks, from directly below, like a crown.
