The building at La Rambla 109 was constructed in 1880 to house the Compañía General de Tabacos de Filipinas, a state-backed monopoly that controlled tobacco production and trade across the Philippine archipelago. The business was enormously profitable and the building reflects it: a solid, self-satisfied neoclassical block that announced colonial wealth on Barcelona's premier promenade. The company's capital came directly from extracted Philippine labour and resources, funnelled back to shareholders in Barcelona and Madrid.

In 1898 Spain lost Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam in the space of a few months following defeat in the Spanish-American War. The Compañía General de Tabacos de Filipinas continued trading but the colonial logic that built this headquarters had collapsed. When the building was converted into a hotel a century later, the owners named it Hotel 1898 — choosing the year of Spain's imperial disaster rather than softening it with any other reference. In Spanish cultural memory, *el desastre del 98* is shorthand for the end of an era, a trauma that generated an entire literary generation (the *Generación del 98*) and redirected Spanish intellectual life.

Standing on La Rambla, the facade gives no outward sign of this history. What you see is a handsome 19th-century commercial building, well maintained and busy with hotel guests. The name does the interpretive work: every guest who books a room is, whether they know it or not, sleeping inside an act of colonial accounting and its reckoning.
