When Ildefons Cerdà designed the Eixample in 1859-1860, each block was intended to have buildings on only two of its four sides, with the remaining two sides left as garden and community space. The chamfered corners — the distinctive diagonal cuts at Eixample intersections — were designed partly to create miniature corner plazas. The interior of each block was supposed to be accessible communal garden.

None of this happened as planned. Private developers built on all four sides of the block as soon as the regulations permitted, then on the interior spaces as well, and finally in the interior of the courtyard itself. By 1900, most Eixample blocks were completely built out with buildings on all sides and interior spaces occupied by workshops, storage, and more buildings. The communal interior gardens existed only in Cerdà's plans.

Since the 1990s, Barcelona has been recovering interior block spaces by purchasing and demolishing some interior structures. The results — small communal gardens accessible from the surrounding streets through gates in the block perimeter — are still called illes interiors (interior islands) after Cerdà's original concept. Most are open to the neighbourhood during daytime hours. They are deeply ordinary as gardens — benches, grass, trees — but their existence in the Eixample blocks is a partial vindication of a planning concept that the market destroyed 130 years ago. The superilla (super-block) programme launched in 2016 continues in the same spirit, reclaiming street space from cars for pedestrian use.
