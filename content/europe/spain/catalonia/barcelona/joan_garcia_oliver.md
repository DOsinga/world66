Joan Garcia Oliver was one of the most remarkable figures thrown up by the Spanish Civil War: a cabinetmaker from Reus, a lifelong anarchist, a leader of the Confederación Nacional del Trabajo, who in November 1936 became Minister of Justice of the Republican government — a government of a state that his ideology told him should not exist.

He accepted the post on the grounds that the war demanded it, reformed the prison system along progressive lines, and throughout his tenure refused to draw a ministerial salary, living instead on the wage of a skilled worker. His tenure lasted until May 1937, when the street fighting in Barcelona — the so-called "May Days" — shattered the revolutionary coalition and the CNT was removed from government.

Garcia Oliver spent the rest of his long life in exile, dying in Guadalajara, Mexico, in 1980 — five years after Franco, long enough to see Spain return to democracy but not long enough to see a memorial placed in his name in the city he had helped defend. The placing of that memorial is itself part of the story of what Barcelona chose to remember once it had the freedom to choose.
