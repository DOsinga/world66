The fin de siècle was obsessed with youth. The Symbolists, the Secessionists, and the Modernistas all returned repeatedly to the young body as a symbol of vitality, potentiality, and the future. In Barcelona, where Modernisme was at its height between 1890 and 1910, this obsession found architectural expression in the flowing organic forms of Domènech i Montaner and Puig i Cadafalch and sculptural expression in the figures that decorated their buildings and the city's public spaces.

A sculpture called *Joventut* in the Eixample fits precisely into this tradition. Youth was not simply a demographic category; it was an ideology — an argument that the new century would be different from the old, that energy and creativity and forward movement were values, that the past was to be respectfully superseded. The Eixample itself, Cerdà's rational grid expanding outward from the old city, was an embodiment of this ideology: new, light, scientific, modern.

The figure that carries this title would have been understood by its original audience as participating in a specific discourse about the city's identity and ambitions. Barcelona in the Modernista period saw itself as a young European capital making its claim on the future. The sculpture is a small part of that argument, placed in the new city that the argument was building.
