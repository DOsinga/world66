Fishermen have always read the sky. Before barometers and weather forecasts, the flight behaviour of birds was one of the primary ways of reading approaching weather. The *au dels temporals* — the storm bird, usually identified as a petrel or similar seabird — appears close to the coast when pressure drops and severe weather is approaching. Fishermen knew this; they had encoded it in lore passed from father to son. A sculpture bearing this name in the Barceloneta, Barcelona's fishing district, is a monument to that knowledge: the empirical, accumulated wisdom of people who worked at sea.

Josep Viladomat was one of the most significant Catalan sculptors of the mid-twentieth century, a student of Josep Llimona and a practitioner of the humanist figurative tradition that continued to work in Barcelona even as international modernism moved toward abstraction. His public works are found across the city, and the *Au dels Temporals* is among his most evocative: a bird, poised, facing the Mediterranean, the source and the danger.

The Barceloneta fishing community has been diminishing for decades; the last working fishing boats still put out from the port, but the neighbourhood has been transformed by tourism and gentrification. The sculpture faces a sea that is now lined with beach bars rather than net-drying racks. But the bird's posture — watchful, alert to what the weather is doing — preserves a memory of when this coast was read for survival.
