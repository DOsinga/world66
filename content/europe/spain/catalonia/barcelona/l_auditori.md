The main auditorium's acoustic ceiling is not fixed: it rises and lowers on motorised suspensions to tune the reverberation time for different repertoire, from Baroque chamber music to full symphony orchestra. Most visitors never look up far enough to notice.

Rafael Moneo won the Pritzker Prize in 1996, three years before L'Auditori opened, and the building reads as a direct rebuttal of the Barcelona model of civic architecture — the expectation, sharpened by the 1992 Olympics, that public buildings should announce themselves with glass and movement. Moneo instead designed an opaque limestone-clad box, its main facade turning away from the street and presenting a near-blank wall to the Avinguda Meridiana. Critics called it anti-urban; defenders called it honest: a concert hall is an acoustic instrument, and acoustic instruments are sealed.

The building shares its block with Ricardo Bofill's Teatre Nacional de Catalunya, completed two years earlier. The contrast is pointed: Bofill's building is a glass-walled temple on a raised podium, a classical citation staged for maximum visibility; Moneo's sits lower, quieter, and inward-facing. Together they form an unintentional debate about what public architecture owes the street. The Museu de la Música occupies the building's lower levels, its collection including instruments from across five centuries — easy to miss if you came for the concert.
