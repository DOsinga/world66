When Barcelona prepared for the 1992 Olympics, the city demolished the *barraquisme* — the shantytown — that had grown up on the Barceloneta beach and along the waterfront. The Barceloneta's beachfront had accumulated decades of illegal structures, including bars, restaurants, and the beloved *xiringuitos* (beach shacks) that had served the neighbourhood. They were cleared to create the new promenade and beach that the city wanted to present to the world. Rebecca Horn's *L'Estel Ferit* (The Wounded Star) was installed as part of the Olympic public art programme, and it was an act of artistic resistance.

Horn positioned four rusting iron cages — stacked in a precarious tower — on the beach precisely where the demolished structures had stood. The cages are crumbling, deliberately allowed to oxidise; the star shape at their apex is damaged, *ferit* (wounded). The work refuses to celebrate the transformation of the waterfront. Instead it mourns what was lost: the informal, working-class beach culture that had existed before the Olympics arrived and replaced it with a designed, international leisure landscape.

Horn is a German artist associated with the body and with mechanical extensions of the body — her earlier work used prostheses, hair extensions, and machines that touched and threatened. The cages of *L'Estel Ferit* continue this interest: they are structures that could contain a body, or that once contained a life. The rust is the point. Things are allowed to decay here, to mark absence, in a city that was otherwise in the business of polishing everything to a shine.
