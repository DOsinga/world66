Sergi Aguilar is one of the most important Catalan sculptors of the generation that emerged after Franco, and his work consistently engages with weight, stillness, and the relationship between object and site. A boat — the fundamental vehicle of seafaring Mediterranean cultures — placed on dry land in the interior of a city is a displacement that changes both the object and the place. The boat becomes a monument; the street becomes, for a moment, a shore.

Barcelona is a maritime city that turned its back on the sea for much of the industrial era. The old port was separated from the city by the railway line and the industrial waterfront; it was not until the 1992 Olympics that the seafront was opened up and the beach became accessible. The Gothic Quarter, where *La Barca* stands, is the oldest part of the city — the medieval core where the Roman settlement grew into the medieval trading city. A boat here is historically apt: this was the city that built the ships and financed the voyages. But it is also estranged from its element in a way that makes you think about what a city has forgotten about its own origins.

Aguilar's boat is not a realistic representation; it is abstracted, simplified, made from stone rather than wood — a material that cannot float, that anchors rather than moves. The paradox is the work: a boat that cannot sail is a boat that can only mean. It means the sea, and the city's relationship to the sea, and the distance between where you are standing and the water that made the city possible.
