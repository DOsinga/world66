The name comes from the novella *La Ben Plantada* written by Eugeni d'Ors in 1911, where Teresa — "the well-planted one" — stood as an allegory for the rooted, dignified Catalan spirit. D'Ors intended her as a counterpoint to the decadence of Modernisme, a calm Mediterranean ideal grounded in tradition rather than ornament. That the city would eventually give her sculptural form is a measure of how deeply the archetype took hold in Catalan cultural imagination.

The sculpture sits on Avinguda de Gaudí, the axis that connects the two great Modernista landmarks of the Eixample: the Sagrada Família and the Hospital de Sant Pau. Placing her here, between those two monumental works, is no accident — she becomes a kind of guardian of that corridor of dreams, a human-scaled counterpoint to spires and towers.

Walking past, most visitors mistake her for a generic civic ornament. Knowing who she is — a character from a manifesto disguised as a novel, a political idea given flesh — changes the encounter entirely. She is one of those rare Barcelona monuments that rewards the curious.
