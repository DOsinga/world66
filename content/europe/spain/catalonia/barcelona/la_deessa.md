The original *Deessa* — "the goddess" — arrived in Barcelona from Rome in 1680, a gift intended to adorn the fountain of the Pla de Palau near the port. She spent centuries moving: repositioned as the city expanded, sheltered during the Civil War, and finally settled in the Plaça de Sant Miquel inside the city hall compound before a copy was made for public display. The version in the Plaça de la Vila de Madrid is that copy, installed in 1954, while the original Roman bronze stays protected indoors.

The square itself is remarkable for reasons underground rather than above: it contains an exposed section of a Roman necropolis, discovered during post-war construction works. Looking down through the glass-protected trench beside the church, you can see the tombs of citizens who buried their dead, as Roman law required, outside the city walls — which ran precisely where you are standing.

The goddess presides over this layering of time without much ceremony, a baroque figure standing above Roman graves in a city that has never quite decided which era it belongs to.
