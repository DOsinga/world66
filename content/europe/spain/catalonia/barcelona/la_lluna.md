Barcelona's policy of embedding contemporary sculpture in neighbourhood squares rather than reserving public art for grand avenues was one of the defining urban strategies of the democratic era after Franco. The city's councillors believed — and to a considerable extent proved — that living around art changes how people relate to their neighbourhood. *La Lluna*, the Moon, is a product of that philosophy: a piece placed not to impress tourists but to give the people of the Eixample and Gràcia border zone something to orient themselves by.

The title points toward the nocturnal and the tidal, forces that act on bodies without being visible themselves. Abstract sculpture in public space often struggles to communicate without explanatory text, but the moon is a subject so universally felt that even a suggestion of its form — its smoothness, its roundness, its quiet authority — tends to land.

The full stop that appears in the official title — *La Lluna.* — is unusual enough to feel deliberate, as if the sculptor wanted to signal a complete thought: this is the moon. End of sentence. Nothing else needs adding.
