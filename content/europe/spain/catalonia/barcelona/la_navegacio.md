Barcelona's relationship with the sea has been the defining tension of its history. The medieval city grew rich on Mediterranean trade, the port shaped the city's layout, and yet for much of the modern era the waterfront was an industrial zone that cut citizens off from the water. The 1992 Olympics changed that — the beaches were built, the port's historic core was opened up — but the symbolic rehabilitation of the sea as part of Barcelona's self-image had been accumulating in public art for decades before.

*La Navegació* belongs to that tradition of maritime allegory that runs through the city's monuments. Navigation as a concept carries layers of meaning: the literal skill of finding your way across water, the metaphorical sense of steering through difficulty, and the historical fact that it was Catalan sailors, merchants, and cartographers who mapped much of the medieval Mediterranean.

The figure faces in the direction that matters: toward the water, toward the horizon, toward the places that Catalan ships once reached.
