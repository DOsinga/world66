The interior of La Paloma — the painted ceilings, the mirrored walls, the wrought ironwork — has not been significantly altered since 1903. What was built for factory workers and seamstresses to dance on Saturday nights survives in the same visual form that hosted sardanes, later rock concerts, and later still drag nights. The discontinuity is in the programming, not the building.

The ballroom was the explicitly working-class side of a cultural divide. Barcelona's bourgeoisie had the Liceu opera house and the Palau de la Música; the Raval had La Paloma. The distinction was never absolute — cabaret and jazz broke across class lines — but La Paloma's geography, pricing, and programming placed it clearly on one side. Over a century of continuous operation, that clarity gave it an identity that proved difficult to replace.

It closed as a regular ballroom in 2009, the victim of noise ordinances and a changing neighbourhood. Since then it has operated sporadically as an events venue while a prolonged argument continues about what it should permanently become — luxury apartments, a hotel, a publicly supported cultural space, or something else. The city has not resolved the question. The intact interior makes the stakes higher than they would be for a derelict building.
