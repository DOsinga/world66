In the fishing community of the Barceloneta, women's domestic labour was as economically essential as the men's work at sea, and lacemaking was one of the primary ways that income entered households between catches. The craft — *punta* or *punt de coixí* in Catalan — required patience, precision, and dozens of small wooden bobbins manipulated in complex crossing patterns over a padded pillow. Girls learned it young, practiced it all their lives, and produced lace that moved through Barcelona's merchant networks into wider trade.

The word *puntaire* means lacemaker, and it carries a specifically feminine social meaning: these were women whose skilled work was chronically undervalued precisely because it happened inside homes rather than in factories or markets. Commissioning a public sculpture in their honour is a form of restitution — placing their labour in the visible, official category of things the city considers worth remembering.

The piece sits in the area where the Barceloneta meets the broader Poblenou waterfront, a neighbourhood that has undergone more radical transformation than almost anywhere else in the city. The lacemakers' Barcelona no longer exists materially. The sculpture is what's left.
