The word *rambla* comes from the Arabic *raml*, meaning sandy riverbed, and that is exactly what this was: a seasonal watercourse that ran outside the medieval city walls. When the walls were demolished and the city expanded in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the dried riverbed became a path, then a promenade, then the symbolic centre of Barcelona's public life. The name is now generic — Spain has many ramblas — but Barcelona's is the original reference point.

The street runs through several distinct sections, each with its own character and historical associations: the Rambla de Canaletes at the top, where a drinking fountain has been the traditional gathering point for celebrating FC Barcelona victories; the Rambla dels Ocells, where bird sellers kept stalls for generations; the Rambla de les Flors, still lined with flower stalls; the Rambla dels Caputxins in front of the Liceu opera house; and the Rambla de Santa Mònica near the port. Each section accumulated its character over centuries of use.

The promenade was the stage for some of the most consequential scenes in modern Catalan history — both moments of liberation and moments of violence. George Orwell described it in *Homage to Catalonia* as the street where the revolutionary Barcelona of 1936 was most visible, where workers in overalls and militia women with rifles walked alongside each other in the strange levelling atmosphere of the early war. That Barcelona is gone, replaced by something more festive and more fragile, but the street remains the place where the city comes to see and be seen.
