By the 1990s and 2000s, Barcelona had become one of the most ethnically diverse cities in southern Europe, as migration from Latin America, North and Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and China transformed neighbourhoods that had previously been almost exclusively Catalan or Spanish in character. Sant Andreu, historically a working-class Catalan stronghold, was among the districts that changed most visibly. Public art was one of the tools the city used to acknowledge and celebrate this shift.

*Les Cultures* — The Cultures — is a piece that tries to do what the most ambitious public sculpture has always attempted: make abstract social ideals tangible. The choice to place it in Sant Andreu rather than in a more tourist-facing location is significant; it is addressed to the neighbourhood's residents rather than to visitors, an expression of the city's intentions toward people who actually live there.

The neighbourhood around it has continued to change. Walking through Sant Andreu today, past shops in a dozen languages and markets selling produce from several continents, the sculpture's optimism about multiplicity feels less like a political statement and more like a simple description of what the street already is.
