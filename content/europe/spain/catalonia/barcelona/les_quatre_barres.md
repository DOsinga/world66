The *quatre barres* — four bars — appear on the coat of arms of Catalonia, Valencia, the Balearic Islands, and several other territories of the former Crown of Aragon, making them one of the most widely used heraldic emblems on the Iberian peninsula. Their origin story is one of the great legends of medieval Catalonia: the Count of Barcelona, Wilfred the Hairy, was supposedly wounded in battle, and the Frankish king dragged his fingers through the blood on the count's golden shield to create the emblem. The story is almost certainly invented in the thirteenth century, but it stuck.

What is historically documented is that the *senyera* — the flag bearing the four bars — was in use by the twelfth century, making it one of the oldest continuously used flags in Europe. Under Franco, displaying it was illegal; Catalans flew it at risk of arrest. The recovery of Catalan autonomy in 1978 and the statute of 1979 restored it to official status, and the years since have seen it multiply across the city on flags, stickers, and public art.

The artwork here, in the Barceloneta neighbourhood near the sea, places the emblem in one of Barcelona's oldest quarters — a neighbourhood built by the Bourbon authorities in the eighteenth century to house people displaced by the construction of the Ciutadella fortress. The bars in this context are not decorative: they are a statement of identity in a place that has always had reasons to assert it.
