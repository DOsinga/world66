The original four columns were erected in 1919 by the architect Josep Puig i Cadafalch, positioned on the axis leading from the city up to Montjuïc in anticipation of the 1929 International Exposition. They represented the four red stripes of the Catalan *senyera* flag — an unmissable nationalist statement planted at the threshold of a government-sponsored event. The Spanish dictatorship under Primo de Rivera, which had been systematically suppressing Catalan cultural and political expression, had them demolished in 1928, just one year before the exposition opened.

The demolition turned them into a martyred symbol more powerful than the physical columns had ever been. For the next seven decades, the bare space where they had stood remained a kind of ghost monument — Catalans knew what had been there, knew why it was gone, and remembered. The reconstruction was completed in 2010, after years of democratic deliberation about whether and how to restore them, finally placing four new columns in almost exactly the original position.

They stand now in front of the Museu Nacional d'Art de Catalunya, looking out over the city and the sea. They are among the few monuments in Barcelona where the story of the object matters as much as the object itself.
