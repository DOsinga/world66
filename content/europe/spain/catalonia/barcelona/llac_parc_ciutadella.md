The lake at the heart of the Parc de la Ciutadella occupies what was once the moat of the Bourbon citadel, a massive military fortress built by Philip V after his forces took Barcelona in 1714 following the War of the Spanish Succession. The citadel was not built to defend Barcelona from external enemies — it was built to control the city from within, its cannons trained on the streets below. The inhabitants of the La Ribera neighbourhood were expelled from their homes to clear the ground for it, given forty days to demolish their own houses or have the army do it for them. The citadel stood as a symbol of military occupation for a century and a half.

In 1869, during the revolutionary period that preceded the First Spanish Republic, the city was finally given permission to demolish the fortress. The work was carried out with civic enthusiasm — General Joan Prim, a Catalan who had risen to power in Madrid, gave the order, and the demolition was treated as a popular festival. What to do with the land occupied diplomats and city planners for years. The decision to make it a park, eventually hosting the 1888 Universal Exhibition, was both practical and symbolic: turning the site of military oppression into a public garden.

The lake was created as part of the park's design, using the moat as its basis and adding the ornamental cascade — designed by Josep Fontserè with the assistance of a young Antoni Gaudí, who worked as a draughtsman on the project — at one end. Rowing boats for hire make it feel like any Victorian park lake, which is in part the point: a space that had been a military installation for 155 years was converted, as thoroughly as stone and water allow, into something ordinary and pleasurable.
