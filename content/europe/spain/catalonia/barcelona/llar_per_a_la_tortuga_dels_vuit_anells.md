The title translates as "Home for the Tortoise of the Eight Rings," and the piece does exactly what it claims: it provides a dwelling for a creature that exists only in the imagination of the artist. This is public art as invitation to play — an object that refuses to be merely decorative and instead asks you to populate it with a fantasy resident, to wonder what the eight rings signify, to consider why a tortoise might need a home of this particular design.

Nou Barris — "nine neighbourhoods" — is the northern district of Barcelona that grew explosively in the 1950s and 1960s as internal migration from poorer Spanish regions brought hundreds of thousands of workers to the city. It was built fast, built cheap, and for decades suffered from a lack of the infrastructure and public amenities that wealthier districts took for granted. The city's investment in public art here was part of a deliberate effort to treat Nou Barris as a full citizen of Barcelona rather than an afterthought.

The tortoise's home sits in a local park, patient and permanent, waiting for its impossible occupant. It is the kind of piece that children understand immediately and adults have to be talked back into understanding.
