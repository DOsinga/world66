Lleida — known in Castilian as Lérida — is the capital of the westernmost province of Catalonia, a city on the Segre river whose history reaches back to the Iberian settlement of Iltirda. It was the site of one of Julius Caesar's most important battles in the Roman civil war, an early episcopal seat, a medieval university town, and a frontier city between Christian and Muslim spheres for centuries. In modern times it is the centre of Catalonia's agricultural interior, a city that feels less Mediterranean and more continental than the coast.

The sculpture on the Passeig de Gràcia bearing its name is part of a programme that treats the great avenue as a map of the Catalan nation in symbolic form — each piece representing a territory, a place, a community that belongs to the larger whole. The Passeig de Gràcia is itself a product of the Eixample expansion of the nineteenth century, the grandest boulevard of the bourgeois city that was building itself alongside the old, and placing these regional identifications here was a statement about what that city was the capital of.

Lleida and Barcelona have often had a complicated relationship, the agricultural interior looking at the commercial coast with a mixture of economic dependence and cultural pride. The sculpture is a small negotiation of that relationship.
