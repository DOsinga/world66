The Llotja (from the Catalan word for a covered arcade or exchange) was built between 1380 and 1392 as the trading hall for Barcelona's merchant class. The Gothic hall inside — the Saló Gòtic, or trading room — is one of the best-preserved medieval civic interiors in Catalonia: a three-aisled hall with pointed arches and an upper gallery where the arbiters of commercial disputes would sit. It was the financial heart of Barcelona at the height of its Mediterranean trading empire.

In 1774, the neoclassical exterior was built around and above the medieval structure, concealing the Gothic hall from the street. The interior was used as a stock exchange until 1994; it is now managed by the Barcelona Chamber of Commerce. The Saló Gòtic can be visited.

The Llotja's most noted function in the 19th and early 20th centuries was as an art school. The Real Academia de Bellas Artes de Sant Jordi, which used the building as its home, ran the most prestigious drawing and painting programme in Catalonia. José Ruiz Blasco, Pablo Picasso's father, taught there from 1895. The young Picasso attended classes at the school as a teenager, showing a technical facility that stunned his teachers. He passed the entrance examination — normally taken over a month — in a single day. The building stands as the unlikely cradle of the artist's formal training.
