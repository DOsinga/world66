In 1996, a group of writers, scholars, and intellectuals gathered in Barcelona to draft what would become the Universal Declaration of Linguistic Rights, a document arguing that language communities — not just states — have rights that deserve international protection. This was an explicitly Catalan initiative, organised from a city where the question of a language's right to exist was not abstract. Two years later, a follow-up declaration was signed in Barcelona that focused specifically on the languages of Europe without states — Catalan, Breton, Welsh, Basque, and dozens more — asserting their equal dignity.

The *Manifest de Barcelona* monument brings that text into physical space, making a political document into something you can stand in front of. In a city where the language question has been intensely contested — during the dictatorship, during the transition, and again in the fraught politics of the 2010s — a monument to linguistic rights carries immediate local meaning even when it speaks in universal terms.

Montjuïc, where the memorial stands, is already a place thick with political memory: the fortress above was a prison and execution site, the exposition below was a stage for competing nationalisms. The Manifest fits that layered landscape.
