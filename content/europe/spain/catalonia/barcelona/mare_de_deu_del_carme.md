The Carmelites arrived in Barcelona in the thirteenth century and established their house in what was then the edge of the city, on land that would become the Raval. The church of the Mare de Déu del Carme was built and rebuilt over the following centuries, surviving fires, suppressions, and confiscations. The most devastating event in its history came in 1835, when anticlerical riots swept through Barcelona and the church was burned along with dozens of others — an explosion of popular fury against the Church's wealth and political power that anticipated the more systematic violence of 1936.

The Raval — historically known as the Barri Xino or Chinese Quarter — has been one of Barcelona's most contested and misrepresented districts. It was the zone where the city's poor, its immigrants, its sex workers, and its bohemians all competed for space, and it was romanticised and stigmatised in equal measure. The church survived all of this, functioning as a spiritual anchor for communities that changed generation by generation.

Today the Raval has been substantially gentrified, with the MACBA museum and the CCCB cultural centre having transformed its northern end into a creative quarter. The church continues as a working parish, serving a neighbourhood that still has more economic diversity than its increasingly fashionable reputation suggests.
