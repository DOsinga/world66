The Coll — the pass or col — is the saddle of land between the hills of the Carmel and the Collserola range, a strategic point that has been a pathway through the Barcelona hills for as long as people have been crossing them. Marian sanctuaries in Catalonia typically occupy exactly this kind of liminal geography — hilltops, passes, the edges of settled land — and the Mare de Déu del Coll follows that pattern, sitting at the threshold between the city below and the forested hills above.

The Carmel neighbourhood that surrounds it grew as one of Barcelona's many informal settlements, houses built without planning permission by migrants who arrived faster than the city could absorb them. The Carmel is also famous for the Bunkers del Carmel — the ruins of a Republican anti-aircraft battery on the Turó de la Rovira that now offers one of the best panoramic views of the city — and the sanctuary is part of the same hillside territory.

The combination of the sanctuary's quiet devotional character and the neighbourhood's self-built, improvisational energy makes this a Barcelona that most visitors never reach, a city within the city that operates on its own rhythms.
