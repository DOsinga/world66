Maternity as a sculptural subject runs from the Roman votive figures found in Barcelona's archaeological layers all the way to contemporary public commissions, and the city's collection of *maternitat* pieces is large enough to constitute its own tour. This reflects something genuine about the importance of maternal imagery in Mediterranean Catholic culture, and something equally genuine about the ways that different periods have used the mother-and-child composition to say different things: about women's roles, about the body, about love, about loss.

The Gothic Quarter, where this sculpture stands, is the most densely layered part of Barcelona's history — Roman walls, medieval palaces, post-war reconstruction, and tourist infrastructure all coexist within a few blocks. A sculpture on maternity here is in conversation with everything from the Roman *Barcino* necropolis nearby to the cathedral's cloister with its resident geese.

Walking the Gothic Quarter in full tourist season can feel disorienting, the history crowded out by the commerce of visiting it. A piece like this offers a momentary recalibration — something that is not performing for you but simply existing, as the city's art has always existed, for reasons that predate your arrival.
