Max was four years old when his family fled Spain for France in 1939 — old enough to make the journey, too young to understand it. Five years later, the Vichy-era round-ups that channelled Jewish families through the Drancy transit camp outside Paris caught the Stammreichs. Max was deported to Auschwitz in 1944 and murdered there at nine.

The Stolpersteine project, initiated by Gunter Demnig in the 1990s, places markers at last known addresses for all categories of Nazi victims: Jews, political prisoners, Roma, disabled people. Barcelona's implementation does not separate Republican deportees from Jewish victims — a deliberate choice that reflects how persecution actually operated, cutting across category lines. A child's stone is the same brass square in the same pavement as any adult's.

The stone is in Horta-Guinardó, a residential district away from the tourist circuits. It is the kind of street where the marker is found by residents on their way somewhere else, not by visitors looking for it. That is part of the project's design: memory distributed through ordinary daily life, at foot level, across the whole city rather than concentrated in a dedicated space.
