The mesh is the thing. Perrault wrapped the tower's glass curtain wall in an outer skin of stainless-steel fabric — the same material logic he used on the BnF towers in Paris and the Velodrome in Berlin — so the building's apparent density changes as you move around it. Head-on from the south it reads as an almost solid silver mass; from the northwest the inner glazing shows through and the floors become legible. This is not a cosmetic effect: the mesh reduces solar gain on the south and west facades while maintaining views from inside, eliminating the need for internal blinds across most of the floor plates.

Perrault received the commission around 2004, when the 22@ district conversion was accelerating and Barcelona was trying to signal that its new technology quarter could attract internationally recognised architecture. The tower was completed in 2008, which meant it opened during the financial crisis rather than the building boom its investors had anticipated. It remains the tallest structure in Poblenou and one of the few in the 22@ zone with an identifiable architect and a coherent facade strategy, as opposed to the district's more typical speculative office blocks.

The immediate surroundings make the tower stranger. Poblenou was Barcelona's densest industrial neighbourhood from the mid-nineteenth century until the 1990s, and the streets around the hotel still contain brick factory buildings converted to co-working spaces and ateliers. The Meliá tower rises from that low-rise fabric without any transitional scale, its mesh skin catching light in a way that has no visual precedent in the neighbourhood — which is either an urban-planning failure or an honest acknowledgement that the 22@ project was always about discontinuity with what came before.
