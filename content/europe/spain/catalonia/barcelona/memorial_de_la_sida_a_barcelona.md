Barcelona in the 1980s was simultaneously experiencing its most euphoric cultural moment — the Movida, the pre-Olympic renovation, the reinvention of democratic public life — and one of its worst public health catastrophes. The AIDS epidemic intersected with a heroin crisis that had hit working-class and marginalised communities particularly hard, and Barcelona's death toll was among the highest of any European city. The gay community, sex workers, intravenous drug users, and their families bore the weight of both the disease and the stigma attached to it.

The memorial was created in the 1990s, while the epidemic was still active and the grief was immediate. Placing it on Montjuïc — on the hill above the city, in the park that contains the city's municipal cemetery — was a deliberate choice: this is where Barcelona places its dead, and the AIDS memorial belongs in that landscape of mourning.

The city's response to the epidemic was complicated, as it was everywhere: too slow, too moralised, too reluctant. The memorial is also a record of that failure, a way of saying that the people who died deserved better from the city they lived in.
