The Mercat de l'Abaceria Central stands at the top of Carrer de Verdi in Gràcia, housed in a 19th-century iron market hall. The building is modest by Barcelona market standards — less architecturally ambitious than the Boqueria or Santa Caterina — but its name is the interesting thing. Abaceria comes from abacers, the medieval Catalan term for the dealers in spices, condiments, salt, and preserved foods. The word derives from the Latin abacus, the counting table used by merchants.

Gràcia was an independent municipality until its annexation by Barcelona in 1897, and the Abaceria was its central market from 1892 until it was absorbed into the expanded city. The spice trade that gave it its name was one of the most economically significant in medieval Barcelona: spices came through the city's port from the eastern Mediterranean trade routes, were stored and sold in specialist establishments, and were used both in cooking and in medicine. The abacers occupied a specific position in the medieval guild hierarchy, licensed and regulated by the city.

The current market hall dates from 1892, when Gràcia's population had expanded enough to require a covered space. It was designed in the iron-and-glass manner that French engineers had developed for covered markets in Paris. The hall is still in operation as a food market, with the usual mix of stalls selling fish, vegetables, and dry goods. On Thursday and Sunday mornings, the surrounding streets have a flea market element. The neighbourhood around it — the streets of Gràcia immediately to the east — contains some of the best casual restaurants in Barcelona.
