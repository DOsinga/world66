The market Barcelonans call La Boqueria began as a street market held against the wall of the Carmelite convent that occupied the west side of the Rambla. When the Liberal government suppressed religious orders in 1835 and the convent was demolished, the city seized the opportunity to formalize what had been an informal arrangement. Construction of a permanent market building began in 1836 and was completed in stages through 1840 and 1914, when the iron structure was given its current form. Officially it is the Mercat de Sant Josep, named for the saint whose feast day falls in March — the month the old convent was cleared. Nobody uses the official name.

The iron and glass canopy over the main hall is among the finest market structures in Europe, a practical building of the 19th century that happens to be beautiful. The entrance on the Rambla is framed by a mosaic designed by Joan Miró, installed in 1979 as part of Barcelona's post-Franco public art program. Inside, the stalls are arranged by type: fish and seafood in the centre and rear, meat to the right, fruit and vegetables on the left, spices and dried goods in the back. At its best the market has been a functional ecosystem — a place where restaurant chefs, neighbourhood households, and market workers interact around actual food.

The transformation has been rapid and has not been kind. Tourist numbers through the 2000s and 2010s overwhelmed the market's social function: stall rents rose, local vendors were priced out or gave up, and the market reorganized itself toward cut fruit cups, candied nuts, and jamon displays optimized for photography. By the early 2020s the market's own management association was discussing quotas and restrictions to slow the erosion. The iron hall is still extraordinary. What fills it has changed.
