The 24-hour flower stalls on Carrer d'Aragó predate the current building. They have occupied the pavement outside the Concepció since the 19th century, through two renovations of the market hall behind them, and have become the default destination for anyone in Barcelona who needs flowers urgently and at an odd hour. The stalls are not a tourist amenity; they exist because the city needs them.

The market itself, built in 1888 to a design by Jeroni Granell, is known to its neighbourhood as La Conce — a contraction that signals familiarity rather than tourist branding. Unlike the Boqueria, which sits on the Rambla and has adapted accordingly, or the Santa Caterina, which was rebuilt as an architectural statement, the Concepció has continued as an Eixample food market with local clientele. The 1990s renovation updated the services without altering the covered hall's proportions.

Granell's iron structure belongs to the same generation of industrial market architecture as the Llibertat in Gràcia and the Barceloneta hall — a period when Barcelona was building covered markets across the city using prefabricated ironwork. Of the markets from that era, the Concepció is among the least altered and the least photographed, which makes it one of the more useful for understanding what a functioning 19th-century market hall looked like in practice.
