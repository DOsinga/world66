When workers broke ground on the Sant Antoni restoration in 2009, they found themselves digging through two thousand years of history. Beneath the iron columns and terracotta floors lay an intact Roman necropolis — burials, urns, and grave goods from the 1st to 4th centuries AD — that pushed the completion date back a full ten years. The necropolis is now partially visible through a glass panel inside the market.

The hall itself was the first market Barcelona built entirely within the new Eixample grid, opening in 1882. Its architect, Antoni Rovira i Trias, had submitted the winning design for the Eixample expansion in 1859 — only to have the city's choice overruled by the Spanish government, which imposed Ildefons Cerdà's rival grid instead. Rovira i Trias spent the rest of his career on municipal projects; a statue of him in the nearby Gràcia neighbourhood still holds a copy of his rejected plan. Sant Antoni was his most significant completed work.

The market's iron structure, designed in three naves with a central octagonal lantern, was a progressive choice for its era: cast iron framing let natural light flood the stalls in a way solid masonry walls could not. After the restoration, the ground floor returned to food vendors while the basement archaeology became a permanent exhibit, making this the only Barcelona market where you can buy tomatoes and inspect a Roman grave within the same visit.
