The roof is what stops people on the pavement outside. Its 325,000 hexagonal ceramic tiles, arranged in 67 colours across an undulating surface designed by Enric Miralles, read from above as an abstract aerial photograph of vegetables, fish, and fruit — the very goods sold inside. Miralles died in 2000 while the project was underway; his partner and successor Benedetta Tagliabue completed it in 2005. The wave form was deliberately chosen to distinguish the rebuilt market from the rigid Eixample grid surrounding it.

The site has been a place of assembly since the 13th century. A Dominican convent, Santa Caterina, stood here from 1219 until 1837, when the Spanish government's confiscation of church properties led to its demolition. The replacement — a covered iron market opened in 1848 — was Barcelona's first, predating the more famous Boqueria by three years in its covered form. That Victorian structure was itself demolished to make way for Miralles's project.

Beneath the current market, sections of the original convent's stone foundations are preserved and visible through a glass floor set into the market's interior. The excavation also turned up medieval graves and architectural fragments. You can walk over them while buying bread.
