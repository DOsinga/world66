The Mercat de Sant Antoni is the oldest surviving purpose-built iron market in Barcelona, pre-dating even the Boqueria in its current form. The structure was designed by Antoni Rovira i Trias — the same architect who submitted the winning plan for the Eixample expansion, only to have it overruled by the central government in Madrid in favour of Ildefons Cerdà's grid — and completed in 1882. The building is a cathedral of cast iron: a central nave flanked by side aisles under a roof of wrought-iron arches, with a basement level below.

The market closed in 2009 for what was projected to be a four-year restoration. It did not reopen until 2018. The delay was caused by the discovery, during foundation work, of a Roman burial ground of exceptional size and quality beneath the basement. The Romans had placed their cemeteries outside the city walls along the main roads; this one lay along the road leading southwest from Barcino. Archaeologists spent years excavating and documenting it before the renovation could continue.

During the closure, the neighbourhood's weekend book and second-hand market — held under the market's exterior arcades on Sundays — continued without interruption. The arcades are one of the building's distinctive features: a colonnade runs around the exterior at street level, creating a covered space that has been a Sunday market since at least the 1930s. The bookstalls, selling vintage comics, old magazines, and second-hand books, have become inseparable from the building's identity.
