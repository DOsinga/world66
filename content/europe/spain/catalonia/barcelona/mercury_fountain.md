In 1937, while Barcelona was under the Republic and the Civil War was entering its most brutal phase, the Spanish Republican government commissioned Alexander Calder to create a centrepiece for the Spanish Pavilion at the Paris Exposition. The pavilion is remembered primarily for Picasso's *Guernica*, but Calder's fountain occupied the entrance and made its own political argument: filled with liquid mercury rather than water, it referenced Almadén in Castilla-La Mancha, the site of the world's largest mercury mine, which Nationalist forces were besieging. The mercury was the lifeblood of the Republic's economy; its loss would be catastrophic.

Calder's mobile engineering — he was the inventor of the kinetic mobile — produced a fountain in which the heavy, silver liquid moved with an eerie, hypnotic slowness, pooling and flowing in ways that water never does. The word "Almadén" was spelled out in the rippling surface. It was one of the most extraordinary pieces of political public art made in the twentieth century, and almost nobody outside the Paris Exposition ever saw it.

The fountain eventually came to Barcelona and was installed in the Fundació Joan Miró, where it remains behind glass for safety reasons — mercury vapour is toxic. You can see it but not stand close to it, which adds to its strange, sealed quality: a protest frozen in time, still liquid after all these years.
