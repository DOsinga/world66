Gràcia was an independent town until Barcelona absorbed it in 1897, and it has never entirely forgiven the annexation. The neighbourhood retains a fierce local identity expressed through its famous August festival, its network of neighbourhood associations, and a general conviction that Gràcia is a real community while the rest of Barcelona is merely a city. The local characters — the *veïns*, the neighbours — are central to this identity, and naming a public sculpture after a specific, diminutive, personal name fits the neighbourhood's self-understanding perfectly.

Miliu is not a hero or an allegory. The name is the kind of name given to an ordinary person, and the figure presumably embodies that ordinariness: the person you see every morning at the bar, who has lived in the same apartment for forty years, who knows everyone and is known by everyone, who is the social glue of the neighbourhood without ever having done anything officially remarkable.

Barcelona's finest public art often operates at this scale — not the grand historical statement but the acknowledgement that ordinary life, lived in ordinary places by ordinary people, is worth a monument too. Gràcia has more of these small human tributes than almost any other neighbourhood, and *Miliu* is among the most affectionate of them.
