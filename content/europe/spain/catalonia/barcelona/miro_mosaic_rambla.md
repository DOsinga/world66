At the midpoint of La Rambla, roughly level with Carrer de l'Hospital, a large circular mosaic is embedded in the pavement. The primary colours — red, yellow, blue, black — are unmistakably Miró. The design incorporates a large black star, a red disc, and the fluid organic forms that became his signature in the 1950s onwards. Most of the people walking over it are looking at the stalls of the Boqueria market on one side or the flow of tourists on the other. The mosaic is under their feet.

Joan Miró made it in 1976, the year after Franco's death, when Barcelona was tentatively reasserting its Catalan identity in public space after four decades of suppression. The mosaic is signed — look for his name in the lower section — and it was placed here deliberately, in the most trafficked part of the most famous street in the city, not in a museum. Miró wanted it to be walked on, to be part of the daily flow. He said he considered it a democratic work, one that required no ticket and no reverence.

The tiles are traditional Catalan ceramic, made to withstand millions of footsteps and decades of outdoor exposure. They have held up. Look for the small hexagonal signature tile in the lower right quadrant. If you are here on a rainy morning before the crowds arrive, and the wet pavement catches the colour, it becomes something else entirely — a painting underfoot, brief and brilliant before the flow of feet begins again.
