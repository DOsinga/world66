Moll de la Fusta — the Timber Wharf — was for centuries the section of Barcelona's port designated for the unloading of wood: structural timber for the city's construction boom, charcoal for the lime-kilns, and ship timber for the Drassanes reial shipyards immediately to the west. The name survived well past the cessation of timber trading; by the mid-twentieth century the wharf was largely given over to transit traffic on the coastal highway that separated the city from the sea.

The transformation came in the 1980s and early 1990s as part of the urban rehabilitation for the 1992 Olympics. The coastal highway was partially buried or tunnelled, the wharf surface was repaved as a pedestrian promenade, and the architect Manuel de Solà-Morales designed a new waterfront edge with a wide elevated walkway and a lower boardwalk at water level. The gamberina sculptures — a large red metal shrimp — were placed along the moll as public art, designed by Joan Miró's studio and famously described as "the world's most photographed seafood."

The Olympic-era transformation was the first step in the longer project of reorienting Barcelona toward the sea, a project that had been blocked since the nineteenth century by the industrial port and the railway tracks that ran along the waterfront. The Moll de la Fusta redesign became the template for the Barceloneta and Poblenou seafront works that followed.
