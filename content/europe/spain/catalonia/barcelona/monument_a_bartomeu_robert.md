In 1899 a newly elected mayor of Barcelona did something almost unheard of in Spanish politics: he resigned rather than collect a tax he considered unjust. Bartomeu Robert had campaigned on the platform that Catalonia was being bled dry by Madrid to pay for the recent colonial wars, wars that had cost Spain Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines. When the central government demanded the municipalities enforce a wartime levy, Robert refused, rallied the Barcelona business community, and sparked what became known as the Tancament de Caixes — the shutting of the cash registers. It was the first major organised act of Catalan fiscal resistance.

The crisis shook the Spanish political system and, more importantly, it persuaded a generation of Catalan industrialists and professionals that their interests would never be protected within the centralised state. Within two years the Lliga Regionalista had been founded, making Catalan nationalism a formal electoral force for the first time. Robert died in 1902, just three years after his resignation, but he was already a hero of the movement he had helped to catalyse.

The monument by Josep Llimona and Lluís Domènech i Montaner was inaugurated in 1910 at the junction of the Passeig de Gràcia and the Gran Via, one of the most prominent intersections in the Eixample. Its placement was deliberate: it stands in the heart of the bourgeois city that Robert had mobilised, in front of the very streets that had shut their shop fronts in protest. The stone reliefs around the base depict not a king or a general but the Catalan people themselves.
