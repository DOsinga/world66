When the column was inaugurated in 1888 for the Universal Exposition, the organisers pointed Columbus's outstretched arm toward the southeast — toward the Mediterranean and, some argue, toward Genoa. Others insist he is pointing toward Africa or simply down the Rambla. Almost nobody is certain he is pointing toward the Atlantic. That directional ambiguity might be accidental, but it has nourished one of Catalonia's most persistent and colourful historical controversies: the theory that Cristòfor Colom was not Genoese at all but a Catalan from a noble family, possibly from Girona, who disguised his origins for political reasons.

The monument itself was designed by Gaietà Buïgas and built specifically for the 1888 Exposition, which aimed to announce a modernising Barcelona to the world. The base is ringed with allegorical figures and carved with scenes from the voyages, and the column itself is 60 metres tall, housing a small lift that carries visitors to a mirador at Columbus's feet. The choice of subject was pointed: Barcelona was claiming a role in the story of the Americas that Madrid had long monopolised.

At the foot of the Rambla, where the old city meets the sea, the column marks the symbolic end of Barcelona's great promenade and the beginning of the port. It was here, according to tradition (though not necessarily to history), that Columbus returned from his first voyage in 1493 and was received by the Catholic Monarchs. Whether or not that meeting ever happened in Barcelona — the evidence is thin — the city has never let go of the story.
