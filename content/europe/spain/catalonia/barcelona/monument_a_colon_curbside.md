The Columbus Monument at the foot of the Rambla was built for the 1888 Universal Exposition and stands 60 metres tall. It celebrates the return of Columbus to Barcelona in 1493 to report to the Catholic Monarchs on his first voyage, which is historically documented. The statue on the top shows Columbus pointing toward the horizon.

The problem — noted in guidebooks but rarely advertised by the city — is the direction Columbus points. He faces roughly southeast: toward Genoa, Columbus's birthplace (or toward the Balearic Islands, or toward the open sea in front of him, depending on who you ask). He does not point toward the Americas, which lie to the west and southwest. Whether this was a deliberate design choice, a navigational error, or an architectural decision based on the aesthetics of the viewing angle is not known. No official explanation has been offered.

The column itself is a cast-iron hollow cylinder. A small elevator inside carries visitors to the circular viewing gallery at the top, 50 metres above the ground. The gallery provides one of the best elevated views of the port, the Barceloneta neighbourhood, and the Rambla — three perspectives that are difficult to combine at ground level. The monument's base contains eight large bronze figures representing the historical kingdoms of Spain as they were at the time of the 1492 voyage. Catalans who point out the navigational mistake are sometimes suggested to be reflecting, perhaps unconsciously, on a monument that celebrates a voyage commissioned by the kingdoms of Castile and Aragon, not by Barcelona.
