It may seem unexpected to find a monument to a Florentine medieval poet beside the sea in Barcelona, but the connection is less arbitrary than it appears. In the late 19th century Barcelona had a significant Italian merchant community concentrated around the Barceloneta and the port, and the Italian state actively promoted the figure of Dante as a symbol of Italian cultural unity — the poet served the same function for the newly unified Italian nation that Verdaguer served for Catalonia. The monument was gifted to the city by the Italian government in 1921.

The timing was also meaningful for Catalan cultural politics. The modernista and Noucentista movements had been debating what a great Catalan epic literature should look like, and the Divine Comedy was the unavoidable reference point — a vernacular language elevated to high art, a poem that made a living spoken tongue the vehicle of philosophical and theological ambition. Joan Maragall, the greatest Catalan poet of the era, had translated parts of Dante and wrote about him extensively.

The sculpture, by the Italian artist Victorio Macho, shows Dante standing in his allegorical laurel crown, gazing seaward. Placed near the end of the Passeig Nacional, it has become a quiet landmark of Barceloneta, a neighbourhood that tends to attract the attention of tourists mainly for its beach and its grilled fish — making the poet of the Inferno something of a surprise encounter at the end of a seaside stroll.
