Francesc Macià is one of the most romantic figures in modern Catalan history: a retired Spanish army colonel who became a militant separatist, organised an armed invasion of Catalonia from France in 1926, was arrested, tried in Paris, and acquitted amid international celebrity. By 1931, after the fall of the Spanish monarchy, he was president of the Generalitat — the restored Catalan government — and had proclaimed a Catalan Republic within a federal Spanish state. The Republic lasted approximately three days before Madrid negotiated it back into a statute of autonomy, but the gesture set the emotional template for Catalan political aspiration for generations.

Macià died in office on Christmas Day 1933, the only Catalan president to die while serving. His funeral drew an estimated million people into the streets of Barcelona, a figure that has probably never been equalled before or since for a political figure in this city. The grief was genuine: he had managed the almost impossible feat of uniting the Catalan left and right behind a single national project, at least briefly.

Plaça de Catalunya, where the monument stands, is the symbolic centre of Barcelona — the point where the old city meets the Eixample, where the Rambla begins, where demonstrations end and begin. Placing Macià's statue here rather than in a peripheral square was a deliberate statement about where he belongs in the Catalan pantheon. The cube-shaped abstract base designed by Xavier Corberó in 1991 deliberately avoids the academic figurative tradition, giving the memorial a contemporary weight.
