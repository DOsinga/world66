Salvat-Papasseit died in 1924 at the age of thirty, having compressed into less than a decade a body of poetry that fused Italian futurism, anarchist politics, and a fierce sensory attachment to the Barcelona waterfront. He grew up poor in the Barceloneta, left school young, worked manual jobs including as a night watchman at the port, and educated himself in borrowed books. His poetry crackles with machinery, ships, flags, and the physical intensity of someone who knows time is short — he had been diagnosed with tuberculosis in his early twenties and spent his working life coughing through it.

His most famous collection, *L'irradiador del port i les gavines* (The Port's Radiator and the Seagulls), published in 1921, was designed to look like a futurist manifesto — typography crashing across the page, words broken and rearranged to suggest speed and industrial noise. But underneath the formal experiment was a tenderness for the city, the sea, and the people who worked them that owes more to popular song than to Marinetti. He is a poet of the Barceloneta before tourism, when it was a working neighbourhood of sailors and factory hands.

The monument near the port honours him with a sculpture that catches something of that energy. He remains a cult figure among Catalan poets and readers who respond to the combination of formal daring and emotional directness — a poet who managed to be avant-garde and popular at the same time, precisely because he never lost touch with where he came from.
