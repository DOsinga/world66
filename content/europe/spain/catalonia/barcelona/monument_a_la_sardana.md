The sardana is one of the stranger national symbols in Europe: a slow, precise circle dance performed to a cobla orchestra, in which participants of all ages and social classes hold hands and count their steps carefully. It looks nothing like the vigorous folk dances associated with nationalism elsewhere, and that restraint is part of its meaning. The sardana demands collective attention and exact timing; nobody leads, everybody follows the same count, and the circle expands and contracts as more people join. It is a discipline of equality.

During the Franco dictatorship, public performances of the sardana were among the earliest cultural expressions to be banned, then grudgingly tolerated, then banned again depending on the political temperature. Dancing the sardana in front of the cathedral became a quiet act of cultural resistance — participants knew they might be dispersed, and came anyway. The monument, installed in 1966 (during the dictatorship), was itself a negotiated compromise: the regime could not easily prohibit a monument to a dance, but the authorities knew perfectly well what cultural statement it made.

The sculptor was Josep Cañas, and the figures are arranged in the circular formation of the actual dance, hands linked, slightly abstracted. Placed on the north side of the cathedral, the monument has presided over countless actual sardanas danced in its shadow — the dance and its memorial occupy the same ground, which is the point.
