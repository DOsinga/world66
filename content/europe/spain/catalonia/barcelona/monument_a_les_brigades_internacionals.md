Between 1936 and 1938 approximately 35,000 volunteers from 53 countries came to Spain to fight against Francisco Franco's Nationalist uprising. Many passed through Barcelona — the city was the political and logistical centre of the Republican resistance — and roughly a third of those who came never returned home. The International Brigades were disbanded in September 1938, in a last-ditch attempt to persuade the international community to pressure Hitler and Mussolini into withdrawing their support for Franco. The gesture failed, and the Republic fell the following year.

For decades after the war a memorial to the Brigades was politically impossible in Spain. Franco had called the volunteers criminals and communist agents; even after his death in 1975 the political transition required careful handling of Civil War memory. It was not until 1996 that the city felt able to erect this monument in the Parc de la Ciutadella, near the Arc de Triomf — itself a Catalan symbol of welcome to the world, chosen deliberately.

Subirachs's choice of the David and Goliath metaphor allowed the sculpture to make its political argument obliquely: the small figure standing against the giant, armed only with courage. The abstraction of the forms means the monument does not look like a war memorial in the conventional sense, which was part of the strategy. But the dedication plaque is entirely explicit, listing the countries from which the volunteers came and naming the battle of the Ebro as their defining sacrifice.
