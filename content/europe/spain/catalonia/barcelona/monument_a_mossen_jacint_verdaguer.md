Verdaguer's life reads like a 19th-century novel: a poor seminarian from rural Catalonia who spent years working the transatlantic run as a ship's chaplain to earn money for his studies, who then wrote the Homeric epic *L'Atlàntida* and the romantic pastoral *Canigó* and became the towering figure of the Catalan literary renaissance, only to fall dramatically from grace in his fifties when he became involved with a group of exorcists his patron considered charlatans. The resulting scandal — his patron suspended his priestly faculties, he appealed publicly, the case played out in the Barcelona press — made him simultaneously a martyr figure for Catalan cultural nationalism and a warning about the dangers of religious enthusiasm.

He died in 1902, and the outpouring of grief was enormous. His body lay in state at the Casa del Canonges while tens of thousands filed past. He was immediately claimed by the Catalan cultural movement as a founding figure, the poet who had proved that Catalan was a language capable of producing literature to match any in Europe. Streets, squares, and institutions across Catalonia were named for him.

The monument at the junction of the Passeig de Gràcia and the Diagonal was inaugurated in 1924, designed by Josep Llimona. Its placement at one of the Eixample's grandest intersections reflects Verdaguer's canonical status: he is treated less as a historical figure than as a national symbol, the poet who gave the Renaixença its epic voice.
