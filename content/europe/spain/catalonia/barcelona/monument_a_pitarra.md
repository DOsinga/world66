Frederic Soler, known by his pen name Serafí Pitarra, stands in bronze on the Rambla at the point closest to the theatre that was his life's work. Soler founded the Teatre Romea on the Carrer de l'Hospital in 1863, where he staged theatrical works in Catalan at a time when Catalan public culture was being suppressed by the political authorities. His plays were comedies and sainets (short popular theatrical pieces) that used the vernacular language of Barcelona's working class — not the literary Catalan of the Renaixença intellectuals but the spoken language of the market stalls and workshops.

Soler is also the author of the poem inscribed at the Fossar de les Moreres, the memorial to the defenders of Barcelona who fell in 1714: "En el fossar de les moreres / no s'hi enterra cap traïdor..." (In the Fossar de les Moreres / no traitor is buried...). This poem, written in the 1870s, became one of the central texts of Catalan nationalist memory. Its author was not primarily a political figure but a popular entertainer — the combination says something about how Catalan cultural resistance operated in the 19th century.

The bronze on the Rambla was installed in 1906, eight years after Soler's death. It sits at the point where the theatre quarter of the Rambla meets the entrance to the Raval — the territory his work inhabited. The Teatre Romea still operates on the Carrer de l'Hospital and still performs in Catalan.
