The fall of Barcelona on 11 September 1714 to the forces of Philip V ended the War of the Spanish Succession and, with it, Catalan self-governance. The Nueva Planta decrees that followed abolished the Catalan institutions, banned the Catalan language from official use, and replaced the old constitutional order with Castilian absolutism. Casanova, the Conseller en Cap — the chief councillor — led the street-by-street defence and was wounded in the final assault. He survived, went into hiding, and lived out his days in obscurity, which is more than most of his colleagues managed.

The date — the Onze de Setembre — became the Catalan National Day, the Diada, and the monument to Casanova on the Via Laietana (close to where the original city wall stood) became the focal point of its commemoration. For much of the Franco dictatorship, when public Catalan cultural expression was suppressed, laying flowers at the Casanova monument was one of the few acts of collective memory that could not easily be banned — the authorities were reluctant to prohibit what could be called simple remembrance of a battle. The annual flower-laying became quietly political and then, from the 1970s onward, openly so.

The monument itself, inaugurated in 1914 on the two-hundredth anniversary of the defeat, shows Casanova carrying the Barcelona city flag. His expression and posture were designed to convey not despair but resolution — a fallen but unbroken defender, which is precisely the political message the monument was built to transmit.
