When Rius i Taulet committed Barcelona to hosting a Universal Exposition in 1888, he had roughly two years to build it, and the site he had in mind — the old Citadel, a massive Bourbon fortress that had oppressed the working-class neighbourhoods of the Ribera for 170 years — was still partly standing. The demolition of the Citadel and the transformation of its grounds into the park that now bears its name (Parc de la Ciutadella) was one of the great urban acts of 19th-century Barcelona, and it happened under brutal deadline pressure.

The Exposition opened on time, received over 400,000 visitors, and introduced Barcelona to the world as a modern industrial city rather than a Spanish provincial backwater. Among the buildings constructed for it was the Arc de Triomf — not a war memorial but a welcome gate — and the spectacular Café-Restaurant by Domènech i Montaner, now the Zoological Museum. The 1888 Exposition was the launchpad for the modernisme movement and, more broadly, for Barcelona's self-image as a European metropolis with ambitions beyond Spain.

Rius i Taulet died in 1889, almost immediately after the Exposition closed, from the exhaustion of the effort he had driven. The monument in the Parc de la Ciutadella honours him at the scene of his great project: he presided over the fair from here, and it was in the park that his legacy is most physically visible. He was not a nationalist in the political sense — he operated within the Restoration system — but he gave the city the confidence and the infrastructure that the nationalists would later build on.
