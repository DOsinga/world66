In mid-19th century Barcelona the sick poor had two options: the Hospital de la Santa Creu, a medieval charitable institution run by religious orders where nursing meant spiritual comfort more than medical intervention, or nothing. Bosch i Alsina, as president of the municipal government in the 1860s and 70s, pushed through the construction of the Hospital de la Sagrada Família and a series of reforms that moved Barcelona's public health infrastructure toward the model of clinical medicine that was then developing in Paris and Vienna. He also oversaw major improvements to the city's water supply and sewage systems during the rapid urbanisation of the Eixample.

His broader significance was as an administrator who understood that the new industrial city generated new kinds of suffering — cholera, typhus, the chronic respiratory diseases of factory workers — that medieval institutions were not equipped to handle. The Eixample was expanding at an enormous rate in these decades, and the sanitary infrastructure had to keep pace or the city would face the catastrophic epidemics that were destroying other rapidly industrialising European cities.

The monument near the Barceloneta honours him in a neighbourhood that was historically one of the poorest and most crowded in the city, and therefore one of the most directly affected by the public health reforms he championed. He is not a famous name today, but the city's capacity to manage the health consequences of rapid urban growth in the 19th century owed a great deal to his practical administrative work.
