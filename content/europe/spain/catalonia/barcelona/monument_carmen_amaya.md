Carmen Amaya was born in 1913 in a shack on the Barceloneta beach, in the impoverished gitano (Roma) community that occupied the shore outside the formal neighbourhood. She died in 1963 in Begur, one of the most celebrated flamenco performers in history. In the fifty years between, she transformed flamenco — particularly the rapid footwork of the baile — while performing before presidents, kings, and Hollywood audiences.

Amaya learned to dance in the tablaos and bars of the Somorrostro — the shanty settlement on the Barceloneta shore that was home to thousands of poor families, mostly gitanos, until its demolition in the 1960s. Her technique was distinctive: she danced in the costume and style traditionally associated with male dancers, including trousers rather than skirts, with an intensity and speed that was unprecedented for a female performer. Her flamenco was developed in poverty; it became wealthy in Hollywood.

The bronze memorial in Barceloneta, installed in 2010, shows Amaya mid-dance. It is placed near the location of the Somorrostro settlement where she was born and grew up — now the beach and promenade that stretch east from Barceloneta toward the Olympic Port. The settlement and its community are gone; the beach that replaced it receives several million visitors annually. The monument is a small, easily missed bronze in a landscape of beach bars and tourist infrastructure, marking the birth of an artist who emerged from one of the most marginalized communities in 20th-century Barcelona.
