Rafael Casanova was the Conseller en Cap of Barcelona — roughly, the chief magistrate — when the city fell to the Bourbon forces of Philip V on 11 September 1714. He was wounded in the fighting, carried from the walls, survived, and was allowed to live out his days in relative obscurity under the new order. He died in 1743, aged over eighty. His courage in the final siege, and particularly his presence on the walls alongside the city's defenders, made him a symbol of Catalan resistance to the centralizing monarchy that abolished Catalan institutions after the conquest.

The statue was erected in 1888 — during the first Catalan cultural renaissance, the Renaixença — by the sculptor Rossend Nobas. It shows Casanova in armour, holding a banner of Catalonia, wounded and determined. The choice of 1888 was deliberate: the Universal Exhibition held that year in Barcelona was a moment of Catalan cultural assertion, and the statue was part of a programme of public monuments that reclaimed Catalan history in public space.

Every 11 September, the Diada Nacional de Catalunya, the statue is covered in flowers and wreaths. The scale of floral tribute has grown with the independence movement: in recent years, the base of the statue disappears entirely under red and yellow carnations. The ceremony is both commemorative and political — mourning a defeat three centuries old while insisting on contemporary Catalan identity. Stand at the statue on any day of the year and look at the base; there are usually a few dried flowers remaining from the last Diada.
