Cristòfol made his most important work in the 1930s, during the same years that Dalí and Miró were developing their surrealist connections through Paris. But unlike them he stayed in Lleida — a provincial Catalan city with no gallery scene and no international art market — making assemblages from wire, mirror, feathers, and found materials that had no audience and no commercial value. He fought on the Republican side in the Civil War, survived, and spent the Franco years working as a sign painter and carpenter. His sculpture was not exhibited publicly until the 1970s.

The delayed recognition of Cristòfol is one of the more instructive stories in 20th-century Spanish art history. The institutional structure of the art world — galleries, museums, critics, collectors — was destroyed by the Civil War and the dictatorship. Artists who remained in Spain, especially those who had fought for the Republic and who worked in avant-garde traditions the regime considered degenerate, simply did not exist as far as the international art market was concerned. Cristòfol was rediscovered by a generation of curators in the 1970s and 80s who were trying to reconstruct the story of Spanish modernism.

The monument near the Rambla del Mar is a reproduction of one of his characteristic abstract pieces — lightweight, airy, built from industrial and natural materials in combination. It serves as both a tribute and an argument: that Spain produced a richer and stranger modernist tradition than the canonical accounts, distorted by the Civil War's interruptions, have usually acknowledged.
