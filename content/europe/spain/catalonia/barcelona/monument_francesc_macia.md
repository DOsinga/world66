Francesc Macià i Llussà (1859-1933) was a Catalan military officer turned republican politician who became one of the central figures of Catalan nationalism in the early 20th century. On 14 April 1931 — the day the Spanish Second Republic was proclaimed in Madrid — Macià appeared on the balcony of the Palau de la Generalitat and proclaimed the Catalan Republic. The proclamation was short-lived: two days later he renegotiated with the Madrid government to accept the formula of a Statute of Autonomy within the Spanish Republic, becoming the first President of the restored Generalitat.

Macià was President until his death from illness in December 1933. He died on Christmas Day; his body was laid in state in the Generalitat palace and hundreds of thousands of Barcelonans passed in front of it. His death was mourned as the loss of the figure who had most clearly embodied Catalan republican identity.

The monument on the Plaça de Catalunya (or more precisely at the edge of it) was created by Josep Maria Subirachs, the sculptor who later completed the Sagrada Família's Passion façade and who produced several of the major public commemorative works of the post-Franco era. The 1991 date — more than fifty years after Macià's death — reflects how long it took for the democratic restoration to produce public monuments to figures suppressed by the Franco regime. The monument shows Macià in his characteristic pose: upright, direct, as much military officer as politician.
