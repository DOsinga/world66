Gaspar de Portolà i Rovira was born in Balaguer, Catalonia, in 1716 and died in Lleida in 1786. Between those dates, he had one of the more extraordinary careers in the history of Spanish colonialism: as governor of the Californias, he led the first overland expedition to Alta California in 1769-1770, founding the Presidio of San Diego and Mission San Diego de Alcalá, becoming the first European to see San Francisco Bay from land, and establishing the chain of Spanish settlements that would eventually become California.

Portolà's Catalan origin is rarely mentioned in Californian historical memory, which tends to emphasise the Franciscan missionaries (mostly from Mallorca and mainland Spain) over the military commanders who organised and led the expeditions. In Catalonia, his achievements are similarly obscure — there is no major monument in Barcelona, and the memorial on Montjuïc is modest.

The expedition of 1769 was organised from Mexico City under the Viceroyalty of New Spain. Portolà commanded the land party while ships carried supplies along the coast. The overland route he pioneered became the basis of the Camino Real that connected the California missions. He returned to Spain in 1776 and spent his final decade in relative obscurity. The cities he helped found — San Diego, Los Angeles, San Francisco — are now among the largest in the United States. His memorial on Montjuïc is visited by almost nobody.
