The square is named after George Orwell, who famously wrote about the street fighting he witnessed in this neighbourhood in 1937 during the Spanish Civil War. The square itself was redesigned in the 1990s under controversial circumstances — locals objected to the changes, and a security camera was installed there, which Orwell's estate found grimly apt. What stands in the centre of this charged space is a sculpture that the neighbourhood immediately renamed.

Leandre Cristòfol was a sculptor from Lleida associated with Catalan avant-garde and surrealism. His monument here, installed in September 1991, is a rounded biomorphic form on a plinth — abstract, organic, slightly mysterious. The official name is simply his own. The neighbourhood had other ideas: it became L'oliva (the olive) or, more irreverently, El tripi (the trip), suggesting something psychedelic in its bulging rounded form.

You are standing in a square named for a writer who documented a revolution failing in real time. Around you is one of the Raval and Gothic Quarter's nightlife zones — bars, late-night restaurants, the smell of the previous evening. The sculpture absorbs all of this without comment. Cristòfol died in 1998; this is one of his few pieces in Barcelona, and one of the few monuments in the city that the residents cared enough about to rename twice.
