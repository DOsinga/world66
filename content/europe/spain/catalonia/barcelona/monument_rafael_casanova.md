Rafael Casanova was the Conseller en Cap (Chief Councillor) of the city of Barcelona during the siege of 1714 — the final act of the War of the Spanish Succession in Catalonia. When the Bourbon forces of Philip V breached the city's walls on 11 September 1714, Casanova led the last resistance with sword in hand and was wounded in the battle. He survived, accepted the subsequent amnesty offered to the city's leaders, and died in 1743.

The bronze statue in the Eixample — erected in 1886, the year after the first Diada Nacional was commemorated — shows Casanova in 17th-century armour holding a banner. It was created by the sculptor Rossend Nobas. From its unveiling, the statue became the physical focus of the Diada (Catalonia's national day on September 11): political organisations have laid wreaths here annually, a tradition that continued even during the Franco period when the Diada itself was suppressed as a public celebration.

The statue's location is not at the point where Casanova was wounded — that is on the Carrer de les Jonqueres — but in the Eixample at the junction of the Carrer de Bruc and the Passeig de Sant Joan. It was placed where it could be easily reached by marchers from across the city. The September 11 wreath-laying, which during the Franco years required careful management to avoid outright suppression, is now a formal element of the Diada. Casanova would be surprised to find himself the patron saint of a national movement that did not exist as such in his lifetime.
