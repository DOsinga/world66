The Monument a la Sardana on Montjuïc, created in 1966 by the sculptor Josep Cañas, shows a circle of dancers engaged in the sardana — the traditional Catalan circle dance that has served as a symbol of Catalan collective identity since the 19th century. The stone frieze captures the characteristic posture of sardana dancers: hands linked and raised, feet performing the complex sequence of steps.

The sardana was one of the few Catalan cultural practices that the Franco regime permitted throughout the dictatorship, partly because suppressing a folk dance would have seemed petty even by the standards of the regime, and partly because the sardana's enthusiasts successfully presented it as an apolitical cultural tradition rather than a nationalist political statement. In practice, it was both. The weekly sardana dances in the Plaça de la Seu, in village squares across Catalonia, and in parks were occasions when Catalan speakers gathered publicly, spoke Catalan, and asserted a cultural identity that the regime was officially trying to suppress.

The 1966 date of the monument — under Franco, approved by official channels — reflects the complex negotiations of cultural politics in the late dictatorship. A monument to Catalan folk dance was permissible in a way that a monument to Catalan political aspiration was not. The sardana sculpture thus occupied the same political space as the dance itself: a coded statement of identity in a medium (folk culture) that the regime had decided not to regulate.
