Sarrià is one of the most historically layered of Barcelona's incorporated villages: it retained its own municipal government until 1921, when it was finally absorbed into the city, and it has preserved its village character more tenaciously than any of the other municipalities swallowed by Barcelona's expansion — Gràcia, Sant Martí, Sants, Sant Andreu — in the great annexations of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Much of what we know about Sarrià's pre-annexation history comes from the meticulous work of its parish priest.

Batlle i Mestre collected documents, interviewed elderly residents, compiled genealogies, and wrote local histories in the tradition of the Catalan mossèn-arxiver — the priest-archivist — who was a crucial figure in the preservation of Catalan cultural and historical memory at a time when the state showed little interest in doing so. The Catholic Church, whatever its political limitations in other respects, provided an institutional framework within which local history could be preserved and transmitted across generations.

The memorial on the streets of Sarrià honours him as a man of the neighbourhood rather than a figure of broader cultural importance — someone whose work mattered precisely because it was local and specific, because it recorded the names and dates and stories that would otherwise have been lost when the old village dissolved into the expanding city around it. Walkers who come through the old Sarrià village centre pass his memorial in a streetscape that his documentation helped to explain.
