Barcelona's medieval city wall was expanded twice: first in the 13th century to incorporate the growing La Ribera neighbourhood, and again in the 14th and 15th centuries to enclose the Raval. The 13th-century wall ran along the waterfront of La Ribera, essentially following the line of the current Passeig del Born and continuing to the sea. Sections of this wall were incorporated into the buildings constructed against it in the 14th and 15th centuries.

Several stretches of the medieval wall are visible in the La Ribera neighbourhood, identifiable by the large irregular stone blocks of the construction and the different level at which they sit relative to the surrounding buildings. The most accessible section is incorporated into the façade of a building on the Carrer de Sant Pere Més Alt, where the change in masonry between the medieval wall and the later building that was constructed against it is clearly visible.

The wall was made redundant as a defensive structure in the 17th century, when the city's fortification system was reorganised around the Ciutadella fortress. Rather than demolishing it — which would have required enormous resources — the city simply built against it and over it. The medieval wall became part of the building stock of the neighbourhood: a foundation for new construction, a partition wall between properties, a structural element incorporated into a changing city. This pragmatic adaptation rather than preservation or demolition is how Barcelona has handled most of its medieval fabric.
