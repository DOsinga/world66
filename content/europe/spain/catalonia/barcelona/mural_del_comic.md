Catalan-language publishing was banned after the Civil War, and the ban was enforced most strictly for official and literary publications. But comics — especially children's comics — existed in a cultural grey zone: the authorities generally considered them too trivial to police strictly, and some Catalan-language comics were permitted to circulate under the radar during the 1940s and 50s. This relative tolerance made the comic strip one of the few spaces where Catalan could be published and read publicly, and it gave the medium a cultural importance in Catalonia that it doesn't have in the same way in other Spanish regions.

Catalan comics have a tradition that stretches back to the late 19th century, with figures like Apel·les Mestres creating strips that combined political satire with artistic ambition. The 20th century brought a rich tradition of popular adventure and humour comics, and the post-Franco era saw an explosion of experimental and underground comics production in Barcelona, which was one of Europe's most active scenes in the 1980s and 90s.

The mural, which runs for a considerable length along its wall in the Poblenou area, references characters and images from this tradition, making a public argument for the legitimacy of comics as a cultural form worthy of monumental treatment. The choice of Poblenou — the former industrial district now being rebranded as a creative hub — is fitting: the mural participates in the neighbourhood's self-presentation as a place where popular culture and artistic ambition have always coexisted.
