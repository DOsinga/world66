The building opposite the Barcelona Cathedral is the headquarters of the Catalan architects' professional association, a brutalist block from 1958 that would be entirely unremarkable were it not for what covers its exterior walls. Three massive concrete friezes run across the façades, rendered in sgraffito (a technique of scratching through wet plaster to reveal a contrasting layer beneath). The designs are by Pablo Picasso. They show scenes from Catalan popular life: a sardana dance, a procession with a giant puppet, children playing, palm branches — everything inflected with Picasso's characteristic stylisation.

Picasso drew the designs in 1960 but could not come to Barcelona to supervise their execution. He had left Spain in 1934 and sworn never to return while Franco remained in power. The Norwegian artist Carl Nesjar, who had developed a sand-blasting technique for transferring Picasso's line drawings into concrete, executed the work on site from the master's cartoons.

The commission was itself an act of political complexity. The architects' college wanted a Picasso work; the Franco regime, which had banned the artist's name from public institutions, had to tacitly permit it. The frieze appeared under the description "decorative work by a Catalan artist" in some official documents. It is the largest Picasso commission ever executed on a public building, and the only major example of his work that he never saw in person. He died in 1973, two years before Franco, and never returned to Spain.
