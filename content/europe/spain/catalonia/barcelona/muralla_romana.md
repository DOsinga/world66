The most disorienting fact about the Gothic Quarter is that it is not medieval. The street pattern, the block size, the position of the cathedral — all of it follows the plan of a Roman colony called Barcino, founded around 15 BC. The walls you can see in fragments throughout the neighbourhood were built to contain that colony, and the city has never meaningfully expanded beyond them until the 19th century.

The original circuit of walls dates from the 2nd and early 3rd centuries. What survives most visibly — the towers on the Plaça Nova and the long section on the Carrer del Paradís — belongs to a later, more massive rebuilding carried out in the late 3rd and 4th centuries. Barbarian pressure on the empire's frontiers made reinforcement urgent across Roman Spain; Barcino's response was a wall six metres thick at its base, eight to nine metres high, with 74 semicircular towers projecting at regular intervals to give archers overlapping fields of fire. That circuit, roughly 1,300 metres in total perimeter, is the boundary of the Gothic Quarter today.

The best single fragment is the section preserved inside the Barcelona History Museum (MUHBA), accessible from Plaça del Rei: a walkway carries you directly over Roman-era streets, drainage channels, and floor mosaics, and the wall rises above you to near its original height. The two Roman towers flanking the medieval gate on Plaça Nova are almost entirely original Roman stonework, incorporated into the neighbourhood by building around and above them rather than demolishing them.
