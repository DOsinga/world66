When MACBA opened in 1995 in the El Raval neighbourhood, the choice of location was a deliberate act of urban cultural policy. The Raval had long been Barcelona's poorest and most stigmatised district — the Barrio Chino of the 19th century, packed with immigrants, sex workers, petty criminals, and the city's marginalised communities. The city government's theory was that a major contemporary art institution would bring investment, rehabilitation, and cultural prestige to the neighbourhood without displacing its residents.

The theory was wrong, or at least incomplete. MACBA succeeded in transforming the northern Raval — the square in front of the museum became a gathering point for skaters and young people, galleries and design studios opened nearby, and the area's property values rose sharply. What the planners had underestimated was the speed and completeness of the subsequent gentrification: the immigrants and poor families who had given the Raval its character were gradually priced out, replaced by tourists, students, and creative-class professionals who wanted to live near what had become a cultural hot spot.

Meier's building — all white planes, ramps, and southern light — is one of the most significant pieces of architecture in 1990s Barcelona, and the permanent collection inside (Miró, Calder, Tàpies, Kiefer, among others) is substantial. The tension between the building's formal brilliance and the social consequences of its insertion into the neighbourhood is part of what makes MACBA interesting to think about, and the institution itself has tried, with varying success, to engage with those consequences through its programming.
