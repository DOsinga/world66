The Museu de Cera opened in 1973 in what had been the Banco Vitalicio building, a grand 19th-century structure at the point where the Rambla meets the Pla de Palau. The choice of location placed it at one of the historic transit points of Barcelona — the old city gate, the route between the medieval town and the port, the area where merchants and travellers had arrived for centuries. The building's previous incarnation as a financial institution gave it the high ceilings, ornate interiors, and theatrical grandeur that a wax museum requires.

The collection follows the established format of the genre — historical figures, celebrities, scenes from history and popular culture, horror tableaux in the basement. Wax museums operate on a straightforward but slightly vertiginous premise: the closer the likeness to the living person, the more unsettling the effect, and the genre has always exploited this uncanniness with varying degrees of self-awareness. Barcelona's version has updated its figures periodically since opening, retiring obsolete celebrities and installing new ones in a kind of cultural archaeology of popular fame.

The museum's neighbour at the foot of the Rambla is the Monument a Colom and the old port. Standing outside, the tourist district of the lower Rambla presses close — souvenir shops, open-top tour buses, the newspaper and flower stalls that are the last remnants of the Rambla's working life. The wax museum participates in this economy of spectacle, offering a slightly retro version of it that somehow feels more honest about what tourism actually is than the more sophisticated cultural institutions nearby.
