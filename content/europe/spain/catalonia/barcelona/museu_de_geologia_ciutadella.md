The Museu de Geologia occupies a distinction that is easy to overlook: it is the oldest public museum building in Barcelona still used as a museum. Designed by the architect Antoni Rovira i Trias and opened in 1882 — six years before the Universal Exhibition that it was built to anchor — it is a sober neoclassical structure in brick and stone, notable mainly for its longevity and the fact that nobody has ever found a better use for it. The museum holds collections of minerals, rocks, and fossils assembled by the naturalist Francesc Martorell i Peña, who bequeathed his collection to the city in 1878 on condition that a building be constructed to house it. The building is therefore the result of a private collection waiting for civic infrastructure to catch up.

The 1888 Universal Exhibition, held in what is now the Ciutadella park, transformed Barcelona's ambitions and its international standing. The Arc de Triomf, the Cascada, the park's reconfigured paths and gardens, the Palau de la Música commissioned in its wake — all flowed from the event's success. The Museu de Geologia preceded all of it, standing in the park when it was still a military zone, waiting for the city to decide what to do with the space. When the exhibition came, the museum simply became part of the ensemble.

The collection itself is worth more than a glance if you have any patience for natural history. The fossil collections cover Catalan geology with particular depth, and the mineral displays are organised in the meticulous Victorian fashion that made natural history museums feel like the inventory of the world. The building is quiet — far fewer visitors than the Ciutadella's more famous attractions — and the combination of the 1882 interior and the largely unchanged display logic gives it a quality of compressed time that more visited museums rarely achieve.
