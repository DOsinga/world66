The museum was founded in 1948 and built its collection through acquisitions and donations over the following decades, accumulating ethnographic objects from Africa, Asia, Oceania, the Americas, and the Mediterranean. The collection reflects the collecting practices of its era: objects were purchased from dealers, received from missionaries, and acquired from explorers and colonial administrators, with little systematic attention to the question of whether the communities that made them had consented to their removal or would ever see them again.

Since its substantial renovation and reopening in 2015 as the Museu Etnològic i de Cultures del Món, the institution has been grappling publicly with these questions in ways that older generations of ethnographic museums did not. The permanent exhibition now attempts to contextualise objects within the social and historical circumstances of their collection, rather than simply displaying them as curiosities or as aesthetic objects stripped of origin. This shift in curatorial approach reflects broader debates in European museums about colonial collections.

The museum's location on Montjuïc, in a building from the 1929 International Exposition, places it in a landscape deeply marked by the politics of display: the 1929 fair was built partly around a "Village of Spain" that exoticised regional and ethnic difference for the consumption of international visitors. The museum now occupies that same hillside with a very different set of intentions, and the contrast between the building's 1929 origins and its current critical project is itself part of what the institution has to navigate.
