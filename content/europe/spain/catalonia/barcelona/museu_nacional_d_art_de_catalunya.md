In the first decade of the 20th century, art historians from the Institut d'Estudis Catalans went into the high Pyrenean valleys and found something extraordinary: dozens of 12th-century churches whose interiors were still covered with the original Romanesque frescoes, largely intact because the villages were too poor and too remote to have undergone the renovations that had stripped similar paintings elsewhere in Europe. The frescoes showed Byzantine-influenced Christs in Majesty, hierarchies of saints, scenes from the Apocalypse — a complete visual theology painted in ochres, reds, and blues that were still vivid after eight centuries.

The discovery created an urgent problem: the paintings were beginning to attract the attention of international dealers, several of whom managed to buy and remove sections before the Catalan authorities reacted. The Institut's response was to employ specialists who had developed a technique for detaching frescoes from their walls — cutting them free in sections, rolling them on cylinders, and transporting them to Barcelona for remounting on new supports. The operation was controversial (the villages lost their art, even if the art was saved) but it preserved what would otherwise have been bought piecemeal by American collectors and scattered across the world.

The Romanesque collection now occupies the apse of the MNAC building on Montjuïc, where the reconstructed apses have been reassembled to approximate their original setting. Standing inside them, surrounded by the painted Christs and the hierarchies of saints, it is possible to understand what these remote mountain churches would have felt like to a medieval congregation — which is a remarkable experience to have in a 1929 exhibition building at the top of a city park.
