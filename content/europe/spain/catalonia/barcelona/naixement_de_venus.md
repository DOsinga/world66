Agustí Querol Subirats was one of the most technically accomplished Catalan sculptors of the late 19th century, trained in Barcelona and Rome, who received major public commissions in Spain, Mexico, and Cuba. His *Naixement de Venus* brought the Botticellian image of the goddess rising from the sea into the vocabulary of academic marble sculpture, with all the meticulous rendering of wet cloth and idealised skin that the tradition demanded and that late-Victorian audiences simultaneously admired and found transgressive.

The work was installed in the Parc de la Ciutadella, the Barcelona city park that was the setting for the 1888 Universal Exposition and that served as the city's primary public outdoor space in the decades that followed. The park's sculpture programme was extensive, combining monumental works with smaller decorative pieces in a way that reflected the ambitions of the expanding bourgeois city: art in public space as a demonstration of cultural seriousness.

By the time the sculpture was installed, the Botticellian Venus had been thoroughly naturalised in Western art — reproduced in prints, referenced in painting and sculpture, familiar to any educated viewer. But the academic sculptural tradition pushed the erotic charge of the image in a different direction from the painting: where Botticelli's Venus is otherworldly and remote, Querol's marble figure has the material presence of a body. The sculpture was considered controversial in its own time, and its location in a public park — where children played and families promenaded — was part of what made it so.
