Fountains with children-on-fish were a popular motif of late 19th and early 20th century decorative sculpture across Europe, reflecting the period's taste for mythological imagery in domestic and civic spaces — a taste inherited from the Baroque but filtered through the lighter sentimentality of the Romantic era. The fish-riding children are distant descendants of Baroque sea gods and tritons, scaled down to the decorative and the charming: the sea as a place of playful fantasy rather than terrifying power.

Barcelona's public parks and squares accumulated a considerable stock of decorative bronze and stone work in the decades around the 1888 and 1929 expositions, much of it now so thoroughly absorbed into the landscape that visitors and residents alike pass it without registering it as art. This invisibility is not a failure of the work but a measure of its success in becoming part of the city's fabric — decorative sculpture that calls attention to itself has failed at its primary function.

The piece in the Eixample's garden squares reflects the broader programme of embellishing the new bourgeois city's public spaces. The Eixample was designed by Ildefons Cerdà in the 1850s as a rational grid of blocks with interior courtyards and public gardens at the intersections; the reality — corners sold to developers, gardens built over — fell far short of the plan. But the surviving garden squares were furnished with fountains and sculptures that gave the neighbourhood its character as a civilised urban space, and small works like this one were part of that furnishing.
