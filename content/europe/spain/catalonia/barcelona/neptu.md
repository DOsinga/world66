The 1929 Exposición Internacional de Barcelona was one of the largest such events ever held in Spain, and it transformed Montjuïc from a bare hillside with a military castle into a sculpted landscape of pavilions, gardens, fountains, and allegorical statuary. The exposition was designed to celebrate Spanish industry and culture, and the iconographic programme drew heavily on classical mythology to give commercial and industrial themes a veneer of timeless prestige. Neptune — god of the sea, patron of maritime commerce and naval power — was a natural choice for an exposition in a major port city.

Most of the exhibition buildings were temporary structures demolished after the fair closed, and most of the decorative sculpture that accompanied them was dispersed or destroyed. What survived did so partly by accident and partly because the 1929 infrastructure was repurposed for the permanent park that the city maintained on the hillside after the exposition. The Parc del Migdia, on the less-visited southern slope of Montjuïc, preserves some of this material in a setting that feels genuinely removed from the tourist circuits.

The persistence of Neptune on Montjuïc connects to a long tradition: the 1888 exposition also used mythological marine imagery, and the cascade in the Parc de la Ciutadella (another exposition remnant) features Neptune in a prominent position. The figure of the sea god recurs in Barcelona's public sculpture as a reminder of the city's fundamental identity as a Mediterranean port, even now that the actual waterfront has been so thoroughly tourist-ified that it is easy to forget how central maritime commerce was to the city's history.
