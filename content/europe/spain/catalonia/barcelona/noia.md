The Spanish Civil War was catastrophic for Barcelona's stock of public sculpture. The Republican government, desperately short of metal for weapons and munitions as the war went on, organised collections of bronze from churches, public squares, and private properties. Religious images were the primary target — the anticlerical wave of the early war had already seen churches looted and burned — but decorative civic bronzes were also collected and melted down. The losses were substantial and largely unrecorded.

Small decorative bronzes like *Noia* survived the war for various reasons: they were easy to hide, or they were in locations that escaped the collections, or they were simply overlooked. The ones that remain are now all the more valuable as evidence of what the city's public spaces looked like before the war — the accumulated decoration of the bourgeois city's peak decades, from the 1880s to the 1930s.

The figure of a young woman in a public garden is one of the most conventional subjects in European decorative sculpture of this period — the Symbolist and Art Nouveau traditions both favoured female figures as allegories of nature, spring, beauty, and youth. *Noia* belongs to this tradition without making any particular claims to allegorical significance. It is simply a well-made bronze figure in a public space, the kind of thing that makes a city feel inhabited and tended, and it has been doing that modest work for a century.
