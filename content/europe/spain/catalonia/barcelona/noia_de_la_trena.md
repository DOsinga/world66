Enric Clarasó was a close friend of Ramon Casas and Santiago Rusiñol, the painters who were central to Catalan modernisme, and he exhibited alongside them at the Els Quatre Gats café that was the movement's social centre in the 1890s. His sculpture, however, was in a figurative vein closer to Rodin's quieter work than to the decorative excess of Modernisme's more celebrated pieces — intimate portraits and genre figures rather than allegorical monuments or architectural decoration.

The history of Catalan modernisme was written largely by critics who valued its most extravagant and ornamental expressions — Gaudí, Domènech i Montaner, Puig i Cadafalch in architecture; the exuberant floral tile work and wrought iron of the Eixample's buildings. Sculptors who worked in a more restrained figurative tradition were less easy to fit into the narrative and were often treated as secondary figures. Clarasó's reputation faded after his death in 1941, though recent scholarship has been more interested in his work.

*Noia de la trena*, a tender portrait of a young woman with a braid, is characteristic of his approach: carefully observed, technically accomplished, emotionally restrained. The braid itself is a detail worth noting — the *trena* (braid) in Catalan culture was a traditional hairstyle for unmarried women, and its appearance in sculpture and painting of this period carries a freight of symbolism about youth, innocence, and regional identity that the Renaixença and modernisme both drew on extensively.
