Via Laietana was blasted through the medieval city between 1908 and 1913, displacing around 2,000 residents and erasing a dense network of alleys that had survived since the Middle Ages. The post office at its port end was the avenue's anchor project, and its construction between 1914 and 1927 repeated the same logic: the building's footprint replaced inhabited blocks of the old Ribera district, the waterfront neighbourhood that had already been partly destroyed by the Citadel fortress in 1716.

The building's architect, Jaume Torres i Grau, worked in an eclectic mode that was already slightly dated by its completion: the facade combines a Neo-Renaissance base with decorative Art Deco detailing on the upper floors, a combination typical of official Spanish public architecture of the period. The main hall inside — a barrel-vaulted double-height space with a marble staircase — was intended to signal civic permanence and the efficiency of the modernising Spanish state.

The building still functions as Barcelona's main post office. The interior staircase is worth a look even if you have nothing to mail; it rises through the full height of the building in a sequence of landings and ironwork balustrades that feels more like a theatre than a government office. The post office counters occupy the ground floor while the upper levels house telecommunications infrastructure and administrative offices largely unchanged since the Franco era.
