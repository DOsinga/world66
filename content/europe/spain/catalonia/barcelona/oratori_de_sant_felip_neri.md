The Oratori de Sant Felip Neri stands on a small square in the Gràcia district that locals treat as a hushed counterpoint to the neighbourhood's busy plaças. The congregation of the Oratorians — followers of the sixteenth-century Roman priest Philip Neri, who believed faith should be joyful and musical — established this church in the eighteenth century, bringing with them a tradition of informal devotion quite different from the austere orders that dominated Catalan religious life.

What most visitors do not expect are the pockmarks that stud the lower stones of the façade. During the Spanish Civil War, fascist aircraft bombed Barcelona repeatedly, and a 1938 raid caught civilians sheltering in this oratory. More than forty people, many of them children evacuated from other districts, were killed. The scars were never repaired — a deliberate choice to preserve memory rather than erase it.

Philip Neri himself was a figure of remarkable irreverence: he reportedly shaved half his beard as a joke, wore odd clothes to humble himself, and once appeared before his bishop in a jester's costume. That spirit of playful holiness makes it worth reading the building not just as a site of tragedy but as the home of a tradition that has always insisted on laughter alongside prayer.
