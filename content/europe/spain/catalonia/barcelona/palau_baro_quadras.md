The Palau del Baró de Quadras on Avinguda Diagonal is one of the most architecturally singular buildings in Barcelona. Josep Puig i Cadafalch designed it between 1902 and 1906 for the Baron de Quadras, a wealthy industrialist, and the result is a building with what amounts to two entirely different personalities. The façade facing Diagonal is late Gothic revival: spiky stone canopies, heraldic carving, trefoil windows, the whole vocabulary of 15th-century Flemish civic architecture rendered in Catalan stone. The façade on the narrower Carrer de Rosselló, just around the corner, is Moorish-Mudejar: horseshoe arches, geometric tilework, carved plasterwork in the Andalusian manner.

Puig i Cadafalch made this bifurcated choice deliberately. He was exploring how Catalan Modernisme could synthesize the different historical traditions that had produced the Iberian aesthetic — the Christian north European Gothic and the Moorish south — without simply copying either. The two façades face each other as a kind of argument about what Catalan identity is made of. The building is now the Casa Àsia, a cultural centre devoted to Asian affairs, which adds another layer to the architectural identity question.

Walk the corner from the Diagonal face to the Rosselló face and try to believe they are the same building. The stone colour is similar, the date of construction is identical, the quality of craft is equally high. Everything else about them is different. Puig i Cadafalch was Gaudí's most distinguished contemporary — less famous now, and arguably more intellectually systematic. This is the building that demonstrates the difference between them.
