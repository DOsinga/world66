The Palau Centelles on Carrer de la Baixada de Sant Miquel is among the best preserved of Barcelona's late-Gothic merchant palaces, commissioned in the 1490s by Bernat de Centelles, a nobleman from Valencia who had accumulated wealth through the eastern Mediterranean trade networks of the Crown of Aragon. The building's main façade retains its Gothic windows with their delicate tracery, and the entrance arch is carved with armorial bearings that mix Castilian and Catalan heraldic traditions — a reflection of the period's political unification.

The interior courtyard and its open staircase are the building's set piece. The staircase follows a Flemish design vocabulary unusual in Barcelona — heavy square balusters with carved bosses at the joints rather than the slim arcade style favoured in contemporary Catalan courtyards. This suggests a northern craftsman was employed, probably via one of the Antwerp trading connections that brought Flemish panel paintings and metalwork into Catalan aristocratic households in the same decades. The connection between Barcelona and the Low Countries during this period was not merely commercial; it was artistic.

The building is in private institutional use and is not open to the public, but the façade and the glimpse of the courtyard through the entrance gate are accessible from the street. It sits on a block that has survived Barcelona's various rebuilding campaigns almost intact, giving a sense of the density and scale of the late-medieval residential core.
