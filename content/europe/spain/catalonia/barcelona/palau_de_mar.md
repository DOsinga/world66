The building was constructed between 1881 and 1900 as a bonded warehouse for goods arriving at the Port of Barcelona — at the time one of the largest port storage facilities in the Mediterranean. Its scale was commercial and functional: long, repetitive bays designed to hold crates, bales, and barrels from the Atlantic and beyond. The port at this period was the artery through which Barcelona's industrial economy breathed, importing raw materials and exporting manufactured goods across Spain's remaining colonial markets and into Europe.

By the late 20th century the building had been superseded by modern containerised logistics and stood unused at the edge of the Barceloneta waterfront. Its conversion into the Museu d'Història de Catalunya, which opened in 1994 as part of the post-Olympic redevelopment of the waterfront, was a deliberate act of repurposing: the space that had stored imported goods from across the world would now store the collected record of Catalan civilisation from the Palaeolithic to the late 20th century. The museum was also a political signal — opened as Catalan cultural institutions were recovering and asserting themselves after the Franco years.

The Museu d'Història de Catalunya covers everything from Roman Barcelona to medieval Catalonia's Mediterranean empire, the industrial revolution, the Civil War, and the dictatorship. It is one of the more thorough and candid national history museums in Spain: it does not flinch at the difficult periods. The building's terrace on the upper floor offers one of the better views over the harbour and the Barceloneta beach.
