Carrer de Montcada was built by medieval merchant families, but the Palau del Marquès de Llió reminds you that the street kept attracting wealth long after the Middle Ages. The palace dates from the eighteenth century, built by a family that had acquired the title of Marquès de Llió under the Bourbon settlement that followed the War of the Spanish Succession. The contrast with the Gothic palaces on either side is legible in the stonework: where the medieval buildings favour pointed arches, sculptural restraint, and vertical emphasis, the Llió palace displays the heavier, more horizontal forms of Baroque civic architecture, the kind of building that communicates solidity and dynastic continuity rather than pious aspiration.

The palace now houses the Museu de Cultures del Món, Barcelona's museum of non-European cultures, which fills its rooms with objects from Africa, Asia, the Americas, and Oceania. The eighteenth-century interior — courtyard, main staircase, principal hall — is well preserved, and the museum's approach is to let the building and the collection exist in productive tension, European aristocratic architecture as a frame for the world it was contemporary with.

What the palace demonstrates most clearly, though, is simply the persistence of Montcada as an address. From the thirteenth century to the eighteenth, the city's wealthy families chose this street. They rebuilt, remodelled, and replaced each other's houses over five hundred years, but they did not leave. The street's prestige outlasted the Gothic, outlasted the Crown of Aragon, outlasted the medieval trade networks that had first made it fashionable. The Llió palace is the last major addition to the row, and it stands as evidence that some streets simply hold their value regardless of what changes around them.
