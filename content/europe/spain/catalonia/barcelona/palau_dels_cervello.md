Carrer de Montcada is a parade of medieval ambition, and the Palau dels Cervelló is one of its anchor points. The palace dates from the fifteenth century, built by a family whose name appears across the history of the Crown of Aragon — the Cervelló were military commanders, royal administrators, and landowners whose fortunes tracked the fortunes of the Aragonese Mediterranean expansion. The palace's façade, with its Gothic arch entrance and the courtyard staircase visible through the gate, follows the standard form of merchant-aristocratic building in Barcelona: austere on the outside, generous within, organised around an interior space that was both practical and theatrical.

The building is now the Galeria Maeght, a branch of the Paris gallery that has occupied it since 1974 and which still hosts exhibitions of twentieth and twenty-first century art within the Gothic shell. The combination is less incongruous than it sounds. The Cervelló built for permanence, and the stone has survived seven centuries of use changes without losing its spatial logic. The courtyard staircase, the proportions of the main hall, the way light enters through the upper windows — these were decisions made for display and reception, purposes not entirely unlike those of a contemporary art gallery.

Standing outside on Montcada, the palace is one of several Gothic fronts in a row. The street was laid out in the thirteenth century, and the families who built on it were consciously competing with one another — each palazzo pushing slightly higher, slightly more elaborately finished than its neighbours. The Cervelló palace sits in the middle of this competition, neither the grandest nor the plainest, a stone record of the family's place in the medieval hierarchy.
