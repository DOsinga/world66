Palau Güell (1886–1890) on Carrer Nou de la Rambla is where Gaudí's mature style begins. Eusebi Güell, the industrialist who became Gaudí's great patron, gave him an almost unlimited budget and a site just off the Rambla to build a private palace. What Gaudí produced was like nothing else in Barcelona: parabolic arches in the entrance hall, a central dome pierced with holes to create a constellation of light, and a roofline of chimneys and ventilation towers covered in coloured ceramic fragments — the first appearance of the trencadís technique he would use for the rest of his career.

The palace served as Güell's main Barcelona residence and a venue for the cultural and political life of Catalan nationalism. Wagner's operas were discussed here; Catalan language and culture were patronised here at a time when both were officially discouraged. Güell used his wealth to fund a parallel Catalan cultural infrastructure, and Gaudí was central to it.

The building was seized by anarchists during the Civil War and used as a prison; the cells in the basement date from that period. It became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1984 and was comprehensively restored between 2004 and 2011. The rooftop, with its forest of ceramic chimneys, is one of the most extraordinary spaces in Barcelona — and almost always quieter than La Pedrera's roof two kilometres up the hill.
