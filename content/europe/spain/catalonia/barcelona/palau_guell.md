Palau Güell was built between 1886 and 1890 for the industrialist Eusebi Güell, who had met Antoni Gaudí at the Paris Exposition and immediately recognised in him someone willing to go further than any other architect in Barcelona. The two men formed a collaboration that would last three decades and reshape the city's skyline. This palace, wedged into a narrow street off the Rambla, was where that partnership found its first full expression.

The building is important partly for what Gaudí figured out inside it. The central hall rises through several floors to a parabolic dome pierced with small holes that filter daylight into a kind of architectural planetarium. Gaudí was working out, at full scale, the structural logic of the catenary arch — the curve a chain makes when suspended freely — that would govern his mature work. Every arch in the palace carries its load with minimal material because the geometry itself does the work.

The rooftop came as something of a surprise even to Gaudí's admirers. Twenty chimneys and ventilation towers, each clad in broken ceramic tile and twisted into organic forms, turn a functional necessity into a sculpture garden. Gaudí would repeat and elaborate this idea at Casa Batlló, Casa Milà, and Parc Güell, but Palau Güell is where the idea was born, and you can see it in its raw, exploratory form before it became a signature.
