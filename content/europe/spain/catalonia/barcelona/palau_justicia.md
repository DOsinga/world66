At the far end of the Passeig de Lluís Companys from the Arc de Triomf, the great bulk of the Palau de Justícia closes off the perspective. Designed by Josep Domènech i Estapà and Enric Sagnier i Villavecchia and built between 1887 and 1908, it is a massive eclectic building in the official manner of late 19th-century European public institutions — part French Beaux-Arts, part Baroque revival, its stone façade detailed with caryatids, heraldic shields, and allegorical figures representing Law, Justice, and the Public Good.

The building has an institutional character that its designers would have considered complimentary: solemn, permanent, slightly overwhelming. Inside, the great central hall and courtrooms were designed to express the authority of the state through scale and materials. This authority was exercised in ways that the building's decorative programme cannot acknowledge: the Palau de Justícia hosted the trials of labour organizers, anarchists, and political prisoners throughout the 20th century, and during the Franco era it served as a venue for military tribunals of Catalans charged with opposition to the regime.

The building now houses the civil courts of Barcelona's justice system and is not generally open to the public. The exterior, however, is one of the most detailed in the city. Walk around it slowly, particularly the rear and side elevations that face away from the main promenade: the carved stonework is consistent on all faces, a sign that the architects were not cutting corners on the parts of the building that tourists would not see.
