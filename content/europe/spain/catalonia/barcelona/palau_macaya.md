The sculptor Eusebi Arnau carved the main portal — a decision worth pausing on, because Arnau was also responsible for sculptural programmes at Casa Amatller and Casa Lleó Morera, and his contribution to Catalan Modernisme was as significant as that of any architect of the period. The portal at Palau Macaya is one of his most accomplished pieces: figures, foliage, and heraldic devices compressed into a Gothic arch that manages to feel both learned and alive.

Puig i Cadafalch was the architect of an argument. Where Antoni Gaudí drew on organic forms and Lluís Domènech i Montaner worked primarily in brick and historicist industrial aesthetics, Puig insisted that Catalan national identity was most authentically expressed through northern European medieval sources — Flemish gables, Romanesque corbelling, pointed Gothic windows — rather than the Mediterranean vernacular or classical tradition. Palau Macaya makes that argument through its facade: the roofline reads as Bruges as much as Barcelona, and that is entirely deliberate. Casa de les Punxes, his largest residential commission, makes the same case at larger scale a few years later.

The building was commissioned by Enric Macaya, an industrialist whose wealth was characteristic of the Catalan bourgeoisie of the period: textile money, recently made, socially ambitious, and genuinely interested in architecture as a form of cultural statement. The Macaya family sold the building in the mid-twentieth century; it passed eventually to the "la Caixa" Foundation, which uses it as a headquarters for social programme administration. The interior courtyard, one of the most refined domestic spaces Puig designed, is occasionally open to the public.
