The Palau de la Música Catalana was built between 1905 and 1908 by Lluís Domènech i Montaner, the most technically accomplished of Barcelona's Modernisme architects, as the home of the Orfeó Català — the Catalan choral society founded in 1891 as an expression of the Renaixença cultural movement. The site had previously been a monastery garden attached to the convent of Sant Francesc de Paula; the building occupies a tight slot in the Sant Pere neighbourhood, hemmed in by existing streets, which partly explains why its extraordinary facades face narrow lanes rather than open plazas.

The building is the only Modernisme concert hall lit entirely by natural light. This was not an aesthetic choice so much as an assumption: Domènech designed for afternoon choral concerts and did not install artificial lighting because he did not imagine the hall being used at night. The consequence is a building that performs differently at different times of day. The back wall of the stage is a stained glass composition — a vast inverted dome of coloured glass that floods performers with changing light — and the skylights and side windows fill the auditorium with filtered colour throughout the day. When concerts began to extend into evenings and artificial lighting was finally added in later decades, the effect was inevitably different from what Domènech had built.

The stained glass stage wall is the largest stained glass composition in the world used as an architectural element in a concert hall. The hall seats around 2,000. UNESCO designated it a World Heritage Site in 1997, alongside the Hospital de Sant Pau, also by Domènech. The Orfeó Català still rehearses and performs here; the building remains a working concert venue, which gives it an aliveness that pure monuments lack.
