The Baroque façade at La Rambla 99 is one of the grandest domestic buildings in Barcelona's old city. It was built between 1772 and 1778 for Manuel d'Amat i de Junyent, the Viceroy of Peru, who had accumulated enormous wealth in South America and returned to Barcelona intending to live in appropriate splendour. He commissioned the palace, watched it rise, and died in 1782 — just four years after it was completed — before he had fully settled in. His widow, Francesca de Fivaller, was considerably younger than he was, and she outlived him for decades, living in the palace and giving it the name it still carries: the Virreina, the Viceroy's wife.

The building now houses a cultural centre and exhibitions, but its main significance is still visible from the street. The façade is a tour de force of 18th-century Catalan Baroque: the central doorway is framed by pilasters and surmounted by a carved relief, the proportions of the windows are carefully calibrated, and the whole ensemble was intended to be read as a statement of imperial wealth. Barcelona at this period was just beginning to accumulate the commercial prosperity that would make it the dominant city of 19th-century Spain; the Palau de la Virreina was built by colonial money flowing back from the Americas.

The courtyard is sometimes open during events and exhibitions. Manuel d'Amat is buried in the church of Sant Antoni Abat, a short walk away. The Virreina herself is buried somewhere in Barcelona, though the location is less well known. The building she made famous still carries her title.
