Avinguda del Paral·lel has a name that is both geographically precise and somewhat improbable for a city street. The avenue runs along the 41 degrees 22 minutes north parallel of latitude — the same parallel that passes through Barcelona Cathedral — and the name was given officially in 1894 when the street was laid out as part of the Eixample expansion. A local journalist had noted the geographic coincidence in the press; the city council adopted the name almost as a joke, but it held.

What the avenue became over the following decades was not a joke. From the 1890s through the 1930s and then again briefly after the Civil War, the Paral·lel was Spain's most concentrated theatre and entertainment district — fifteen or more venues at its peak, ranging from formal theatres to cabarets, music halls, and circus acts. The Apolo, the Arnau, the Espanyol, the Condal: the names appear in memoirs and theatre reviews of the period as casually as the names of streets. Carmen Amaya, the Catalan-Roma flamenco dancer who would become one of the most celebrated performers of the 20th century, danced on the Paral·lel as a child. The painter Joan Miró's father took him to see the shows here when he was young.

The Civil War and the Franco years damaged and then killed the entertainment district. Censorship restricted what could be performed; poverty restricted who could attend; and the spread of television in the 1960s finished what repression had started. The theatres closed one by one. The Apolo survived and became a nightclub; the Gran Teatre del Grec, on Montjuïc above, carries the tradition of summer outdoor performance. In recent decades there have been efforts to revive the Paral·lel as an entertainment avenue, with some theatres reopening, but the density and vitality of the original era has not returned.
