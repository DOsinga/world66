The Escorxador — the municipal slaughterhouse — operated on this site from 1891 until it closed in 1979. When the city decided to convert it to a park, they commissioned Joan Miró to create the centrepiece. Miró, who was eighty-seven and knew he might not live to see it installed, designed *Dona i Ocell* (Woman and Bird), a mosaic-covered tower 22 metres tall whose biomorphic forms blend the female figure and a bird in the typically ambiguous way of his mature work.

The sculpture was installed in 1983, the year Miró died. Within weeks, nationalists had splashed white paint over the ceramic surface, reading the red, yellow, and white pattern as the Spanish flag rather than as abstract Catalan Modernista mosaic. Miró had been a committed Catalan nationalist himself; the irony was noted.

The slaughterhouse connection lingers in the park's older name, which many Barcelonans still use. The site is also notable as one of the few large open spaces in the Eixample grid — Cerdà's original plan had reserved the interior of every block as a garden, but almost all of those spaces were eventually built on. The Parc de Joan Miró is the exception that shows what the Eixample might have been.
