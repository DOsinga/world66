Eusebi Güell had visited the English garden cities — semi-rural residential developments for the wealthy professional class, set in landscaped grounds, at a remove from industrial city life — and wanted to create something similar on the Pelada hill above Gràcia. He commissioned Gaudí to lay out the roads, terraces, and common areas of what would be called Park Güell.

The development failed almost immediately. Of the sixty plots available, only two buyers came forward. One was the show home, which Gaudí himself bought and lived in for twenty years (it is now the Casa Museu Gaudí). Güell's family donated the whole complex to the city in 1918, seven years after construction stopped and three years after Güell's death. Barcelona turned it into a public park.

Gaudí's contribution to the infrastructure — the undulating ceramic bench, the hypostyle hall of Doric columns, the viaducts threading through the hillside — were designed for a private residential estate. They became, by accident of commercial failure, one of the most visited public spaces in Europe. The UNESCO listing came in 1984. In 2013 the most popular section was fenced off and ticketed, because the number of visitors had made it impossible to experience as a park at all.
