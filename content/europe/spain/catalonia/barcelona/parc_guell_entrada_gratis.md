Park Güell was conceived by Eusebi Güell in 1900 as a private residential development: a garden city of 60 houses on the hill above Gràcia, designed by Antoni Gaudí. Only two houses were built before the project failed commercially — the plots sold poorly, partly because the location was considered too remote from the city centre, and the development was abandoned. In 1918 the city of Barcelona bought the land and opened it as a public park.

The park's most famous elements — the Dragon Staircase, the Hypostyle Hall (the forest of columns), and the serpentine bench terrace — are concentrated in a relatively small central area that the city fenced off and made ticketed in 2013, when visitor numbers were causing damage. This "monumental zone" receives several million visitors annually and must be booked in advance.

The rest of the park — the forest paths, the stone viaducts that Gaudí built to carry the internal roads across the hillside, the lookout points, the remains of the entrance pavilions — is still free and largely unvisited by comparison. The viaducts are among Gaudí's more purely engineering achievements: three levels of stone arches following the terrain, using tilted columns to carry the load from the roadway above to the hillside below. Walking the park's forest paths rather than queuing for the terrace produces a different experience — the park as Gaudí intended it, a landscape with constructed elements, rather than a terrace with a park attached.
