The Mínims — the Order of Minims, founded by Francis of Paola in the fifteenth century — established a convent in Gràcia in the sixteenth century, when the village was still an independent settlement above Barcelona. The order took its name from the Latin *minimi*, the very least — a deliberate claim to greater humility than the Franciscan Friars Minor. Their houses were typically modest in scale.

In 1835, the liberal government of Spain passed the Ecclesiastical Confiscations Act, suppressing the male religious orders and confiscating their property. This was the moment that transformed the physical fabric of many Spanish cities: convents became schools, barracks, markets, prisons, or were simply demolished. In Gràcia, the Mínims church survived the suppression and was converted into a parish serving the lay population.

Gràcia retained its independent municipal identity until 1897, when it was forcibly annexed by Barcelona over the protests of its inhabitants. The parish of Santa Maria de Gràcia, housed in the former convent church, is one of the institutional anchors that gives the neighbourhood its persistent sense of being a village that was swallowed by a city rather than a district that always belonged to one.
