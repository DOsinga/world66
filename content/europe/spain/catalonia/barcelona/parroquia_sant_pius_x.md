Giuseppe Sarto, who became Pius X in 1903, was the son of a village postman from the Veneto who rose through the Italian church hierarchy and was elected pope after the death of Leo XIII. His papacy combined genuine reforming energy — he reorganised canon law, restructured seminary training, restored Gregorian chant, lowered the age of first communion — with a fierce reaction against what he called Modernism.

The 1907 decree *Pascendi dominici gregis* condemned as heretical a range of theological methods that tried to reconcile Catholic doctrine with historical-critical scholarship, evolutionary thought, and modern philosophy. The anti-Modernist oath it required of seminary teachers was enforced until 1967. The effect was to suppress a generation of Catholic intellectuals and close off the Church from the most productive currents of European thought for half a century.

Pius X was beatified in 1951 and canonised in 1954, during the pontificate of Pius XII. Parishes named after him typically date from that period, when the canonisation was a major event in Catholic devotional life. He is one of only two popes canonised in the twentieth century.
