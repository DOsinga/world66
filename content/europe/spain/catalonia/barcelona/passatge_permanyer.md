The Eixample — Barcelona's 19th-century expansion grid — was designed by Ildefons Cerdà as a rational utopia. Each block was to have a central garden accessible to all residents. The garden courtyards were mostly never built, taken over by further construction as the century progressed. What was built instead, in a handful of locations, were private interior passages: hidden residential lanes tucked behind the street façades and accessible only through archways that look, from the outside, like any other Eixample doorway.

Passatge de Permanyer is the best preserved of these. Pass through the arch on Carrer de Pau Claris and you emerge into a completely different scale: a narrow pedestrian lane flanked by two-storey Gothic-revival houses with individual front gardens, wrought-iron fences, potted plants, and an atmosphere of improbable calm roughly fifty metres from the traffic of central Barcelona. The houses date from 1864 and were designed in an English cottage style — specifically evoking the residential terraces and mews lanes of Victorian London that Cerdà had studied as models.

The passage was built by a private developer, Narcís Permanyer, for upper-middle-class families who wanted urban convenience combined with a private address. It remains residential. The gardens are maintained. During the week, if you walk the length of the lane slowly, you will probably see nothing except the architecture and the plants. On Sunday mornings, occasionally, a resident comes out to water something. The effect — a secret English village in the middle of Barcelona's most famous commercial district — does not diminish with familiarity.
