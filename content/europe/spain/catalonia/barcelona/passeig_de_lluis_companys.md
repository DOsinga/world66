The promenade was built as the ceremonial spine of the 1888 Universal Exhibition, running from the Arc de Triomf — the exhibition's main gate — down to the Ciutadella park where the pavilions stood. It was named for Lluís Companys only after the transition to democracy. Companys was President of the Generalitat de Catalunya from 1934 until the fall of Catalonia in 1939, when he fled to France. In August 1940 the Gestapo arrested him in Brittany and handed him to Franco's police. He was brought to Barcelona, tried by a military tribunal in approximately fifteen minutes, and shot in the moat of Montjuïc castle on 15 October 1940. He reportedly refused a blindfold and removed his shoes to die on Catalan soil barefoot. His last words: *Per Catalunya!*

The promenade itself has the formal geometry of exhibition infrastructure: wide, tree-lined, with the Arc de Triomf in Moorish-revival brick at one end — designed by Josep Vilaseca, using red Montjuïc brick — and the Ciutadella gates at the other. The buildings along it include the Palau de Justícia and several institutions that received their current purpose from the 1888 exhibition. It is used now for weekend cycling, protest marches, and the kind of unhurried promenading that wide boulevards invite.

The juxtaposition the street creates is unresolved and intentional. A pleasant Sunday walk under plane trees, named for a man murdered by the state for the crime of being Catalan and elected. The name is a political statement as much as a commemoration, placed in a city that takes the politics of its street names seriously.
