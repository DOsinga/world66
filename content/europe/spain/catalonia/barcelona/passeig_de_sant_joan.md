Cerdà's Eixample plan designated several broad avenues as the grid's structural promenades, and Passeig de Sant Joan was meant to anchor the northeastern side as Passeig de Gràcia anchored the centre. The difference in outcome tells you something about how money and ambition distribute themselves in real cities. Passeig de Gràcia attracted the textile barons and banking families who commissioned the Manzana de la Discordia buildings; Passeig de Sant Joan got the institutional commissions — the Palau de Justícia at the Arc de Triomf end, civic foundations, and the headquarters of professional bodies — which made it quieter, more austere, and substantially less photographed.

The Modernisme buildings are still outstanding. Puig i Cadafalch's Casa Macaya (1901), at number 108, has one of the finest Gothic-Modernista courtyards in Barcelona, now used as an exhibition space by the Fundació La Caixa. Further down, his Palau del Baró de Quadras (1906) is now the Casa Àsia cultural centre; its carved facade includes an extravagance of dragons and knights that Puig i Cadafalch never bothered to tone down. Domènech i Montaner worked the adjacent streets.

The passeig runs from the Arc de Triomf at its southern end — the triumphal arch built in Moorish-revival brick for the 1888 Universal Exhibition — north through the Eixample toward Gràcia. The median is wide enough for an actual promenade, which locals still use for it, particularly on weekend mornings when the bike lane fills and the café tables on the cross streets spill toward the central strip.
