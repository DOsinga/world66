Pau Casals (Pablo Casals in Spanish) was one of the most celebrated cellists in the history of Western music, born in El Vendrell in 1876 and trained in Barcelona. He rose to international fame in the early 20th century, performed before presidents and kings, and was considered the master interpreter of the Bach solo cello suites — which he had discovered in a Barcelona music shop in 1890, in a manuscript that everyone else had dismissed as exercises.

In 1939, when Franco's forces completed their conquest of Spain, Casals went into exile in Prades, a small town in French Catalonia just across the Pyrenean border. He refused to perform in any country that recognised the Franco regime, and he refused to return to Spain as long as Franco lived. This self-imposed exile lasted until his death in 1973 — 34 years — and made him the most visible symbol of Catalan resistance to Francoism in the international cultural world. He performed his last public concerts as gestures of resistance: at the United Nations in 1971, he played his composition El Cant dels Ocells — a Catalan folk song — as a statement of Catalan identity.

The monument in Pedralbes marks the area where Casals spent his formative years in Barcelona, studying and performing before his international career. He died in Puerto Rico, in exile, without returning to Catalonia. His body was repatriated after Franco's death and buried in El Vendrell. The Pedralbes monument, installed after the transition to democracy, is one of many commemorations scattered across Catalonia for a man who chose 40 years of exile over the accommodation with power.
