Ludwig Mies van der Rohe designed the German Pavilion for the 1929 International Exposition in Barcelona as a demonstration of the Weimar Republic's commitment to modern design. The building used travertine, marble, onyx, and glass in a composition of floating planes and open spaces that had no precedent — no columns obscuring the interior, no traditional ornament, no hierarchy of rooms. It was the building that made Mies famous.

The pavilion also contained two chairs — the Barcelona Chair — designed by Mies and Lilly Reich specifically for King Alfonso XIII and Queen Victoria Eugenia to sit on during the opening ceremony. The chairs entered production and are still manufactured today.

The pavilion was demolished when the Exposition closed in 1930. For fifty years it existed only in photographs, drawings, and the memory of architects who had visited it. In 1983, the Barcelona city government decided to reconstruct it. The team — Ignasi de Solà-Morales, Cristian Cirici, and Fernando Ramos — worked from Mies's original drawings and contemporary photographs to build an exact copy, opened in 1986. The reconstruction raises questions that are still debated: is the rebuilt pavilion authentic? Is a copy of a demolished masterpiece the same building? The answer depends entirely on what you mean by *the building*.
