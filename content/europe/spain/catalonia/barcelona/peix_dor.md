The enormous bronze fish that shimmers outside the Hotel Arts has no official connection to Barcelona's maritime past, yet it has become one of the city's most photographed objects. Frank Gehry designed it for the 1992 Summer Olympics as an abstract exercise in form: the scales are titanium panels layered over a steel armature, and the structure weighs around 54 tonnes. Gehry was in the middle of developing the curvilinear computer-aided techniques he would later use for the Guggenheim Bilbao, and Peix was effectively a prototype.

The fish stands at the point where the old industrial waterfront of Barceloneta met the sea before the Olympic construction transformed the coast. The entire neighbourhood behind it — the Vila Olímpica — was built on a former industrial wasteland that had cut the city off from the Mediterranean for more than a century. The fish, then, marks a boundary that no longer exists: the edge of a city that turned its back on the water and then, in the space of four years, built a new coastline.

Gehry himself has said that fish are his favourite form — he sees them as the perfect pre-architectural shape, evolved over 300 million years before the Greeks invented the column. The Peix in Barcelona was not his first fish sculpture, but it remains his largest freestanding one. Locals generally call it L'Estel de Ferro (the Iron Star) when they mean the Calatrava telecommunications tower on Montjuïc, but the Gehry fish has no popular alternative name: it is simply el peix.
