Before municipal water arrived in Barcelona's outlying villages, estates like Can Travi relied on private mines — the Catalan word *mina* here meaning a stone-lined underground channel, not an excavation for ore. The mine at Can Travi tapped a spring in the Collserola hills and carried water downhill by gravity alone, a technology unchanged since Roman times. The pillar you see here is the distribution point where the channel surfaced, and where tenant farmers and estate workers came to fill their jugs.

The neighbourhood of Horta-Guinardó preserves more of these hydraulic remnants than anywhere else in Barcelona, because the area remained largely agricultural until the mid-twentieth century. Elsewhere in the city, industrialisation and speculative development swept away the old water infrastructure; here, the terrain — steep, broken, inconvenient for builders — protected it. The Can Travi estate itself survives as a restaurant complex, its farmhouse converted but its bones intact.

What the pillar cannot show you is the extraordinary engineering underground: kilometres of hand-cut stone tunnels, some wide enough to walk through, maintained by specialist workers called *miners* who inspected and repaired them by torchlight. The Ajuntament has catalogued dozens of these channels beneath the Collserola foothills, and some can be visited on guided tours. The pillar above ground is the visible tip of an invisible hydraulic city.
