Sandwiched between the back of the cathedral and the Arxiu de la Corona d'Aragó, Plaça de Garriga i Bachs is so small it barely qualifies as a square. You could cross it in a dozen paces. But the bronze relief on its wall stops most people who happen through: it commemorates the twelve hostages — ordinary Barcelonans, among them the physician Joan de Déu Garriga i Bachs himself — executed here by French forces in 1809 during the Peninsular War. Their names are engraved beneath a sculpted scene of the firing squad.

The plaza's medieval paving has been relaid several times, but the proportions are unchanged from the period when it served as the cathedral chapter's private court. The Arxiu building behind it — formerly the Palace of the Lieutenant, then the Crown of Aragon archive — has one of the finest Renaissance courtyards in Catalonia, though access is via a separate entrance on Carrer dels Comtes.

Locals use the square mainly as a shortcut between the cathedral cloister garden exit and the side streets of the Gòtic. The memorial plaque was installed in 1905, almost a hundred years after the executions, during the Catalan cultural revival that reclaimed many such stories of resistance.
