Genet first came to Barcelona in the early 1930s, broke and homeless, and survived by begging and petty theft in the lower Raval — then known as the Barrio Chino, one of the most notorious red-light and criminal districts in Europe. He describes the specific streets and dosshouses in *The Thief's Journal* (1949): the Calle Mediodia, the beggars' quarter, the hierarchy of degradation among those who lived outside the law. The book is not a romanticization; it is a precise social document of a world that no longer exists in anything like its original form.

The square was named after Genet as part of the Raval's post-Franco rehabilitation — a recognition that the neighbourhood's pre-gentrification character had produced literature of permanent value. It is a small, quiet space: a few benches, some trees, a modest scale that fits its surroundings. The naming sits alongside the Plaça de George Orwell a few streets away as a deliberate act of urban memory, marking the Raval's literary history in a neighbourhood that has been substantially redeveloped since the 1990s.

The MACBA (Museum of Contemporary Art) opened a few hundred metres away in 1995 and anchored a wave of gentrification that transformed the area. The streets Genet walked still exist in outline but their population and character have changed. The square carries the name quietly, without interpretive panels or statuary — a literary marker that rewards those who arrive knowing what they are looking at.
