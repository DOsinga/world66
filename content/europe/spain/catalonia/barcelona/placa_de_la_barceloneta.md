The Barceloneta neighbourhood was laid out in a strict geometric grid by the military engineer Juan Martín Cermeño between 1715 and 1755. The grid is unusually precise even by Eixample standards: all streets are parallel or perpendicular, all blocks are 400 by 60 metres, and all apartment buildings were designed to be four storeys maximum (to prevent the neighbourhood from becoming a fortified position that could resist artillery). The Plaça de la Barceloneta is the only exception to the relentless linearity: a square at the neighbourhood's centre where the church of Sant Miquel del Port creates a public space.

The square was the original market place of the neighbourhood. The fishermen who lived in Barceloneta sold their catch here before the dedicated fish market was established closer to the port. It was also the social centre: the square's fountains, installed in the 18th century, were the water supply for a neighbourhood without domestic piping, and women gathered here daily to collect water and to exchange news.

The fountain at the square's centre dates from 1797 — a four-sided column with decorative medallions, one of the more complete examples of late 18th-century urban furniture surviving in Barcelona. The square was restored in the 1990s as part of the Olympic waterfront renovation, but the basic spatial relationship — church, square, fountain, surrounding grid — has not changed since Cermeño drew the plan.
