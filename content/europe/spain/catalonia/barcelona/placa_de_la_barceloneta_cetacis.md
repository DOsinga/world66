A small memorial in the Barceloneta neighbourhood commemorates the cetacean hunting that was practiced from the Barcelona shore from the medieval period to the 19th century. Sperm whales, pilot whales, and dolphins were hunted in the waters off the city when they came close to shore, a practice documented in Barcelona's maritime records from the 14th century onward.

The medieval cetacean hunt was a significant event when it occurred: a stranded or chased whale represented an enormous quantity of valuable material — meat for the poor, oil for lamps and machinery, bone for artisanal products. The hunt was regulated by the municipality, which claimed a portion of the whale's value as a tax. Detailed records survive of several Barcelona whale kills in the medieval period, including descriptions of the animals' size, the manner of their capture, and the distribution of the carcass.

The memorial is not widely known and is easily missed. It occupies a small space in the neighbourhood that was once the fishing community whose boats pursued these animals. The memory of cetacean hunting from a beach that is now lined with cocktail bars and tourist sun-loungers requires some imaginative effort. But the Mediterranean of the 14th century was a very different sea from the depleted, warm, tourist-visited body of water it has become, and the memorial is a reminder of what the sea produced and what the city took from it.
