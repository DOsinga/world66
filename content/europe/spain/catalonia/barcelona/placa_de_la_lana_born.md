The small square called Plaça de la Lana — Wool Square — sits in the El Born neighbourhood at the junction of several medieval streets. The name points directly to what made this neighbourhood prosperous: the wool trade. In the 14th and 15th centuries, when Catalonia was a maritime commercial power with trading houses from Bruges to Alexandria, wool was the primary raw material of the textile industry that underpinned Barcelona's wealth.

Catalan merchants did not shear the wool themselves. The raw fleece came from the vast sheep-grazing lands of Castile and Aragon, moved through trade networks, and arrived in Barcelona where it was bought, sorted, and distributed to the craftsmen who would card, spin, weave, full, dye, and finish it into cloth. The square and the streets around it — including the nearby Carrer dels Cotoners (Cotton Street) and Carrer dels Assaonadors (Tanners Street) — formed a neighbourhood whose street names were its economic record. Each craft had its zone, and the zones had addresses because buyers, sellers, and craftsmen needed to find each other.

The wool trade declined with the broader crisis that hit Catalonia in the late 15th century — the Black Death, the economic union with Castile that shifted Atlantic trade away from Mediterranean networks, and the wars that ravaged the countryside. By the time El Born was razed and rebuilt as a military zone after 1714, the wool trade it had been built to service was a memory. The square survived the destruction, which was concentrated slightly to the south, and kept its name as one of the neighbourhood's most legible historical traces.
