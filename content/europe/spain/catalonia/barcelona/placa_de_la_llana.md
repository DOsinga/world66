Wool was the engine of medieval Barcelona's wealth. The city imported raw fleece from Spain's interior, processed it in workshops throughout the Ribera district, and exported finished cloth across the Mediterranean. At the centre of that trade stood this square, where merchants, brokers, and cloth-finishers met to assess quality, fix prices, and settle contracts. The plaça functioned as an open-air exchange before the Llotja formalised such dealings in stone.

Llana comes directly from Latin lana, wool. The word barely changed in two thousand years of use because the commodity it described barely changed either: sheep, shearing, spinning, weaving, fulling. What changed was scale. Catalonia's textile industry in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries was sophisticated enough to undercut Italian competition in some markets and to sustain a merchant class wealthy enough to build the Gothic palaces that still line the nearby Carrer de Montcada.

The square sits in the Ribera neighbourhood, whose name (riverbank) reflects its original position on the shore before land reclamation pushed the waterfront south. The combination of water, port access, and a dense network of craft guilds made Ribera the economic core of the medieval city — and Plaça de la Llana one of its busiest corners.
