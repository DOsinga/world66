The Gloriosa of September 1868 toppled Queen Isabella II and drove her into permanent exile in France. It was a military pronunciamiento in origin, but the ground that made it possible had been prepared by years of republican organising — and Gràcia, then still an independent town outside Barcelona's walls, was among the most politicised communities in Catalonia. The Federal Republicans, who dreamed of a Spanish federal republic built from sovereign municipalities upward, had their stronghold here.

The revolution that the square commemorates set off a six-year period of constitutional experiments: a regency, a short-lived monarchy under an Italian prince who hated the job, and finally the First Spanish Republic of 1873 — which itself collapsed within a year into cantonal uprisings, military coups, and the eventual Bourbon restoration. Gràcia's radicals were in the thick of all of it. The square functioned as the neighbourhood's political forum: the place where proclamations were read and protests assembled.

Today the square is a terrace-chairs-and-children kind of space, lively in the evening when the Gràcia neighbourhood takes to the street. The revolutionary name sits over it lightly, but a glance at the Bar Revolució sign, the occasional political banner draped from a balcony, and the density of neighbours who actually know each other suggests the civic tradition has not entirely evaporated.
