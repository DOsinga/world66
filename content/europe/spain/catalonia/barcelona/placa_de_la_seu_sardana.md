The Plaça de la Seu is the large forecourt of Barcelona's Gothic cathedral, bordered by the cathedral's main façade to the north and the Pia Almoina and Palau del Bisbe to the east and south. It is the most photographed square in the Barri Gòtic and also, on Sunday mornings from around eleven o'clock, a public dance floor. Groups of mostly middle-aged and older Barcelonans gather in concentric circles to dance the sardana, holding hands in a ring, following the music of a cobla — a traditional Catalan ensemble — either live or recorded.

The sardana as a public tradition reached its current form in the 1880s, when the Catalan cultural revival (Renaixença) adopted the circle dance as a symbol of popular community. Its characteristic feature is the equality of participants: there is no lead dancer, no center performer, no audience as such. Anyone can join any circle by simply stepping in and taking hands; the steps are simple enough to pick up within a few minutes. The tradition was repressed under Franco — dancing sardana was illegal for a period in the 1940s — which transformed a folk dance into a political act and permanently associated it with Catalan identity.

The square itself was redesigned in the twentieth century; the cathedral's neo-Gothic main façade was only completed in 1913, giving the square its current formal character. Beneath the paving, archaeologists have located traces of the Roman forum that preceded the site.
