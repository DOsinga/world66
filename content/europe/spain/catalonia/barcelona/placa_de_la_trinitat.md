The Plaça de la Trinitat is easy to miss: a three-cornered space not much larger than a living room, squeezed between buildings on what was once the edge of the Jewish quarter. Its triangular shape is not a design choice but a consequence of three converging medieval street lines, none of which were planned in relation to each other. The square occupies the approximate footprint of the former church of the Holy Trinity, which was built for a confraternity in the 14th century and demolished in the early 19th.

The square's adjacency to the Call — the Jewish quarter that was destroyed in the 1391 pogrom — is historically significant. The church of the Trinity was one of those built on the ruins of synagogues and Jewish homes after the destruction. The renaming of the neighbourhood through Christian topography was systematic: Jewish street names were replaced with saints' names; former synagogue sites became chapels. The Trinitat church continued this pattern before it too was eventually erased.

What remains is the triangular space, now planted with a small orange tree and bounded by tall apartment walls. It is used primarily as a shortcut between the Carrer de la Boqueria and the Carrer de Ferran — two of the Gothic Quarter's busiest pedestrian streets — by people who know it exists. Most visitors to the neighbourhood pass within 30 metres of it without registering its presence. The name Trinitat is now the only visible trace of the church that gave it its shape.
