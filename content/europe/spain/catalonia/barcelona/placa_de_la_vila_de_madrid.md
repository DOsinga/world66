Roman law prohibited burials within city walls; the dead were interred along the roads outside the gates. This stretch of the *via sepulcra* ran west from the Roman gate of Barcino and was used for burials from the 1st to the 3rd century AD. The medieval city built over it; the modern city built over the medieval city; the tombs sat undisturbed until building works in the 1950s broke through to them. The decision to leave the excavation open rather than rebury it was not inevitable — it was a choice, made when Barcelona was not yet in the habit of preserving Roman remains in situ.

The excavation is accessible from the square without payment or barriers. Visitors can walk down into the sunken area and stand within arm's reach of the tombs. Some are simple stone markers; others are more elaborate funerary monuments with inscriptions that remain partially legible. The site is managed by the Museu d'Història de Barcelona, which provides interpretive panels, but the physical access is unrestricted.

The square itself — Plaça de la Vila de Madrid — takes its name from a 15th-century hospice for pilgrims travelling to Madrid, a reminder that this part of the Barri Gòtic sits just inside the line of the medieval walls. The layering of Roman burials, medieval hospice, and modern square in a single compact space is characteristic of Barcelona's old city, where two millennia of occupation have compressed into a few metres of ground.
