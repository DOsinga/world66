The virreina — literally the viceroy's wife, or in this case his widow — owned the land on which the square was eventually laid out. The title refers to the consort of one of the Spanish viceroys of Peru, whose family held property in what was then the independent town of Gràcia before it was absorbed into Barcelona. The name has stayed while everything else changed: the widow's land became a square, the square became the neighbourhood's heart.

On any given evening, the café tables under the plane trees are occupied by several simultaneous social worlds that barely overlap — elderly regulars, young parents with pushchairs, groups occupying the same chairs they have occupied for years. During the Festa Major de Gràcia in August, the square is decorated by residents of the surrounding streets and becomes the centrepiece of what is arguably Barcelona's most genuine popular festival: handmade, neighbourhood-funded, with no corporate sponsorship.

It is also where the neighbourhood protests begin. Gràcia has a tradition of civic mobilisation — against tourist apartments, against noise ordinances, for Catalan independence, against Catalan independence — and the Plaça de la Virreina is where people gather before marching. The square manages to be, simultaneously, completely ordinary and the place where things happen.
