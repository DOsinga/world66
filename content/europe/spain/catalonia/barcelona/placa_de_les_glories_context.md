When Ildefons Cerdà drew his 1860 plan for the Eixample expansion of Barcelona, the Plaça de les Glòries (Square of Glories) was not peripheral: it was the centrepiece. Cerdà envisioned a grand octagonal plaza at the intersection of three major avenues — the Gran Via de les Corts Catalanes, the Avinguda Diagonal, and what is now the Avinguda Meridiana — that would serve as the new civic and commercial heart of the expanded city.

The plaza was never built as planned. The Eixample was developed piecemeal by private investors who had no interest in creating the public infrastructure Cerdà had intended. The intersection remained an improvised traffic junction for much of the 20th century, dominated by a highway overpass built in the 1990s that cut the area into disconnected fragments. The overpass was itself demolished in 2013-2014 as part of a new urban regeneration plan.

The regeneration of Glòries is ongoing: the Torre Glòries (formerly Torre Agbar), the Els Encants flea market under its spectacular mirrored canopy, and the Museu del Disseny (Design Museum) have transformed the area since 2010. But the plaza remains unresolved as a civic space — a 150-year-old urban planning aspiration that has not yet been fulfilled, in the city that built an amusement park and a church simultaneously for 140 years without finishing either.
