At his feet, literally cast into the ground, is the plan he was never allowed to build. The bronze statue of Antoni Rovira i Trias shows the architect seated on a bench, and embedded in the pavement below him is a scale map of his rejected Eixample design — a radial scheme of boulevards spreading from the old city, quite different from the relentless grid Ildefons Cerdà imposed. Rovira's plan had won the city's own competition; the Spanish central government in Madrid overruled Barcelona and chose Cerdà's. The square, in the Gràcia neighbourhood, was the city's quiet acknowledgement of the injustice.

Gràcia itself amplifies the political irony. It was an independent municipality with a strong republican and anticlerical tradition — the town that resisted annexation to Barcelona longest, finally absorbed only in 1897. Placing a monument to an architect slighted by Madrid, in a neighbourhood that resented Barcelona's own authority, layers the grievances neatly. The surrounding streets still feel more village than city: narrow, sociable, with a pace the Eixample never had.

The square is modest by Barcelona standards — more a widening of the street than a formal space — which suits the subject. Rovira never built his masterwork. What he got was this: a bench, a statue, and his plan underfoot, to be walked over by the neighbourhood for the rest of time.
