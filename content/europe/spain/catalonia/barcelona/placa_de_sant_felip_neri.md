On 30 January 1938, two bombs hit the Plaça de Sant Felip Neri. Most of the 42 dead were children who had taken shelter in the church basement. The impact craters are still clearly visible in the lower stonework of the church facade — roughly circular gouges about 30 centimetres deep. The Francoist regime, which controlled the postwar historical record, attributed the attack to anarchists and suppressed evidence of Nationalist air force involvement. The craters were not officially acknowledged as the result of a Nationalist bombing until decades after the dictatorship ended.

The square itself is one of the most enclosed and beautiful in the Gothic Quarter: small, cobbled, and L-shaped, with a 17th-century fountain at its centre and the church of Sant Felip Neri (completed 1752) occupying the east side. It is off the main tourist routes and remains genuinely quiet — a sharp contrast to the Barri Gòtic streets a hundred metres away. A shoemakers' guild museum occupies one of the medieval buildings on the north side.

Gaudí is documented to have lived in the neighbourhood during his years working on the Sagrada Família and is said to have crossed this square daily. He did so for the last time on 7 June 1926, when he was struck by a tram on the Gran Via a few streets north. Unrecognized, he was initially taken to a hospital for paupers. He died three days later.
