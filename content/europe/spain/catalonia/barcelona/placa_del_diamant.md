Rodoreda left Barcelona with the Republican retreat in 1939 and did not return to Catalonia until 1979. She wrote the novel in Geneva in the late 1950s, working entirely from memory. The square she recreated — the opening dance, the summer light, the sense of ordinary life on the edge of catastrophe — was a place she had not stood in for twenty years. The novel was published in Barcelona in 1962, in Catalan, under the Franco regime, and was immediately recognized as a masterpiece. It has since been translated into more than thirty languages.

The bronze statue of Colometa — the novel's protagonist — stands in the square's southwest corner. Sculpted by Xavier Medina-Campeny and installed in 1982, it shows a woman with her arms thrown back and her mouth stretched open in a silent scream. The pose is taken from a moment near the novel's end, when Colometa returns to the square and releases something she has been carrying since the war. The statue is easy to miss; there is no plinth, no monument. She stands at street level among pigeons.

The square itself is modest by design — a shaded neighbourhood space in the Gràcia district with a modernist-era kiosk and benches. Families use it without ceremony. The literary weight it carries is invisible until you know to look for it; then the ordinary square becomes something else.
