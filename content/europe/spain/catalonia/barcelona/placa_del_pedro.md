A *pedró* was the post to which the condemned were tied: for public flogging, for branding, or for display after execution. Medieval Barcelona's punishments were administered in public, and the Raval — the district outside the old city walls, where the poorest lived — had its own site of civil terror. The name has outlasted the gallows by centuries, now attached to one of the Raval's most animated squares without most of the people drinking coffee there giving it a second thought.

The Capella de la Mare de Déu de la Misericòrdia at the edge of the square is the physical anchor that connects the medieval Raval to the present. Built in the early 18th century, it predates most of the surrounding neighbourhood and has survived every wave of demolition and renewal that remade the Raval around it. It remains an active parish chapel, which is unusual in a quarter whose other institutions — convents, hospitals, charitable houses — were dissolved, sold, converted, or destroyed during the convulsions of the 19th century.

The square itself is one of the more genuinely local spaces in the Raval: smaller than the Rambla del Raval, less trafficked by tourists than the Boqueria end of the neighbourhood, with a mix of residents that reflects the Raval's current demography — South Asian, North African, Latin American, and the remaining old Barcelonins who remember the quarter before it became a subject of urban policy.
