The reception of Columbus at the Palau Reial Major is the fact that reorients this square. In April 1493, Columbus arrived back in Spain and was summoned to the court of Ferdinand and Isabella, which was then in Barcelona. He was received in the Saló del Tinell — the great barrel-vaulted throne room that faces the square — and presented his evidence: live indigenous people, parrots, gold ornaments, and accounts of the islands. The news of the Americas entered European consciousness in this room.

The Saló del Tinell itself is extraordinary independent of that history. Built between 1359 and 1370 by Guillem Carbonell for King Peter IV, it is a Gothic hall of six bare stone arches spanning 17 metres each — one of the largest unsupported medieval vaults in Europe. No columns, no interruption. The Capella de Santa Àgata (1302) is built directly on top of the surviving Roman wall, and the Torre del Rei Martí — a five-storey lookout tower added in 1555 — rises above the roofline.

Below the square, the MUHBA (Museu d'Història de Barcelona) has excavated 4,000 square metres of Roman and early medieval Barcino directly underfoot. The visit takes you down through layers: Roman streets, a 1st-century fish sauce factory, a 6th-century early Christian basilica with a bishop's baptistery. The square is the cap of a buried city.
