The Plaça d'Espanya was designed as the grand entrance to the 1929 International Exposition, with the Palau Nacional of Montjuïc visible at the end of the long Avinguda de la Reina Maria Cristina beyond it. The square's architecture — the twin Venetian-inspired towers, the large fountain with allegorical sculpture, the curved Venetian-style buildings — was intended to frame an approach to the hill as theatrical as the approach to an opera stage.

The Arenes de Barcelona, the circular structure at the square's edge, was built in 1900 as a bullfighting arena seating 16,000 spectators. It operated until 2011, when Barcelona became the first city in Spain where the autonomous government banned bullfighting. The arena was then converted into a shopping and leisure centre by the British architect Richard Rogers, who added a glass dome and roof terraces above the original bullring walls while preserving the 19th-century Moorish-revival exterior.

The conversion is a genuine architectural achievement — the original building's circular form is preserved while entirely new uses are inserted — but it also encodes a political and cultural shift. The bullfighting ban was as much a Catalan nationalist statement (distancing Catalonia from a practice associated with Castilian culture) as an animal rights measure. The former arena, now housing a cinema, restaurants, and chain stores under a glass dome, represents the reappropriation of a Spanish cultural institution for Catalan commercial purposes.
