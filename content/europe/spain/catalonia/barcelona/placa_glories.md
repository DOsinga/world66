On Ildefons Cerdà's 1859 plan for the Eixample, the point where the Gran Via de les Corts Catalanes, the Avinguda Diagonal, and the Avinguda del Paral·lel converge was marked as the new city's symbolic centre — the place equivalent to a cathedral square or a royal palace, the point from which Barcelona would orient itself into its 19th-century future. Cerdà called it the Gran Plaça, and he drew it as a large public space at the confluence of the three main axes of the new district.

It did not happen. The convergence of the three avenues was built, but the plaza was filled by a tangle of roads, an elevated ring road, and the infrastructure of a city that had grown without civic ambition in the 20th century. For most of its existence, the space was known as a traffic nightmare — a Gordian knot of intersecting lanes, underpasses, and elevated motorway that made pedestrian crossing nearly impossible. The Torre de les Glòries (formerly Torre Agbar) went up nearby in 2005, a landmark that acknowledged the area's symbolic potential without transforming it.

The elevated ring road that cut across the plaza was finally demolished in 2013, and a large-scale redesign project began to try to realize — 150 years late — something closer to Cerdà's intention. The Parc de les Glòries is currently being completed in phases: a large park with pavilions and green space, connecting the neighbourhood to the nearby Parc del Clot. The process is ongoing. Cerdà imagined the heart of a new city; what has been built instead is a record of everything the city chose over the century and a half that followed his plan.
