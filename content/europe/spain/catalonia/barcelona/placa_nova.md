Barcino's main gate — the Porta Praetoria — stood here. The two cylindrical towers that flanked it survive: one incorporated into the medieval Bishop's Palace, one standing semi-freestanding. They are Roman military construction from the reign of Augustus, roughly 2,000 years old, and you can walk to within arm's reach of them. The wall they anchored ran around the entire city; the towers on the Plaça Nova are the most accessible surviving section.

Facing the Roman towers across the square is the headquarters of the Barcelona College of Architects (COAC), completed in 1962 by Xavier Busquets. The entire first-floor facade is covered by a sgrafitto frieze designed by Pablo Picasso — at the time living in exile in Cannes, having not set foot in Spain since 1934. He produced the design without seeing the building or the square it faces, working from photographs and from memory. The frieze depicts prehistoric figures, Catalan folk dancers, and the gegants — the giant parade figures that are a fixture of Catalan festivals. The contrast across the square, Roman stone on one side and Picasso's line drawing on the other, is among the more genuinely strange juxtapositions in the city.

The square is a working civic space as much as a monument. On Sundays, sardana dancers form their circles here in the shadow of the cathedral. During major festivals, the gegants themselves muster in the square before processing through the Gothic Quarter. At Christmas, a market runs down from the cathedral steps and fills the open space.
