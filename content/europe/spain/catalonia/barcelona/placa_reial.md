The lamp posts are easy to walk past without registering their authorship. Gaudí entered a city council competition for street furniture in 1878, one year after graduating as an architect, and won. The six-armed post in the centre of the square and the three-armed post near the fountain both carry his characteristic iconography: the winged helmet of Mercury at the top, and dragon-wing brackets supporting the arms. These are not retrospective attributions — the helmet and wings were deliberate symbolic choices, not decorative convention. They are among the earliest examples of Gaudí's visual language, laid down before he had any major commissions.

The square was built in 1848 on the site of the Capuchin convent of Santa Madrona, demolished in the 1835 confiscations that stripped the Church of its urban landholdings across Spain. The design by Francesc Daniel Molina follows the neoclassical colonnaded model of squares like the Place des Vosges in Paris: three sides arcaded, the fourth open to the Rambla. The central fountain, the Three Graces, dates from the original construction. The palm trees were planted later and now overtop the roofline.

Through the 1980s and 1990s the square was Barcelona's backpacker hub — the place where travellers arriving at the cheap pensions on the Rambla came to drink, buy cheap hash, and sit. It has cleaned up considerably since then but retains its bars, its late-night energy, and its Sunday stamp and coin market, which has run continuously since the 1940s.
