Plaça Reial, the colonnaded square of Neoclassical grandeur behind La Rambla, has two lamp posts at the two ends of its central axis. They are not the kind of lamp posts that register immediately as objects of particular note — they are simply part of the square. But these are Antoni Gaudí's first public commission, designed in 1878 when he was 26 years old, days after receiving his architecture diploma. They are the oldest surviving work by Gaudí anywhere in Barcelona.

The posts are bronze, six-armed, their shafts decorated with dragons and serpents in a style that already hints at the naturalistic vocabulary Gaudí would develop over the next forty years. The helmet atop each post is winged, a reference to Mercury, the god of commerce, which connected them to the adjacent Boqueria market area. The six arms branch outward in a way that anticipates the branching structural forms of Gaudí's mature work — not historicist, not copying any historical style, but already trying to find the forms that nature uses.

The City of Barcelona commissioned lamp posts for several public spaces in 1878. Gaudí designed these for Plaça Reial and also a version for Pla de Palau, near the post office. The Plaça Reial examples have survived intact. They are not roped off or protected by glass — they stand in an open public square that is famous mainly for its restaurants, its street life, and the slightly menacing energy it sometimes acquires at night. The lamp posts are there during all of it. They predate everything Gaudí is famous for, and they tell you, if you look closely, that he was already entirely himself.
