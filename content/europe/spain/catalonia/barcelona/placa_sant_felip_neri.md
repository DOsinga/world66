The small circular square of Sant Felip Neri is one of those Barcelona corners that appears in travel photography as an example of Gothic Quarter picturesque: cobblestones, an old well, a Baroque church façade, a single tree. The photographs rarely mention what is embedded in the lower walls of the church on the left side of the square: dozens of small circular pockmarks, 10 to 15 centimetres across, distributed across the stone surface roughly two metres from the ground.

These are shrapnel scars from an aerial bomb dropped on 30 January 1938. The square was a refuge point: children from the nearby Sant Felip Neri school had taken cover here during an air raid. The bomb struck the square directly. The official count was 42 dead; the actual number was probably higher, including many children. The marks in the church wall were made by the metal fragments that spread outward from the impact point. They were never repaired. The decision to leave them — or simply the absence of any decision to remove them — has preserved a record in the stone that no plaque or inscription could quite replicate.

The square has a complicated history even before 1938. The space was created in 1837 when the convent of Sant Felip Neri was demolished during the liberal suppression of religious houses; the current church is the surviving element of a larger complex. The guild of shoemakers, who were closely associated with the church, installed bronze shoes in the paving as a memorial to their trade's connection to this space. Stand in the square at midday and look at the church walls carefully: the pockmarks are at a consistent height from the ground, the geometry of a pressure wave.
