Behind the cathedral apse, where the tourist flow thins out and the medieval stone loses its gift-shop gloss, the Placeta de Manuel Ribé occupies a sliver of space that most maps barely bother to label. It is the kind of square that exists because the medieval city could not always fill every corner with a building — the geometry of the Barri Gòtic is full of these accidental gaps, given names and benches as if to make them respectable.

Manuel Ribé was a Catalan sculptor who worked in Barcelona in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, a figure of local standing rather than international renown. The square carries his name as a modest civic gesture, the sort of thing the city did for its craftsmen and artists in the era when the Eixample was going up and the Gothic quarter was being excavated and partly reinvented. Standing here, you are a metre or two from the back of the cathedral cloister wall, close enough to hear the geese that the cathedral keeps in its interior garden — a medieval tradition, maintained because the flock of thirteen geese commemorates Santa Eulàlia, patron of the city, who was thirteen years old when she was martyred.

The square has no great monument, no famous pavement, no historical plaque that explains why you came. That is precisely its appeal. The cathedral's bulk dominates from one side; a narrow lane leads back toward the Via Laietana on the other. In between, for a few minutes, the noise of the Barri Gòtic drops away and the scale of the medieval city — low, dense, built for people on foot — becomes briefly legible.
