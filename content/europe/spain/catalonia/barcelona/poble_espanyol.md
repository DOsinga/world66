The architects Josep Puig i Cadafalch and Miquel Utrillo assembled the 117 buildings from surveys conducted across Spain. Each structure is a full-scale reproduction of a real building from a specific region — Aragonese mudéjar towers, Andalusian whitewashed lanes, Castilian plazas, Basque farmhouses — arranged into a walkable village on Montjuïc. Construction took twelve weeks in 1929. No other country built anything comparable for the 1929 exhibitions, and no similar structure from that generation of world's fair villages has survived intact elsewhere.

The village's survival through the Civil War was contingent rather than guaranteed; the Francoist authorities briefly used the enclosed site as an outdoor detention facility in the early 1940s before repurposing it as a craft and tourism venue. Under the dictatorship, the Poble Espanyol served an ideological function as a showcase of unified Spanish regional identity — the same identity it had been built to celebrate in 1929, now reframed for a different regime. The irony that Puig i Cadafalch, a committed Catalan nationalist, had designed the showcase was not acknowledged.

Post-Franco, the site has operated as a craft village, events venue, and open-air museum, with workshops producing ceramics, glass, and textiles in the reproduction buildings. The architecture is the exhibit: the Andalusian quarter looks different from the Castilian quarter looks different from the Galician quarter, and the transitions between them were designed by architects who had studied each original in situ. Whether or not this constitutes authenticity, the craftsmanship of the 1929 construction is itself a historical object.
