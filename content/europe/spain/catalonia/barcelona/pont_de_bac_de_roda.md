Before the Alamillo in Seville, before the Puente de la Mujer in Buenos Aires, before the World Trade Center Transportation Hub, Calatrava built this. The Pont de Bac de Roda is where his signature language — white-painted steel arches inclined at aggressive angles, structure as choreography — first appeared at full scale, spanning the railway tracks between the Clot and Sant Andreu districts in what was then an undistinguished industrial corner of Barcelona.

The structural logic is layered. Two pairs of inclined arches, one over each carriageway, lean outward in opposite directions; their tension rods meet the deck at intervals and the whole system is held in equilibrium by the interaction between arch and road. Pedestrian walkways cantilever from the outer arches and descend to stairways and small parks on either side of the tracks — a detail that turns a functional crossing into something closer to a civic promenade. Calatrava was thirty-five when the commission was awarded.

The bridge was part of the Olympic urban-renewal programme that rebuilt significant tracts of Barcelona's post-industrial north in the years leading to 1992, though it predates the Olympic decision by a year. At the time it was simply a neighbourhood connection, meant to end the isolation that the railway had imposed on the Clot for decades. It is now listed as a protected element in the city's architectural heritage catalogue — the only such designation among Calatrava's Barcelona works.
