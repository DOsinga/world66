Barcelona's medieval walls were demolished in stages throughout the nineteenth century, as the city burst out of its historic boundaries and the Eixample grid was laid over the fields beyond. The demolition was celebrated at the time as progress — the walls had become a stranglehold on commerce and growth — but within a generation, historians were mourning what had been lost. The Porta de Santa Madrona survived because the dense urban fabric around it made demolition awkward and expensive.

Santa Madrona was a Christian martyr whose cult was popular in medieval Catalonia, and the gate bearing her name marked one of the main exits from the walled Raval towards the port. Merchants, pilgrims, criminals, and soldiers all passed through it. The gate also controlled access to the foreshore, where fishing boats landed and where the city's galleys were beached for repair. Whoever held the gate held the city's relationship with the sea.

The surrounding Raval neighbourhood was, for most of its medieval history, a zone of religious institutions: convents, hospitals, and the institutions that cared for the city's poor and sick. The wall and its gate enclosed this charitable district as firmly as it enclosed the commercial city to the east. Today the Raval is one of Barcelona's most diverse and densely populated neighbourhoods, and the old gate stands as a reminder that the boundaries between inside and outside have always been contested and provisional.
