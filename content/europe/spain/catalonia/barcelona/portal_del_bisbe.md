Most people on Carrer del Bisbe are photographing the neo-Gothic bridge that connects the Palau de la Generalitat to the Casa dels Canonges overhead — a 1928 addition that looks medieval, and is not. Below the bridge and behind the crowd, embedded in the buildings on either side of the street, are two cylindrical towers of Roman stone that actually are what they look like: defence towers built in the 1st century CE to guard the main gate through the walls of Barcino.

The towers are not freestanding — they have been built into the surrounding structures over nineteen centuries and you can only see sections of their curved masonry. But what you can see is substantial: courses of large Roman stone blocks, unmistakably ancient, the fabric of a colonial city that was already four hundred years old when the Western Roman Empire fell. The gate these towers flanked was the northeastern entrance through the city's rectangular Roman perimeter.

Walk slowly and look at the wall surface at street level on both sides of the passage. The boundary between Roman construction and medieval and later additions is visible if you know to look for the change in stone size and laying pattern. The gate is named for the Bishop's Palace nearby, not for the Romans who built it. They would not have recognized the name; they would have recognized the towers.
