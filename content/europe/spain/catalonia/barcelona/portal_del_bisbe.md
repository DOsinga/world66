When the Romans founded Barcino around 10 BC, they enclosed it with walls so well built that Barcelona's medieval builders simply kept using them. The Portal del Bisbe — the Bishop's Gate — preserves one of the original Roman access points through those walls, now visible as an embedded arch beneath later construction. The stone here dates to the same period as the great Roman monuments of Nîmes and Segovia; Barcelona simply buried its rather than displaying it.

The gate takes its current name from the Bishop's Palace, which occupies one side of the narrow lane. For most of the medieval period, this passage was controlled by the diocese, and the Bishop's authority over who entered this corner of the city was a source of constant friction with the municipal government. The nearby Bridge of the Bishops (*Pont del Bisbe*), a neo-Gothic confection added in 1928, was so resented by Catalan nationalists that a local legend holds that the city will fall if the carved skull on the bridge is ever removed — a curse that neatly captures the political charge of even ornamental architecture in Barcelona.

The lane itself, only a few metres wide, follows the original Roman street grid with remarkable fidelity. The insulae of Barcino, the rectangular blocks of the Roman city, still govern the property boundaries of the Gothic Quarter today. Walking through the Portal del Bisbe, you are not merely crossing a threshold; you are tracing a line drawn by Roman surveyors two thousand years ago.
