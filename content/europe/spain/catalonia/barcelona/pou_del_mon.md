Sculptor Antoni Llena created *Pou del món* in the early 1990s as part of the wave of public art commissioned for Barcelona's Olympic transformation. The title sets up a conceptual joke and a genuine meditation simultaneously: a well goes down into the earth, but the earth is a globe, so where does the well come out? The image of drilling through the planet to reach the opposite side is a childhood thought experiment that Llena elevates into a piece of civic furniture.

Poblenou at the time of the commission was still half industrial wasteland, its factories emptied by deindustrialisation but not yet claimed by the creative economy that would follow. Public art in this context was partly decorative, but also a signal — a declaration that this was a neighbourhood worth beautifying, worth taking seriously. *Pou del món* sits in a small plaza that now feels integrated into daily life, surrounded by apartment blocks and local cafés, its philosophical pretensions worn smooth by familiarity.

The Catalan word *pou* (well) carries literary associations that the Spanish *pozo* does not quite replicate: Catalan poetry has long used the well as a symbol of depth, of memory, of what is preserved below the surface of visible life. Llena's title draws on that tradition even as it undercuts it with the absurdist geography of a well that passes through the planet. The tension between the serious and the playful is, in many ways, the signature of Barcelona's public art at its best.
