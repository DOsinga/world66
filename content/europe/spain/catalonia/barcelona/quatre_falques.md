The name *Quatre Falques* — Four Wedges — refers both to the sculpture itself and to the informal name given to the surrounding district, which developed in the 1960s as immigrants from Andalusia and other parts of Spain arrived in Barcelona seeking factory work. The wedge shape suggests something being driven in, inserted, forced into a gap — and the residents of Nou Barris were themselves wedged into the margins of the city, in housing built fast and cheap on whatever land was available.

The Nou Barris district (its name means 'Nine Neighbourhoods') was for decades one of the most politically active parts of Barcelona, with strong neighbourhood associations that fought the Franco-era municipal government for basic services: paved streets, schools, parks, and public transport. The sculptures were commissioned after the return of democracy as a form of civic recognition — an acknowledgement that this peripheral district had its own culture and its own history worth commemorating.

The choice of abstract geometry rather than figurative art reflects the democratic-era preference for public art that does not impose a single narrative. The four wedges can be read as working-class solidarity, as the convergence of immigrants from different regions, as the geometric abstraction fashionable in 1980s public art, or simply as four large and satisfying shapes in a plaza. The openness of the interpretation was itself a political statement in a city still learning what it meant to make art for everyone.
