The second section of La Rambla, the Rambla de les Flors, has carried its name since 1853, when the city of Barcelona granted flower sellers the right to set up permanent stalls along the central promenade. Before that date, flowers were sold on the Rambla informally, as they were in many Spanish cities, but the grant of permanent concessions transformed the trade into an institution. Within a generation the stalls had become one of the defining images of Barcelona — banks of color in a city whose medieval streets were mostly grey stone.

The flower market runs through the morning and into the afternoon. The stalls sell cut flowers, potted plants, and seeds; the assortment changes with the season, though the stalls closest to the Palau de la Virreina tend toward more elaborate arrangements, while those near the Liceu lean to everyday bunches. On the feast of Sant Jordi — April 23rd, Catalonia's version of Valentine's Day — the entire Rambla becomes a river of roses, as Catalans exchange books and flowers according to a tradition that long predates the designation of the date as a literary occasion.

Federico García Lorca spent time in Barcelona in 1925 and wrote about the flower stalls here with the intensity he brought to everything Andalusian or Mediterranean — the colors, the women vendors, the way the market smelled in the morning. He was murdered in 1936, early in the Civil War. The stalls he saw are the same stalls, occupied by some of the same families whose grandparents held the original concessions. The flowers, unlike almost everything else on La Rambla, are still mostly for people who live in the city.
