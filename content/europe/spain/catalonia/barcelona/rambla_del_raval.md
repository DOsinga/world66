The Rambla del Raval was created by the city government between 1999 and 2000 as part of a programme to reduce the density of the Raval neighbourhood, which at the time had approximately 100,000 inhabitants per square kilometre — one of the highest population densities in Europe. Sixty-two buildings were demolished to create the 300-metre boulevard. The displaced residents were relocated; many of the families who had lived in the Raval for generations were moved to social housing on the city periphery.

The boulevard is lined with palm trees and has a wide pedestrian central strip. At its southern end sits a massive bronze cat by the Colombian sculptor Fernando Botero, installed in 2001. Botero's signature style — everything inflated to extreme roundness — produces a cat that is simultaneously charming and slightly threatening. It has become a local landmark.

The human costs of the Rambla's construction were significant and remain contested. The neighbourhood was already undergoing gentrification pressure; the demolitions accelerated displacement and transformed the social character of the surrounding blocks. The Raval is now home to a large South Asian and Maghrebi population that arrived largely after the demolitions. What the urban planners got right is the fundamental transformation of a neighbourhood that had no public space whatsoever; what they may have got wrong is how the creation of that space was managed.
