The demolition was substantial: an entire city block of 19th-century fabric — tenements, workshops, small businesses — was cleared to insert a 300-metre pedestrian promenade into the densest neighbourhood in Barcelona. The city's stated rationale was aeration (the Raval's density was among the highest in Europe), tourism access, and neighbourhood renewal. Critics called it eviction by urban planning: the residents displaced were predominantly poor, elderly, or recently arrived immigrants who could not afford to return to the new Raval that rose around the new open space.

What came with the Rambla del Raval: the Barceló Raval hotel (the round building at the northern end, completed 2008), Fernando Botero's enormous bronze cat installed in 2003, a weekend street market, and considerable footfall from tourists accessing the MACBA and CCCB cultural centres nearby. The cat is large, smooth, and invites climbing; children and tourists oblige. Whether any of this constitutes neighbourhood improvement depends on whose neighbourhood you think it was and is.

The Rambla is genuinely pleasant to sit on — wide, sunny, with the scale that the surrounding Raval streets deliberately lack. It functions well as a public space. That it was imposed rather than evolved, and that its costs were borne by people who had no voice in the decision, is the fact the design cannot resolve. Walk it knowing both things: it is lovely, and it replaced something else.
