The Rambla dels Caputxins takes its name from the Capuchin convent that occupied a large block on its eastern side until the Liberal government suppressed religious orders in 1835 and sold off convent properties across Spain. The Capuchins were Franciscan friars; their Barcelona house had been one of the larger religious establishments in the city. After the suppression the buildings were demolished and the land developed, eventually becoming the site of the Gran Teatre del Liceu, Barcelona's opera house, which opened in 1847. The Liceu and the Café de l'Òpera face each other across this section of the Rambla in a pairing that has defined Barcelona's cultural geography for 175 years.

The Liceu has burned twice — in 1861 and again in 1994, when a spark from a welder's torch started a fire during restoration work that destroyed the auditorium in hours. The rebuilding after 1994 was completed in 1999 and restored the original 19th-century interior while adding modern stagecraft. The building that opens onto the Rambla, with its ornate facade and the crowds of pre-performance theatre-goers, is largely a reconstruction of a reconstruction.

George Orwell arrived in Barcelona in December 1936 to fight for the Republic and found himself caught in the street fighting of May 1937, when anarchist and Stalinist factions turned on each other in a conflict that prefigured the ultimate Stalinist suppression of the independent left. He spent four days on the rooftop of the Poliorama cinema, just a few steps up the Rambla, and in the nights he could hear firing from the Liceu direction. Orwell describes the Rambla dels Caputxins in Homage to Catalonia as a street where, a few weeks earlier, he had strolled with his wife and now crouched behind a sandbag on one side of it with armed opponents on the other side.
