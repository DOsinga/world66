La Rambla is not one street but five, each with its own name and history, laid end to end along the course of a medieval stream. The northernmost is the Rambla dels Estudis, which takes its name from the Estudi General — Barcelona's university, founded in 1401 by King Martí l'Humà. The university occupied a building on the right-hand side of the street as you walk toward the sea, and for three centuries this stretch carried the energy of students, professors, booksellers, and the debates that cluster around places of learning.

The name survived the building. After the War of the Spanish Succession, when Barcelona fell to the Bourbon forces of Philip V in September 1714, one of the explicit punishments the new king imposed on the defiant city was the demolition of the Estudi General. Philip V distrusted the Catalans and their institutions; removing their university was both practical — it might produce more opposition — and symbolic. Students were dispersed to Cervera, a small Castilian-sympathizing town in the interior, which was awarded a new royal university as reward for its loyalty. Barcelona would not have a university again until 1842.

The stretch acquired a second nickname — Rambla dels Ocells, the Bird Rambla — because for most of the 20th century it hosted a market of caged birds. Canaries, parakeets, finches, and exotic specimens were sold from wooden stalls along the central promenade. The bird market moved in the 1980s, replaced by a stamp and coin market on weekends. The university is commemorated by nothing visible; the name is all that remains.
