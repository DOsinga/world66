The Rambla de Santa Mònica is the final and southernmost section of La Rambla, ending at the Columbus monument and the old port. It takes its name from the Convent de Santa Mònica, an Augustinian house founded in 1626, one of the last religious foundations to occupy the Rambla before the Liberal suppression of the 1830s. The convent building survived the 19th century and was eventually converted in 1988 into the Centre d'Art Santa Mònica, one of Catalonia's publicly funded contemporary art spaces. The conversion preserved the convent's basic structure while hollowing it out for gallery use; the building's ecclesiastical exterior and the art world interior exist in a tension the centre has never quite resolved.

This end of the Rambla has always been the roughest. It is the closest section to the port, and for most of Barcelona's history as a maritime city that meant sailors — a population that needed wine, companionship, and gambling, and that funded the establishments which provided all three. The lower Rambla was the entry point into the city for anyone arriving by sea, which until the railways meant almost everyone coming from anywhere outside Catalonia. The area around the Rambla de Santa Mònica and the Barri Xinès to its west became the city's most concentrated red-light district, a geography that persisted well into the 20th century.

The contemporary street is calmer, though the sex trade has not entirely gone from the surrounding lanes. Portrait painters and pavement artists set up along this section; the Centre d'Art stages openings that attract people with no connection to the neighbourhood. At the end, where the Rambla meets the sea, is a roundabout with the Columbus column at its centre, pointing, as always, out toward a horizon that in Columbus's time nobody had yet crossed.
