The name Ramon Bou i Espinosa will not appear in most histories of Barcelona, which is precisely the point of the memorial. The early twentieth century in Barcelona was a period of extraordinary violence between employers, the state, and the organised working class — a period historians call the *pistolerisme*, when both sides hired gunmen to assassinate opponents. Hundreds of workers and union organisers were killed, most of them as anonymous in death as they had been in life.

Poblenou was at the heart of this conflict, its factories and workshops densely unionised through the anarcho-syndicalist CNT. The neighbourhood's workers staged some of the most significant strikes in early twentieth-century Spain, demanding the eight-hour day, better wages, and the right to organise. Many paid with their lives. The memorial to Ramon Bou i Espinosa is one of the few public acknowledgements in Barcelona that ordinary people — not generals, not politicians, not industrialists — also shaped the city's history.

The recovery of this kind of local memory accelerated after the death of Franco and the restoration of democratic institutions in the late 1970s. Neighbourhood associations, historians, and families of victims spent decades identifying the dead and finding places to remember them. The result is a scattered geography of small memorials across the old working-class districts — Poblenou, Sants, Gràcia — that requires patience and curiosity to find, but which together constitute an alternative map of the city.
