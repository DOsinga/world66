The sculptor Andreu Alfaro, who created *Raspall del vent*, was one of the leading figures of Spanish geometric abstraction in the second half of the twentieth century. His characteristic idiom — slender steel rods, graceful curves, the play of light and shadow — is fully present here, but with an added dimension: the piece actually moves. The 'brush of the wind' lives up to its name, responding to air movements that most passersby would not even notice, making visible what is otherwise invisible.

Kinetic sculpture in public space poses a particular curatorial challenge: the mechanism must be robust enough to survive weather, vandalism, and decades of movement, while remaining delicate enough to respond to gentle air. Alfaro solved this through careful engineering of the pivot points, and the piece has survived its urban setting better than many more static works. Maintenance records for Barcelona's public sculpture reveal a sobering landscape of cracked pedestals, missing plaques, and bronze darkened past legibility — this one still moves.

The Poblenou *rambla* where the sculpture stands was designed in the 1990s as a democratic public space, a neighbourhood street rather than a tourist attraction. The art programme that includes *Raspall del vent* reflects that ambition: works that reward slow attention rather than demanding it, that become part of the texture of daily life. You might walk past it a hundred times before you notice it moving, and then you watch it for longer than you intended.
