The Rec Comtal (Count's Canal) was one of the most important pieces of infrastructure in medieval Barcelona. Built in the 10th or 11th century — possibly during the reign of Ramon Berenguer I — it drew water from a weir on the River Besòs at the point where the river reaches the coastal plain, approximately 7 kilometres northeast of the city. The canal ran southwest through what is now the Sant Martí, Poblenou, and Sant Pere neighbourhoods, reaching the mills and workshops of the Born and delivering water to the medieval city.

The canal powered grain mills, paper mills, and tanning workshops along its route. It was managed by the Counts of Barcelona and subsequently by the city government, which collected fees from the industrial operations that depended on it. Medieval water rights in Catalonia were precisely defined and frequently litigated; the Rec Comtal's distribution rules are among the most detailed medieval administrative documents surviving from Barcelona.

The canal fell into disuse in the 19th century as Barcelona's industrial water supply was reorganised around piped water from the Tibidabo springs and later from reservoirs. The physical channel was gradually absorbed into the street network. The trace of the Rec Comtal survives in the curving alignments of several streets — including the Carrer del Rec in the Born — that follow the canal's course rather than the medieval grid. On a map, the Rec Comtal's ghost is visible as the set of streets in the Born and Sant Pere that don't quite follow the geometry of the surrounding neighbourhood.
