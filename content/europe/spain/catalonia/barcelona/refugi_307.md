Below the streets of Poble Sec, a network of tunnels runs for 200 metres under the residential blocks. This is Refugi 307, one of the estimated 1,300 civil defence shelters that Barcelona's population dug for themselves during the Spanish Civil War, when the city became the first European capital to be systematically bombed from the air in wartime. The shelter was begun in 1936 and expanded through the war, dug manually by neighbourhood residents without professional assistance.

Barcelona was bombed repeatedly by the Nationalist air forces — by Italian aircraft operating from Mallorca under the Condor Legion framework — throughout the war. The bombing of the city, which killed hundreds of civilians, was a deliberate terror tactic and a rehearsal for the systematic aerial bombardment that would characterize the Second World War three years later. George Orwell was in Barcelona during part of this period, convalescing from a throat wound at the Hotel Continental on La Rambla; his account in Homage to Catalonia describes the air raids with characteristic matter-of-factness.

The shelter in Poble Sec was rediscovered and excavated in the 1990s. It holds about two thousand people at capacity and has its own ventilation system, hospital bay, and separate areas for children. Tours of the shelter run regularly; the entrance is on Plaça de Valerie Powles, marked by a small interpretive sign. Inside, the tunnel dimensions are human-scale — wide enough for people to pass but not generous — and the walls are still the original stone and concrete. It is cool underground, even in summer.
