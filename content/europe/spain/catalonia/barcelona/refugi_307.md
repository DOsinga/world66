Barcelona was the most bombed city in the Spanish Civil War, subjected to sustained aerial bombardment by Italian and German aircraft flying in support of Franco's Nationalist forces. Between 1936 and 1939, the city absorbed hundreds of raids, killing over a thousand civilians and wounding many more. The people who dug Refugi 307 did so with shovels and pickaxes, working by night to hollow out more than two hundred metres of tunnel under the Carrer de Nou de la Rambla in a matter of months.

The shelter was a community project organised through the neighbourhood civil defence committee, funded by a modest tax on local businesses and maintained by volunteers. It had its own electricity supply, ventilation, a well, a medical bay, and latrines — a self-sufficient subterranean village. The population of Poble Sec, a dense working-class district, knew the sound of the air-raid siren intimately and learned to reach the shelter within the three minutes that separated a distant aircraft from the moment of impact.

After the war, the shelter was sealed and forgotten — too associated with Republican resistance to be commemorated under the Franco regime, and too obscure to be discovered by the urban renewal projects of the 1960s and 1970s. It was rediscovered in the 1990s and excavated by a team of volunteers who removed decades of debris to reveal the original structure almost intact. The guided tours now offered are among the most affecting historical experiences available in Barcelona, not because of any dramatic exhibit, but because the tunnel itself is the document.
