At the centre of the building is a room that has not changed its function in 260 years. The oval anatomy theatre, completed with the building in 1764, was designed for the systematic public dissection of human cadavers before an audience of medical students and curious citizens — a practice that Enlightenment medicine considered essential to scientific progress. The tiered stone seating rises in a full ellipse around a marble table at the centre. A skylight provides the natural overhead light that 18th-century surgeons required. The proportions are intimate: perhaps 80 spectators, close enough to see the work.

The building was designed by Ventura Rodríguez, the Madrid-based architect who also designed the Basilica of Nuestra Señora del Pilar in Zaragoza and the Pamplona Cathedral facade. His commission in Barcelona was part of a broader Bourbon effort to institutionalise Enlightenment learning across Spain — the Real Colegio de Cirugía de Barcelona, which occupied the building from its founding in 1760, was one of the most advanced surgical schools in Europe, producing techniques and practitioners that influenced medicine across the Spanish empire.

The building stands on the Carrer del Carme in the Raval, a location chosen deliberately: the Hospital de la Santa Creu, the city's main hospital, was across the street, providing a continuous supply of bodies for the dissection theatre. The Academy still occupies the building and holds regular public tours; entry to the anatomy theatre is included. There are few spaces in Barcelona — or anywhere — where the intellectual ambitions of the 18th century are this physically present.
