The Raval is often described as Barcelona's 'other side of the Ramblas,' the dense, historically poor neighbourhood that the city's boosters spent centuries trying to clean up. That cleaning-up reached its most violent expression in the 1840s, when military governor Espartero ordered the demolition of hundreds of buildings — and the displacement of tens of thousands of residents — to create a cordon around the Citadel. The Reina Amàlia excavation gives a glimpse of what was lost.

The archaeological traces here represent streets and structures that existed for centuries before the nineteenth-century interventions, when the Raval was not a slum but a mixed neighbourhood of convents, workshops, and modest housing. Medieval Barcelona was not divided into bourgeois and working-class districts in the modern sense; different social groups lived in close proximity, sorted more by craft and religious affiliation than by income. The street pattern preserved in the excavation reflects that older, more mixed urbanism.

Urban archaeology in Barcelona has accelerated since the return of democracy, as building projects are required to allow excavation before construction begins. The results have sometimes been startling: Roman walls under parking lots, medieval cemeteries under apartment blocks, the footprint of monasteries demolished in the nineteenth-century disentailment. The Reina Amàlia site adds another layer to this palimpsest, a city written and overwritten so many times that every excavation reveals something previously unknown.
