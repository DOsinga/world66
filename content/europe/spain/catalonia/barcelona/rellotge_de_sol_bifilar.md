The bifilar sundial is not widely known outside horological circles, which is part of what makes finding one in a Barcelona park such a pleasant surprise. Invented in 1922 by the French mathematician Hugo Michnik, the bifilar design uses two parallel threads at different heights to produce a shadow that traces a straight line across a flat dial — solving an optical problem that had plagued traditional gnomon-based designs. The result reads local solar time with remarkable accuracy.

The sundial in Poblenou was calibrated specifically for Barcelona's latitude (approximately 41.4 degrees north) and its position within the time zone. This matters more than it might seem: Barcelona is geographically far to the east of the meridian that defines Central European Time, meaning the sun rises and sets noticeably later than clocks suggest. In midsummer, solar noon in Barcelona falls around 14:00 by the clock — a fact that explains, and justifies, the city's famously late meal times. The sundial tells a truer time than your phone.

Scientific instruments in public space have a long history in Barcelona, from the astronomical clocks of the medieval cathedral to the meteorological equipment that still occupies corners of the Ciutadella park. The city has always had a strong tradition of popular science, fed by the Escola Industrial, the University, and the many learned societies that flourished in the nineteenth century. The bifilar sundial continues that tradition, offering a small lesson in physics and astronomy to anyone willing to stop and read the instructions mounted beside it.
