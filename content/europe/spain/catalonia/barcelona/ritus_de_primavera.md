Igor Stravinsky's *Le Sacre du Printemps* caused a riot at its Paris premiere in 1913 — the dissonance, the primitive rhythms, the explicit eroticism of the choreography were too much for an audience expecting decorative ballet. The Barcelona sculptor who chose this title for a public work was invoking that transgressive energy deliberately, placing a reference to scandal and renewal in a neighbourhood — Gràcia — that had its own long tradition of popular festival and cultural independence.

Gràcia was an independent municipality until it was absorbed into Barcelona in 1897, and it has never quite forgiven the annexation. The neighbourhood maintains its own identity through the Festa Major de Gràcia each August, when residents decorate their streets in competition with each other, transforming the entire district into a temporary open-air gallery. The themes are often irreverent, sometimes political, always ambitious — a civic creativity that *Ritus de primavera* seems to echo in its suggestion of collective celebration.

The spring rite in Stravinsky's ballet ends in the death of the chosen maiden, who dances herself to exhaustion as an offering to the gods of renewal. It is not an entirely comfortable myth. Public art that carries this kind of ambivalence — beauty and violence, celebration and sacrifice — tends to outlast purely decorative work, because it gives residents something to think about rather than merely something to look at. Whether passers-by in Gràcia recognise the Stravinsky reference or not, the title lodges in the mind.
