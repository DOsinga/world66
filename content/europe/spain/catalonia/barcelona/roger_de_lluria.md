Roger de Llúria's most famous boast — that no fish dared show itself above the waves unless it bore the four bars of the Aragonese crown on its tail — was not modesty but a statement of fact, at least geographically. As Grand Admiral of Aragon and Sicily in the 1280s and 1290s, he commanded the western Mediterranean with such authority that no rival fleet dared challenge him for years at a stretch. His victories at Malta, Castellammare, and the Cape of Orlando established Catalan naval supremacy in the Mediterranean and made possible the empire of Aragon that would eventually include Sardinia, Sicily, Athens, and Neopatria.

Born in Lauria in southern Italy but brought to the Catalan-Aragonese court as a child, Roger de Llúria represents one of the most interesting aspects of medieval Catalan expansion: the way it absorbed talent from across the Mediterranean and made it Catalan. The *Almogàvers* — the ferocious mercenary infantry who terrified Byzantine Greece in the early fourteenth century — were similarly diverse in origin, united by a common military culture rather than ethnic identity.

The bust in the Gothic Quarter places him near the Cathedral, in the ancient heart of the city that his fleets served. Barcelona's maritime empire is the foundation of its medieval golden age, and Roger de Llúria is its most dramatic embodiment — a figure whose career reads like fiction but is exhaustively documented in the chronicles of Bernat Desclot and Ramon Muntaner, two of the masterpieces of medieval Catalan literature.
