The rondas — Ronda de Sant Antoni, Ronda de Sant Pau, Ronda de la Universitat, Ronda de Sant Pere — form the inner ring road that separates Barcelona's old city from the Eixample. They were created in the 1850s and 1860s when the city's medieval walls were demolished, a long-delayed event that the inhabitants had been demanding for decades. The walls had stopped Barcelona from expanding outward, penning the population into an overcrowded space that public health reformers of the period described in terms that still shock — density and mortality rates comparable to the worst industrial slums in Europe. When the walls came down, the stone was carted away and the rondas were laid in their place.

Ronda de Sant Antoni marks the western boundary of El Raval, the quarter that sat against the old wall and suffered most from the overcrowding it enforced. The street is wide by old-city standards, a standard nineteenth-century boulevard, lined with the kind of commercial and residential buildings that went up quickly once the wall's footprint was free. The Mercat de Sant Antoni sits just off it, a fine iron market hall of 1882 that was recently restored. On weekends, the market's exterior hosts a secondhand book market that has been running for decades.

Standing on the Ronda now, the old city is on one side and the Eixample's regular grid is on the other. The boundary is not quite a wall — the transition is gradual, the Eixample chamfered corners pushing up against the irregular old-city blocks — but the change in urban texture is unmistakable. On one side, streets that evolved over centuries; on the other, a rational plan that was imposed in a single generation. The Ronda is the seam between them, and the demolished wall it replaced is the reason the seam exists.
