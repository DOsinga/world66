The *rosa dels vents* — rose of the winds — is the compass rose that appears on medieval portolan charts, the remarkable navigational maps produced in Catalonia, Mallorca, and Genoa from the thirteenth century onwards. These charts, drawn on sheepskin with extraordinary accuracy, were the tool that made the expansion of Mediterranean trade possible. The names of the winds they carried — Tramontana, Gregal, Llevant, Xaloc, Migjorn, Llebeig, Ponent, Mestral — are still used by Catalan sailors today, eight points that divide the horizon into a system older than modern meteorology.

The installation in the Gothic Quarter is both a navigational symbol and a piece of urban poetry. The eight wind names mark the cardinal and intermediate points, but they do so with a lexical richness that the English compass cannot match: *tramontana* comes from 'beyond the mountains' (the Alps or Pyrenees, depending on where you stand), *migjorn* means 'midday south' (the sun at noon), *gregal* means 'Greek' (the direction of Greece). Each point is a compressed history of Mediterranean geography and trade.

Portolan charts were produced in Barcelona and Palma de Mallorca for export across the known world, and the city's cartographers — many of them Jewish, working in the Mallorcan school's tradition — were among the most sophisticated geographers of the pre-modern era. The Abraham Cresques atlas of 1375, now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, is the masterpiece of this tradition: a world map of dazzling ambition that reaches from the Atlantic to China. The *rosa dels vents* underfoot connects this pavement to that extraordinary heritage.
