Between the late 1940s and the early 1960s, Barcelona absorbed the largest wave of internal migration in its history. Hundreds of thousands of people arrived from Andalusia, Murcia, Extremadura, and other poor regions of Spain, drawn by the city's factories and construction sites. The municipal government had made no provision for them. So they built their own houses — with salvaged bricks, corrugated iron, whatever materials they could gather — on the steep hillsides and waste ground at the city's edges.

The Nou Barris district was one of the main receiving areas for this migration, and the ruins preserved here represent the first generation of that improvised urbanism. The houses were not temporary in the minds of those who built them; families invested years of weekend labour and whatever money they could save, adding rooms as children were born and incomes grew. But the construction was often structurally marginal, built without permits and therefore without access to services, and when the city finally extended its infrastructure to these areas in the 1970s and 1980s, many of the original structures were demolished and replaced.

The decision to preserve these ruins rather than clear them entirely was taken as part of the neighbourhood's post-democratic regeneration, when residents of Nou Barris began to insist that their history — the history of migration, self-organisation, and community building — was worth preserving. It is an unusual memorial: not to a single event or person, but to a collective act of improvised survival that built one of the city's most densely inhabited districts from nothing.
