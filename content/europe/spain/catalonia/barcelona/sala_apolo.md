Sala Apolo opened in 1943, four years into the Franco dictatorship, which makes it remarkable that it opened as a ballroom at all. The early 1940s were the most restrictive years of the regime — cultural life was suppressed, Catalan was banned in public — but people still needed to dance, and Apolo was one of the few licensed venues where they could. What began as a concession to controlled leisure became, over decades, the opposite of what the regime intended.

By the 1980s it had shifted to rock, punk, and alternative music, hosting the Catalan rock movement — Sopa de Cabra, Els Pets, and their contemporaries — that was simultaneously a musical and a linguistic project. Singing in Catalan was political; Apolo was where it happened in front of an audience.

The building at Carrer de la Nou de la Rambla 113 is architecturally intact in ways that matter: the original ballroom ceiling, with its curved plasterwork, still hangs above whatever is happening below it. The two-room format — main hall and the smaller Nitsa club — gives the venue an unusual flexibility for its age.
