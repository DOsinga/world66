Salvador Roca i Roca is another of the working-class martyrs whom Poblenou's community has chosen to remember in public space — part of the broader recovery of erased histories that followed the end of the Franco dictatorship. The memorial is small, unpretentious, and easy to miss, which makes finding it feel like a more significant act than visiting a grand monument. It asks you to seek, rather than presenting itself.

The early twentieth century in Barcelona was marked by a cycle of strikes, lockouts, and political assassinations that killed hundreds of people on both sides. The *pistolerisme* of the 1910s and 1920s — so called because both employers and unions hired gunmen to eliminate opponents — was a form of low-level civil war that preceded the formal conflict of 1936-1939 by two decades. Workers like Roca i Roca were targets not because they were important in any conventional sense, but because they were effective: known in their neighbourhood, trusted by their workmates, capable of organising a strike.

Poblenou's working-class culture was deeply shaped by the *ateneus* — worker-run cultural centres that provided education, libraries, theatre, and political debate — and by the CNT, the anarcho-syndicalist union that was the dominant organisation among Barcelona's factory workers. The memorials scattered through the neighbourhood are a kind of counter-archive, preserving the names and stories that official history omitted. Together they constitute a different map of the city, one organised not by power but by solidarity.
