Ambrose of Milan is not a saint you encounter often in Catalonia — the Catalan church has its own rich hagiographic tradition, and the great fathers of the Latin church tend to take second place to local martyrs and Marian devotions. The presence of a parish named for Ambrose in Sant Andreu de Palomar (the old village around which this neighbourhood grew) suggests a specific historical moment: the nineteenth-century ecclesiastical reorganisation of Barcelona, when new parishes were created to serve the rapidly growing industrial suburbs.

Sant Andreu de Palomar was an independent municipality until it was absorbed into Barcelona in 1897, the same year as Gràcia and several other outlying villages. The church of Sant Ambròs served the expanding working-class population of the district, which was filling with textile factories and the workers who operated them. The dedication to Ambrose — patron saint, among other things, of bishops, bee-keepers, and candle-makers — may reflect the specific devotion of the founding parishioners, or simply the availability of the name in the diocesan register.

Ambrose himself is one of the most politically sophisticated of the church fathers, famous for forcing the Emperor Theodosius to do public penance for the Massacre of Thessalonica in 390 — one of the earliest instances of a Christian bishop successfully asserting moral authority over imperial power. That tradition of the church as a check on state power had particular resonance in Catalonia under the Franco regime, when the church was officially allied with the state but local clergy often protected Catalan language and culture in quiet ways.
