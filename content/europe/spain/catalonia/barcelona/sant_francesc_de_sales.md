Francis de Sales, the seventeenth-century Bishop of Geneva, was unlike almost every other Counter-Reformation figure in that his method was persuasion rather than coercion. Writing in elegant French rather than academic Latin, he addressed lay readers — and specifically lay women — as capable of spiritual depth without entering a convent or monastery. His *Introduction to the Devout Life* (1609) was the first work of Christian spirituality written for people living ordinary lives in the world, and it became one of the bestselling books in the history of printing.

The Salesians — formally the Visitandines, founded by Francis with Jeanne de Chantal — established a convent in Barcelona in the nineteenth century, part of the wave of religious congregations that arrived during the Restoration period when the church was recovering from the depredations of liberal disentailment. The church they built in the Eixample reflects the neoclassical taste of the period, ordered and rational rather than dramatically Gothic or exuberantly Baroque.

Francis de Sales became patron of writers and journalists in 1923, when Pius XI noted that his extensive use of printed pamphlets in his missionary work in the Chablais region of Savoy made him a forerunner of the Catholic press. The designation has always had a certain irony, given that de Sales also wrote extensively about the dangers of gossip, idle chatter, and the corruption of language by worldly concerns. Whether the journalists who invoke his patronage have read those warnings is another question.
