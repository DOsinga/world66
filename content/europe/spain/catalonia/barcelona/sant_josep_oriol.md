Josep Oriol died in 1702, and his body was barely cold before the miracles began — or at any rate, before reports of miracles began. The crowds that gathered at his funeral were so dense that city officials feared a public order crisis, and the canonisation process that eventually elevated him to sainthood in 1909 was one of the longest and most contested in modern Catalan church history. The church dedicated to him in the Gothic Quarter is less important as architecture than as a social document: evidence of how intensely popular religion operated in a pre-industrial city.

Oriol was a doctor of theology at the University of Barcelona and a canon of the Cathedral, but his reputation rested not on learning but on his work with the poor. He gave away most of his income, subsisted largely on bread and water, and spent his nights in prayer with an intensity that disturbed his colleagues and attracted the devoted. His particular charism was healing — he is credited with hundreds of cures, many of them involving conditions that his contemporaries understood as supernaturally caused. The distinction between the miraculous and the merely medical was not yet clearly drawn.

The church stands near the Plaça de Sant Josep Oriol, a small square that on weekends fills with art sellers and their watercolour cityscapes. The contrast between the commercial surface and the intense religious history just inside the door is characteristic of the Gothic Quarter, where every third building contains a story that the tourist economy has no particular reason to tell.
