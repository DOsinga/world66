Lazarus — *Llatzer* in Catalan — is the patron saint of lepers, and the hospital that bore his name was established on the western edge of medieval Barcelona to care for those excluded from the city proper. Leprosy carried not just medical but spiritual stigma in the Middle Ages, and lepers were formally expelled from the community through a ceremony that functioned like a living funeral. The hospital of Sant Llatzer was both a medical facility and a liminal space, marking the boundary between the sick and the well.

The chapel that survives is Romanesque in its core, dating to the twelfth century, which makes it one of the oldest intact religious structures in the city. The Romanesque style — massive, round-arched, thick-walled — precedes the Gothic revolution by several generations, and walking into it after the soaring verticals of the Cathedral is a genuine temporal displacement. The proportions are human, the space quiet, the stone worn smooth by centuries of use.

The hospital complex of which the chapel was part has long since been demolished or converted, and the Raval neighbourhood around it has been transformed multiple times: by the medieval convents that occupied the district, by the industrial expansion of the nineteenth century, and by the urban renewal projects of the twentieth. Sant Llatzer survived each of these transformations partly through institutional continuity — it has never been entirely without a congregation — and partly through the sheer difficulty of demolishing Romanesque stonework.
