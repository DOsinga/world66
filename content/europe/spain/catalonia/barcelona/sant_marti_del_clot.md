El Clot — the pit or hollow — takes its name from the topography of the land before urbanisation filled it in: a depression in the plain that collected water and made farming difficult but provided clay for the tile and brick industries that were among the first factories in the area. The church of Sant Martí del Clot was built as the industrial population grew and the old rural parish of Sant Martí de Provençals could no longer accommodate everyone — a simple story of demographic pressure that lies behind dozens of nineteenth-century churches in the expanding suburbs.

The parish of Sant Martí del Clot developed alongside the factories that made this part of Barcelona. The workers who filled its pews during Sunday Mass spent the rest of the week in textile mills, chemical plants, and the railway workshops that were among the largest employers in the city. The relationship between the church and the organised labour movement was complex and often adversarial — the CNT and the anarchist movement were explicitly anticlerical, and church-burning in 1936 would claim several buildings in this area — but the parish persisted, serving those residents who maintained their religious practice through political upheaval.

The church's presence at the centre of the neighbourhood gives El Clot one of the most intact urban forms among Barcelona's former industrial suburbs: a central plaza, a market building, and a church arranged in a pattern that mirrors the structure of the old Catalan village, even though the entire district was built within a century. The effect is an urbanism that feels older than it is, rooted in a tradition of civic space that the industrial age disrupted but did not entirely destroy.
