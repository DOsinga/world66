The neighbourhood that Sant Miquel del Port anchors was itself an act of calculated displacement. After the siege of 1714, Philip V ordered the demolition of the entire Ribera district — one of Barcelona's most densely populated quarters — to build the star-shaped Ciutadella fortress. Some four thousand residents lost their homes. The compensatory solution was Barceloneta: a new grid of narrow streets built on reclaimed sand at the city's waterfront, designed in 1753 by military engineer Juan Martín de Cermeño.

Three churches were planned simultaneously for the new district, of which Sant Miquel del Port was the most prominent. Its construction was completed that same year, making it the first major civic building in the neighbourhood. Inside, the barrel-vaulted nave is decorated with the coats of arms of the naval guilds — the calkers, the pilots, the fishermen — a reminder that this was always a working church for a working waterfront community, not a prestige commission.

The façade, compact and restrained by Baroque standards, reflects the tight geometry of Cermeño's street grid: there was simply no room for theatrical steps or sweeping forecourts. The church sits flush with the surrounding blocks, its modest bell tower the only landmark visible from the Barceloneta beach promenade.
