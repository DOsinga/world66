Peter Nolasco was a remarkable figure even by the standards of medieval religious entrepreneurialism. A prosperous merchant from the Barcelona hinterland, he founded the Order of Our Lady of Mercy in 1218 with an explicit and practical mission: to ransom Christian captives held in Muslim territories, by travelling to those territories, negotiating with their rulers, and paying market rates for human lives. The order became one of the wealthiest in the medieval western Mediterranean, its members travelling regularly to Granada, North Africa, and even Egypt.

The Mercedarians — named for the Spanish *merced*, meaning grace or mercy — developed a theology of substitution that distinguished them from other ransoming organisations: members took a fourth vow, pledging to offer themselves as hostages if the ransom money ran short. The documentary record suggests this vow was actually fulfilled on multiple occasions, making the Mercedarians unique among religious orders in having members who routinely traded their freedom for that of strangers.

The church in the Raval reflects the order's later wealth and confidence: a Baroque interior with the visual programmes of triumph and martyrdom that the seventeenth century favoured. The neighbourhood around it has changed dramatically since the church was built — the Raval is now one of Barcelona's most diverse and densely populated districts, with a large immigrant community from South Asia, the Maghreb, and Latin America — but the church continues to function as a parish, its elaborate Baroque theology unchanged by the world around it.
