Agnes of Rome, martyred around 304 AD, is one of the earliest and most venerated female martyrs in the western church — a teenager who refused to renounce her faith or marry a Roman nobleman, and was executed when she could not be broken. Her iconography typically shows her with a lamb (*agnus* in Latin, giving rise to the pun with her name that medieval theologians adored), and she appears in altarpieces and devotional images across Catholic Europe. A parish named for her in one of Barcelona's quieter residential districts carries the whole weight of that tradition into an entirely domestic setting.

The Sarrià neighbourhood, where the church is located, was an independent municipality until 1921 — later than most of Barcelona's absorbed villages — and it retains a distinctly small-town atmosphere in the upper reaches of the city. The streets narrow, the pace slows, and the mix of modernista villas, convent walls, and quiet plazas gives the impression of a Catalan hill town that happened to be swallowed by a metropolis. The church of Santa Agnès fits that character: modest, functional, serving a stable residential community.

The post-Civil War period in which the church was (re)built was one of intense ecclesiastical activity in Barcelona. The burning of churches in July 1936 had destroyed or damaged dozens of buildings, and the Francoist regime promoted church reconstruction as a symbol of national restoration. The result was a wave of new or rebuilt religious buildings across the city, many of them architecturally unremarkable but sociologically significant as evidence of which communities were considered worthy of investment in the new order.
