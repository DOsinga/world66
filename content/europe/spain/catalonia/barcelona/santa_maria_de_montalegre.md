The name *Montalegre* — Happy Mountain — refers not to any local topography but to a Carmelite monastery in Catalonia's interior that sent its members to found a house in Barcelona in the fourteenth century. The Carmelites chose a site in the Raval, then still largely open land outside the city walls, and built a monastery complex that would dominate this quarter for centuries. What survives today — the Gothic cloister, principally — is the architectural residue of an institution that shaped the neighbourhood's physical form long after the institution itself had changed beyond recognition.

The monastery was among those seized under the disentailment laws of 1835, when the liberal government of Spain dissolved the religious orders and sold their property. In Barcelona, as elsewhere, these seizures were accompanied by violence: the Raval's monasteries were attacked and burned, their contents looted or destroyed, their communities scattered. The Carmelites returned after the political climate shifted, but to a diminished and partially destroyed complex; the church you see from the street is a much-rebuilt structure, while the cloister behind it is genuinely medieval.

The cloister is now integrated into a cultural centre run by the Ajuntament, offering guided visits and occasional exhibitions. It is one of the most significant pieces of Gothic architecture in the Raval and one of the least known, because it lies entirely hidden behind a street facade that gives nothing away. The contrast between the bustling, multicultural Raval outside and the quiet Gothic geometry within is one of the more striking urban experiences the neighbourhood offers.
