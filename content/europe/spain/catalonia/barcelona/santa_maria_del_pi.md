The church of Santa Maria del Pi (Saint Mary of the Pine Tree) anchors three connected squares in the heart of the Gothic Quarter: Plaça del Pi, Plaça de Sant Josep Oriol, and Placeta del Pi. The pine tree of the name has been replaced several times; the current specimen in the square is a descendant of the original that stood here when the church was founded in the 9th century. The tree predates the Gothic building, which was constructed between 1319 and 1391.

The façade is notable for its restraint — a large unadorned stone wall framing a single enormous rose window. When it was installed in the 14th century, the window was the largest Gothic rose window in the world. The pane was destroyed in 1936 during the Spanish Civil War, when the church was attacked and burned by anarchist militants who saw the Catholic Church as an institution of class oppression. The stained glass was replaced with modern designs in the 1990s, and the rose window was rebuilt as a close approximation of the original. Inside, the nave is a single hall without side aisles — the Catalan Gothic manner, which favoured width over height, pragmatism over verticality.

The squares on the south and west sides of the church are among the best-used public spaces in the old city. On weekends, portrait artists and folk musicians occupy the south square; the cafés have tables out in all seasons. The church's position — between La Rambla and the medieval network of the Barri Gòtic — makes it a landmark that locals and tourists arrive at from multiple directions. People sit on the stone steps of the church. The pine tree, whatever its vintage, is in reasonable health.
