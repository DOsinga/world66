The Carmel hill — *Turó del Carmel* — is one of Barcelona's most distinctive topographic features, rising steeply above the Gràcia neighbourhood and offering panoramic views over the city and the sea. The sanctuary dedicated to the Virgin of Carmel occupies its slopes, in a tradition of hilltop Marian devotion that runs throughout Catalonia: the mountain is sacred, the Virgin presides over it, and pilgrims climb to reach her. In Barcelona's case, the 'mountain' is a modest hill of some 267 metres, but the principle holds.

The Carmelite connection runs deep: the order takes its name from Mount Carmel in what is now Israel, where the prophet Elijah is said to have dwelt and where the first Carmelite hermits established themselves in the twelfth century. The Virgin of Carmel is the order's primary devotion, and Carmelite communities spread this cult wherever they settled. The Barcelona sanctuary is part of that worldwide network, connecting a hillside neighbourhood to a mountain in the Levant and to Carmelite houses across the Catholic world.

During the Civil War, the summit of the Carmel hill was occupied by a Republican antiaircraft battery, one of the installations that defended the city against the Italian and German bombing raids that killed hundreds of Barcelona's civilians. The battery's concrete emplacements are still visible near the summit, and the Museu d'Història de Catalunya has worked to document and preserve them. The coexistence of the sanctuary and the antiaircraft battery on the same hill captures something essential about Barcelona's layered history: the sacred and the violent, the ancient and the twentieth-century, occupying the same ground.
