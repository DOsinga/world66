Sarrià was an independent municipality until 1921, making it the last of Barcelona's surrounding villages to be absorbed into the city. It resisted annexation through the same period when Gràcia (1897), Sant Andreu, Sant Martí, and Sants (all 1897) were being absorbed. By 1921, Sarrià was surrounded on all sides by Barcelona and had no practical alternative to joining, but the merger was contested and bitterly felt.

The Carrer Major de Sarrià preserves the character of an old Catalan village high street: narrow, lined with two and three storey buildings of mixed age, with the kind of neighbourhood commerce — a pharmacy, a bar-grocery, a hardware shop — that has been compressed out of the main city. The Plaça de Sarrià at the top of the street, with the church of Sant Vicenç, was the village's municipal centre. It still functions as a neighbourhood square rather than a tourist destination.

Sarrià's annexation left a long memory. The neighbourhood maintained and maintained its local festivals, its social associations, and its civic identity in ways that the earlier-annexed villages had not. The Festa Major de Sarrià, celebrated in September, is organised by associations that trace their history to the independent municipality. The Carrer Major is not a heritage street but a living one: the distinction between a Barcelona street and a village high street is in the scale and the tempo rather than in any preserved architecture.
