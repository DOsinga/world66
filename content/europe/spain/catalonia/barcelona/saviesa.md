Plaça de Catalunya is sometimes described as an open-air museum: 28 sculptures were installed when the square was completed in 1929, including two programmatic pairs conceived as pillars of Catalan civic identity. Saviesa (Wisdom) was placed beside Treball (Labour) on the seaward side of the square near the Ramblas, embodying the idea that physical work and intellectual guidance must go together. The sculptor Miquel Oslé i Sáenz de Medrano worked in such close collaboration with his brother Llucià that their output is treated as effectively joint; Llucià made the companion Treball while Miquel took Saviesa.

The composition depicts an allegorical female figure — Minerva — in a tunic with an aegis on her chest, holding a helmet in one hand while the other controls a rearing horse whose hooves crush a serpent. The symbolism is direct: reason mastering brute force, knowledge defeating ignorance. The selection came from an open competition in 1927 that attracted 90 entries, making the Oslé brothers' shared vision the judges' decisive choice. At 3.20 metres, Saviesa is among the larger pieces in the square.

The word itself derives from Latin sapientia, filtered through Old Catalan — the same root as "sapient" in English. Walking the sea-facing edge of Plaça de Catalunya and finding these two bronzes side by side, each three metres tall, each embodying an abstract value the city wanted to claim as its own, is to encounter 1929 Barcelona's self-image at its most confident.
