The Jardins de Joan Maragall wrap around the Palauet Albéniz, Barcelona's official reception pavilion for visiting heads of state, and are closed to the public on weekdays — which means Serena is seen by far fewer people than most civic sculptures despite being on Montjuïc, one of the city's busiest hills. The gardens open on weekends and holidays only, and the work stands in a peristyle courtyard framed by Ionic columns and enclosed by cypress trees: a deliberately classical setting for a quietly classical figure.

The sculptor, Pilar Francesch i Ventalló (born 1941), trained at the Escola Massana in Barcelona and continued her studies in Florence. She later returned to teach at Massana, and among her other commissions was a portrait bust of King Juan Carlos I for the Barcelona city council — a range that reflects her facility across both the civic and private portrait traditions. A replica of Serena stands at the Escola Massana entrance. The title derives from Latin serenus, meaning calm, clear, and bright — the cypress enclosure and Ionic columns amplify exactly the mood the word names.

The combination of restricted access and garden setting makes Serena one of those Barcelona artworks that rewards the visitor who makes the specific effort. Arriving at the right time — a weekend morning before the tourists reach Montjuïc's main attractions — the garden can feel genuinely private, and the figure within it earns its name.
