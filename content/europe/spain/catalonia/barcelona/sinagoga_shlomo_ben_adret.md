Shlomo ibn Adret — the Rashba (1235–1310) — was the foremost rabbinic authority in the Jewish world during the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. As rabbi of Barcelona for roughly 50 years, he wrote approximately 3,000 responsa: legal rulings on questions sent from Jewish communities across Europe and Asia Minor, which fill thousands of pages still studied today. From this building, in 1305, he issued one of the most controversial decrees in medieval Judaism: a ban excommunicating any Jewish man under 30 who studied physics or metaphysics — physicians-in-training specifically exempted. The ruling convulsed Sephardic intellectual life for decades.

The synagogue's rediscovery is a story about paperwork as much as archaeology. In 1987, historian Jaume Riera i Sans reconstructed the exact route of a thirteenth-century royal tax collector through the Call — Barcelona's Jewish quarter — by cross-referencing records with Talmudic specifications for synagogue construction, and identified the building. Structural proof came from its orientation: the building's east-facing alignment breaks with the northwest-southeast street grid of the surrounding Gothic Quarter. It was deliberately rotated to face Jerusalem.

The building has Roman-era foundations dated to the third and fourth centuries AD, and may have served as a place of Jewish worship since late antiquity — which would make it one of the oldest synagogue sites in Europe. After the 1391 pogrom and the 1492 expulsion, it passed through uses as a hospital, warehouse, and private dwelling before the Call fell silent. Reopened as a synagogue and museum in 2002, it is both an active place of worship and a link to a community that shaped medieval Barcelona's intellectual and commercial life.
