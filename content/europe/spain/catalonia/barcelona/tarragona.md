Jaume Otero i Camps (Maó, Menorca 1888 – Barcelona 1945) worked in a classicising style described by contemporaries as approaching Greek perfection. His commission for the 1929 International Exposition called for four allegorical sculptures representing the provinces of Catalonia: Barcelona, Girona, Lleida, and Tarragona. The Tarragona figure represents a seated male flanked by female maidens, a fish, and fishing nets — evoking the province's Roman heritage and maritime character.

The version in Plaça de Catalunya is the second draft. Otero's original stone version — which still stands on Avinguda Diagonal — reportedly featured a fully nude female figure that had to be covered with a cloth at the pubis after complaints, and a naked angel riding a dolphin that was eliminated entirely. The bronze in the square is the morally approved replacement. Barcelona in 1929 was navigating the tensions between classical artistic convention and the conservative social expectations of Primo de Rivera's dictatorship, and this sculpture carries that negotiation in its DNA.

The name Tarragona derives from ancient Tarraco, once the capital of Hispania Citerior and one of the most important Roman cities in the peninsula. The word is pre-Roman, possibly Iberian in origin — so even the city's name carries a layered history that the allegory in bronze is trying to compress.
