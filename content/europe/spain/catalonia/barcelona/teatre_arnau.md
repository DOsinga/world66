The Avinguda del Paral·lel was Barcelona's entertainment district from the 1890s onward — the working-class answer to the bourgeois opera houses of the Eixample. The Arnau, which opened in 1903, was its most enduring venue: a theatre for revue, zarzuela, and variety shows aimed at the factory workers, port labourers, seamstresses, and political activists who lived in the Raval and Poble Sec. The tickets were cheap, the acts were loud, and the politics of the audience were rarely conservative. Carmen Amaya danced here. Raquel Meller, the Aragonese singer who became one of the most famous performers in 1920s Europe, built part of her reputation on this stage.

The Arnau survived the Civil War, the Franco years (with heavy censorship), and the transition to democracy. It adapted through different formats — variety theatre, music hall, briefly a cinema — but remained a working venue until 2007, when it closed for a restoration that has not happened. The building has been derelict since, the subject of repeated municipal plans and announcements that have produced no result. The facade on the Paral·lel remains, the name in faded lettering above the entrance, alongside a neighbourhood that has been gentrifying around it without absorbing it.

The Arnau's dereliction has become something of a symbol for Barcelona's unresolved relationship with its working-class cultural heritage: preserved enough not to demolish, neglected enough not to use, debated enough to generate political capital without generating construction.
