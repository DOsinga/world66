The founding collective — Fabià Puigserver, Carlota Soldevila, and others — had spent the dictatorship years working in exile or underground. When the Lliure opened its first season in the Mercat de les Flors building in Gràcia, it was a public statement that Catalan-language theatre could be serious, ambitious, and European without needing the state's endorsement. The name "Lliure" — free — carried every possible meaning at once.

In 2001 the company moved to a purpose-built venue inside the Pavelló de l'Agricultura on Montjuïc, a structure originally built for the 1929 International Exhibition and converted to house two separate stages. The original Gràcia building became the Mercat de les Flors, Barcelona's main contemporary dance venue — the two institutions share a history and a neighbourhood of buildings without competing.

Today the Lliure programmes across drama, opera, and physical theatre, maintaining the European repertory ambition that defined its founding. Its Montjuïc campus — two auditoriums in a converted exhibition pavilion — still looks unlikely from the outside, which is part of its appeal.
