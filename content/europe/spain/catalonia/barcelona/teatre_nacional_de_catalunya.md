The provocative choice was the point. Ricardo Bofill designed the Teatre Nacional de Catalunya as a neoclassical stone-and-glass temple — colonnades, a pediment, steps rising to a monumental entrance — at a moment when Catalan cultural identity was inseparable from modernisme and the avant-garde. Neoclassicism in Catalonia had long been associated with the Spanish centralising state; Bofill, a Catalan architect who had built postmodern social housing estates in France using the same classical vocabulary, was forcing a reclamation. The building caused argument before its foundation was poured.

The theatre opened in 1997, two years before the adjacent Auditori concert hall completed the cultural complex. Its brief is specifically Catalan: all productions are in Catalan or in translation into Catalan, and the programming covers Catalan-authored drama as well as international work staged in a language the institution is legally committed to defending. The main hall seats 900; two smaller spaces handle experimental and studio work.

It sits at the edge of the 22@ innovation district, which has transformed what was once Poblenou's derelict industrial fabric into offices and universities. The temple sits oddly amid this, which was probably also the intention — a monumental civic anchor in a district that had none.
