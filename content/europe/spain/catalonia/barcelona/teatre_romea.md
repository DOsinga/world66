The Teatre Romea opened in 1863 on Carrer de l'Hospital, in the middle of the working-class Raval, and it has been staging plays almost continuously since. The façade is restrained neoclassical, easily missed between the taller buildings on either side, but the auditorium inside retains much of its horseshoe galleried form from the original construction. The theatre was renovated in 1997 by Helio Piñón and Albert Viaplana, who preserved the shell while modernising the stage machinery.

Its real distinction is political. Under the Franco dictatorship, when Catalan was banned from public life, the Romea was one of the few venues permitted to stage plays in the language under the cover of being a "regional curiosity." Productions here were monitored by censors but continued throughout the 1950s and 1960s, making the stage a crucial node of cultural resistance. Playwrights like Salvador Espriu and directors working under pseudonyms kept the Catalan dramatic tradition alive here when it had nowhere else to go.

The theatre's name honours the nineteenth-century Catalan actor Julià Romea, whose portrait hangs in the foyer. The Raval setting is unchanged: the same streets of the former red-light district surround it, and the door opens directly onto the pavement with no plaza buffer.
