Push open the door at number 10 Carrer del Paradís and you step into the courtyard of the Casa de l'Ardèbol, a medieval building like any other on the outside. Then look up. Four slender Corinthian columns rise eleven metres from a limestone podium, their fluted shafts intact, their carved capitals still legible after two thousand years. These are the columns of the Temple d'August — the main religious building of the Roman colony of Barcino, built in the 1st century BCE and dedicated to the emperor who gave it his name.

The columns were not always visible. For centuries they were incorporated into the structure of the medieval building and then into a palace that later occupied the site. They were rediscovered in the late 19th century and have been preserved in place ever since. The podium they stand on marks the highest point of the Roman city — this hill, known as Mont Tàber, was why the Romans chose this location. From here, the city sloped down to the walls and the gates, and the temple could be seen from the sea.

The four surviving columns are not the whole temple. The building originally had six columns across the façade and perhaps ten along the sides, on a podium roughly fourteen metres wide. What you see is the back corner of a structure that would have dominated the forum of Barcino, which lay somewhere under the present-day streets to the north and west. The entrance is now a museum administered by the city; the ground floor is free to enter during opening hours. Stand among the columns and try to reconstruct the orientation: the forum stretched towards where the Cathedral stands now, on ground that was already sacred and already civic before Christianity arrived.
