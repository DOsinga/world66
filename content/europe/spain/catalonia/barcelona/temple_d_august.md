The Roman colony of Barcino — founded around 10 BC — was a small, orderly provincial city, its forum sitting at the intersection of the cardo and decumanus, the two main axes that Roman city planners laid down wherever they built. The Temple of Augustus occupied one corner of that forum, and when the colony eventually dissolved into the medieval city, the temple was absorbed into successive buildings rather than systematically demolished. The result is one of Barcelona's most startling discoveries: stepping through an unremarkable door in the Gothic Quarter and into a medieval courtyard, you find four columns rising to their full height, untouched, in an interior space.

The columns belong to the temple's peristyle — the colonnaded outer walkway — and their survival owes much to the fact that later builders found it easier to build around them than to demolish them. For centuries they stood inside a private building, visible to few. The medieval Palau dels Lloctinents eventually came to enclose the space, and in the nineteenth century scholars began investigating what exactly these columns were doing inside a private courtyard.

The temple is now managed by the Museu d'Història de Barcelona (MUHBA) and is accessible free of charge. Seeing these columns in situ — not relocated to a museum, not reconstructed, but standing precisely where Roman masons placed them two thousand years ago — is a different order of experience from anything a display case can offer.
