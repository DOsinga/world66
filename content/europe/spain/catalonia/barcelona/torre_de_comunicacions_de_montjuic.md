When Telefónica commissioned a telecommunications tower for the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, Santiago Calatrava produced a structure with a second function hidden in plain sight. The shaft leans at precisely 17 degrees, calibrated to the angle of the summer solstice sun in Barcelona's latitude; the needle casts a shadow that reads as a working sundial on the Plaça d'Europa beside it. On the summer solstice, the shadow falls due north at solar noon. Most visitors register only the sweeping white form — meant to evoke an athlete raising the Olympic flame — and miss the astronomical precision.

The tower stands 136 metres tall and was designed for Telefónica to broadcast television coverage of the Games. It was classified as a Cultural Asset of the Cultural Heritage of Catalonia within a generation of its construction — unusually fast for a structure so recent, reflecting how thoroughly it became identified with the transformed Barcelona that the Olympics produced. The base is clad in trencadís, broken-tile mosaic in pure white: Calatrava connecting to the Catalan tile tradition while distinguishing himself from Gaudí's polychrome palette.

The hill's name, Montjuïc, derives from Mons Iudaicus — Hill of the Jews — reflecting a medieval Jewish cemetery that once occupied its slopes. Standing at the tower's base, with that etymology in mind and Calatrava's solar geometry above, the hilltop gathers several centuries of Barcelona's history into one viewpoint.
