Gas lighting arrived in Barcelona in 1843, and the Catalana de Gas factory supplied the city's streets and homes for over a century before closing in 1971. The 45-metre water tower, designed by architect Josep Domènech i Estapà in 1905–06, served the factory's cooling and fire-safety systems and was given considerably more architectural elaboration than industrial function required: a rectangular base rising to an octagonal mid-section, topped by a circular reservoir, the exterior decorated with exposed brick and blue-and-white geometric ceramic tilework. Inside, an imperial staircase transitions to a cast-iron spiral stair — the kind of interior grandeur that says the building was meant to be seen as an institution, not merely a machine.

The factory was demolished in 1989 during Olympic preparation. The tower, a gasometer frame, and the former offices were deliberately retained, isolated from the complex that once surrounded them. In that isolation the tower became the carrier of a local story: an engineer named Pau spent two years designing the factory's pumping mechanism, was publicly disgraced at the inauguration when it failed, and threw himself from the top. Two weeks later it was discovered that the British-manufactured valves operated in the opposite rotational direction from their European equivalents — a simple adjustment would have saved him. Local legend holds that English visitors to the tower cannot perceive the ceramic colours, seeing only grey, haunted by the British valve-maker's role in Pau's death.

Domènech i Estapà moved between eclecticism and Modernisme throughout his career, and the tower sits comfortably in neither camp — it is pragmatic and decorative at once, a working object that wanted to be taken seriously.
