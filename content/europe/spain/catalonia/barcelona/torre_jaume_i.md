The cable car was an engineering demonstration as much as a passenger service. Built for the 1929 International Exhibition, the line ran from the Barceloneta beach tower across the full width of the working commercial port to the Montjuïc hillside — a route that traversed active dockland that visitors could not otherwise enter. The towers had to be tall enough to keep the cables clear of shipping, which is why Torre Jaume I stands 107 metres above the waterline.

The line stopped operating after the Civil War and did not reopen until 1967. By then the port had changed and the original industrial rationale was gone, but the engineering remained sound and the view from the gondolas — looking down into the port basin and out across the Mediterranean — was not obtainable any other way. It has operated, with interruptions for maintenance and refurbishment, ever since.

The tower is named after King James I of Aragon, the thirteenth-century monarch who took Valencia, Mallorca, and Ibiza and built the Catalan-Aragonese commercial network across the western Mediterranean. The naming was not accidental: the 1929 exhibition was partly a celebration of that maritime heritage, and siting the port tower at the foot of the city James I had made into a trading capital was a piece of deliberate historical choreography.
