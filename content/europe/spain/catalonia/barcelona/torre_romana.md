Around 250 AD, raiders identified in Roman sources as Franks and Alemanni swept through Hispania with unusual destruction, reaching as far as Tarraconensis. Barcino's response was to build — fast. The second circuit of walls, constructed over the late third and into the fourth century, was formidable: up to 8 metres thick in places, with 81 towers reaching 18 metres high, enclosing the colony behind one of the most substantial late-Roman urban fortifications in the peninsula. The speed and scale of the investment reflects the anxiety of the times.

Medieval Barcelona incorporated these walls rather than demolishing them. Successive centuries of construction built against and over the Roman fabric, using the towers as foundations and the wall sections as party walls. The fourteenth-century Gothic chapel of Santa Àgata stands with its foundations literally resting on the Roman wall at Plaça Ramon Berenguer. At Plaça Nova, two original Roman gate towers still flank what was the Porta Praetoria — now partly at basement level because Barcelona's street surface has risen several metres since Roman times.

The surviving tower visible here marks the line of the ancient perimeter. At Plaça dels Traginers, Carrer de la Tapineria, and Carrer del Correu Vell, where the wall surfaces are exposed, you can trace the full scale of the second-century investment. What now looks like background material — stone embedded in later construction — was once the hard outer edge of everything.
