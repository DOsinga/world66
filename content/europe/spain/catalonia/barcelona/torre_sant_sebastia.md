Stand at the base and look through the ground-floor windows: the machinery is still there. The 1929 winding and tensioning equipment that drives the cable across the 1,300-metre harbour span has never been fully modernised out of sight, and the industrial apparatus of the original installation remains legible at street level in a way that the more visited Torre Jaume I does not offer. It is one of the few places in Barcelona where a working piece of the 1929 exhibition infrastructure is still operational and visible.

The Barceloneta tower is the lower of the two main towers, sitting on the edge of the beach neighbourhood that was built in the eighteenth century to house the workers displaced when the Ciutadella citadel was constructed. From the tower the cable car rises steeply toward Torre Jaume I and then continues to the Montjuïc station — the full crossing takes about eleven minutes and travels 75 metres above the water at the midpoint, over the working port basin.

The ride is one of the few ways to read Barcelona's topography in a single glance: the grid of the Eixample laid against the irregular old city, the hard edge of the port, and the Montjuïc hill rising behind. Sailors navigating into Barcelona for centuries used Montjuïc as their landmark; the cable car crosses the exact line of sight they would have followed.
