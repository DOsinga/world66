At the entrance to Plaça d'Espanya, two tall towers rise identically on either side of the avenue leading up toward Montjuïc. They are red brick with white stone dressings, square-plan below and octagonal above, each terminating in a cylindrical spire. They are direct copies — reduced in scale but faithful in form — of the campanile di San Marco in Venice. These are the Torres Venecianes (Venetian Towers), designed by Puig i Cadafalch for the 1929 International Exposition and completed in 1927.

The choice of a Venetian campanile as the model for the entrance to a Spanish world's fair says something specific about how Catalonia understood its own cultural identity in the 1920s. Venice was the capital of a maritime empire — the Serenissima — that had been the commercial partner and rival of the Crown of Aragon throughout the medieval Mediterranean. Barcelona's merchants had known the Venetians well; the lagoon city and the Catalan-Aragonese empire had competed across the same trading routes for centuries. Replicating the campanile was not a copy but an assertion of equivalence.

Puig i Cadafalch, who designed them, had been president of the Commonwealth of Catalonia (Mancomunitat de Catalunya) until the Primo de Rivera dictatorship abolished it in 1925. Two years later, he was building the entrance towers for a Spanish national exposition at the order of the same regime. The towers were completed and remain one of the most visible landmarks at the base of Montjuïc. Look back at them from the avenue above, with the mountain behind you: they frame the city below with the precise intended monumentality.
