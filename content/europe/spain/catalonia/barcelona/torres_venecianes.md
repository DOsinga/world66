The towers are not trying to be subtle. Ramon Reventós copied the campanile of San Marco explicitly, at roughly half scale, and built two of them to flank the Plaça d'Espanya entrance to the 1929 International Exhibition — an act of architectural quotation so bold it amounts to a statement. The statement was consistent with the exhibition's declared theme: the historic ties between Mediterranean nations. Barcelona was claiming membership in the civilisational tradition that ran from Venice through Genoa to Catalonia's own medieval maritime empire.

The 1929 exhibition remade the entire Montjuïc hillside and the approaches to it. The avenue connecting Plaça d'Espanya to the Palau Nacional was designed for theatrical effect: visitors arriving by tram or on foot would pass between the towers, then proceed up a sequence of fountains and terraces to the palace at the summit. The towers marked the threshold between the everyday city and the exhibition's constructed spectacle.

They remain in place because nothing has displaced them. The avenue they frame — now Avinguda de la Reina Maria Cristina — still hosts the Font Màgica light show in the evenings, so the towers continue to serve as the ceremonial entrance they were built to be. Their historicism is no longer ironic; it has simply become Barcelona's own history.
