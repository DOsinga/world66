In 1987, Barcelona's mayor Pasqual Maragall commissioned two internationally celebrated sculptors to place works in the Parc de la Creueta del Coll, a former industrial quarry in the working-class Gràcia neighbourhood that had been converted into a public park. The pairing was deliberate: Eduardo Chillida's Elogi de l'Aigua — a massive concrete form suspended by cables over a swimming pool — and Ellsworth Kelly's almost weightless vertical blade represent completely opposed post-war sculptural philosophies compressed into a single quarry park.

Kelly (1923–2015) was primarily known as a painter — his shaped canvases and flat colour fields made him one of the defining figures of American post-war abstraction. His public sculpture was relatively rare, and this commission, arriving when he was in his mid-sixties, was exceptional: a permanent outdoor work in a European city at a scale that demanded rethinking his usual vocabulary. The blade of weathering steel, measuring roughly 9.9 metres tall, turns Totem into a vertical painting in space, its surface oxidising to a rust tone that shifts with light and weather.

The word "totem" derives from the Ojibwe ototeman, meaning one's kinship group, and Kelly used it for its connotation of upright, singular, authoritative presence — a marker that says this place matters. In a park built on a quarry in a neighbourhood that was not on the international art circuit before 1987, the act of planting that marker was its own statement about what kinds of places deserve to be taken seriously.
