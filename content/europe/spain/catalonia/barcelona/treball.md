Treball means "work" in Catalan, but the word's etymology cuts against its honorific use: it derives from Vulgar Latin tripaliare, meaning to torture, itself from tripalium — a three-staked instrument of punishment. In medieval Latin, "to labour" and "to be tormented" were the same word, a reminder that physical work was historically understood as toil inflicted on the powerless rather than something to celebrate. The sculptor Llucià Oslé i Sáenz de Medrano placed a laurel crown on his nude worker's head: the crown of emperors and generals given to a man with a hammer. The base inscription reads "Trabajo" — the Spanish, not Catalan, form — reflecting the bilingual political landscape of 1929 Barcelona under Primo de Rivera's dictatorship.

Treball stands beside its programmatic twin, Saviesa (Wisdom), on the sea-facing side of Plaça de Catalunya. The two were conceived as a pair — labour and intellect as the twin foundations of Catalan civic identity — and were chosen together from a competition that attracted 90 entries. Llucià Oslé made Treball; his brother Miquel made Saviesa; the brothers worked in such close collaboration that art historians treat their output as effectively joint. At 3.45 metres by 1.25 by 3.70, Treball is the larger of the two.

The rearing horse in the composition is the element that most repays attention: it appears in both sculptures, differently controlled. In Saviesa, the horse is mastered by intellect; in Treball, it accompanies labour as raw power. The square's full sculptural programme of 28 works makes sense only when you see these two as its conceptual anchor.
