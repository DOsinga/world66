Nou Barris (Nine Neighbourhoods) is the northernmost district of Barcelona, composed of nine distinct barrios that were developed informally or through mass social housing construction in the 1950s and 1960s. The district's identity was defined by its distance from the city centre, the low income of its residents, and the inadequacy of its public infrastructure: few parks, narrow streets, no wide pedestrian spaces.

The Via Júlia was redesigned in 1987 as a model of what urban public space in a working-class neighbourhood could be. The street was widened, a central promenade was planted with trees and furnished with benches, and artworks were commissioned for the public space. The project was part of the broader programme of urban improvement that preceded the 1992 Olympics — a programme that was explicitly meant to benefit peripheral neighbourhoods rather than concentrating investment in the tourist zones.

The Via Júlia set a precedent for subsequent pedestrianisation projects across the city. It demonstrated that working-class neighbourhoods could support high-quality public space — that the assumption that nice public spaces were for nice neighbourhoods was wrong. The Olympic programme produced several of these peripheral investments alongside the more celebrated waterfront and Montjuïc improvements. The Via Júlia, visited by almost no tourists, represents the aspect of the 1992 transformation that was actually about the residents of Barcelona rather than about its visitors.
