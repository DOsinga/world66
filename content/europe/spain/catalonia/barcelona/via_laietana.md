Stand at any intersection of Via Laietana and look at the buildings on either side: they are abruptly modern, facing each other across a boulevard that was never meant to be here. This is because it wasn't. Via Laietana was cut through the medieval fabric of Barcelona beginning in 1907, following a plan by the city engineer Àngel Baixeras that had been debated since the 1880s. When the demolitions were complete, 2,153 buildings had been destroyed and more than nine thousand people had been forced to leave one of the most densely inhabited neighbourhoods in Barcelona.

The rationale was modernization. The dense warren of medieval streets between the Barri Gòtic and the Born district was considered a health hazard — dark, airless, overcrowded — and cutting a wide boulevard through it would allow traffic to flow from the port to the Eixample. The model was Haussmann's boulevards in Paris, which had produced a similar pattern of demolition and displacement two generations earlier. Like Haussmann's work, Via Laietana solved certain engineering problems while also conveniently scattering a politically active working-class population.

The neighbourhood that was demolished was the medieval core of the La Ribera district, one of the areas that had grown up outside the original Roman walls to serve the expanding commercial port in the 12th and 13th centuries. Some of what was lost can still be inferred from the street pattern: on the east side of Via Laietana, the medieval streets of the Born survive intact; on the west side, the Gothic Quarter survives. What was between them — the dense connective tissue of medieval Barcelona — is now a road with tram tracks that runs from Plaça d'Antoni López to Plaça de Urquinaona.
