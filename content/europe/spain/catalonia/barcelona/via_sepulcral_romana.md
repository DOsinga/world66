Roman law prohibited burial within city walls, so cemeteries lined the roads leading out of town. Barcino's western gate opened onto what is now the Plaça de la Vila de Madrid, and the road leading toward Emerita Augusta (modern Mérida) was flanked by tombs in the standard Roman fashion — built to be seen by travellers, combining commemoration with an advertisement of the family's status. The cemetery served middle and lower-class residents, enslaved people, and freed persons: a cross-section of Barcino's full social range, not its elite.

The site lay undisturbed for a specific reason: alluvial deposits buried it after the fourth century, making the ground too waterlogged for medieval builders to plunder for stone. The tombs were found exactly where placed, in 1954–56 construction works, with follow-up excavations in 1959 and 2000–2003. Rather than removing the finds to a museum, the city displayed them in situ in an open trench, preserving the spatial relationships between 85 burials, their stone cupae (barrel-shaped covers), altars, stelae, and mounds. Among the discoveries: an infant buried in a third-century amphora manufactured in what is now Tunisia, interred with a bronze bracelet and gaming pieces. The site also contains a Neolithic circular burial structure predating Barcino by thousands of years.

The MUHBA site, opened in its present form in 2008, is one of the few places in Barcelona where you can stand at street level, look down into an open trench, and see the ancient city not reconstructed or represented but simply still there — the dead lying where they were laid.
