  The Barandales figure — a hooded penitent carrying a candle, cast in bronze and placed outside the museum — has become the most photographed image in Zamora. It was created in 1999 by sculptor Ramón Chaparro and represents the anonymous, silent nature of the Zamoran Semana Santa processions, which are notably more austere and solemn than the famous parades of Seville or Valladolid. In Zamora, the brothers process in near-total silence, with only the beat of drums and the shuffle of sandals on cobblestones.

Zamora's Holy Week processions are considered among the finest in Spain — more austere and dramatic than the more famous parades of Seville or Málaga. The Museo de Semana Santa, housed in a modern building adjacent to the Iglesia de San Juan, displays the elaborate carved wooden floats (pasos) and the gold and silver vestments used in the processions throughout the year. These objects are extraordinary — the craftsmanship is of a very high order and the scale of some of the pasos is remarkable.

The museum gives excellent context for understanding what Semana Santa means to the city, which comes to a complete stop in Holy Week. The processions take place at night by candlelight, with the brotherhoods (cofradías) in their distinctive pointed hoods — an image that outsiders sometimes find startling but which has a deep local religious significance. If you can visit in Holy Week, the experience is unforgettable; the museum does a good job of conveying it out of season.
