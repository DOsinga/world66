**O Ribeiro** is one of Galicia's oldest and most distinctive wine regions, a comarca of ten municipalities spread across terraced hillsides in the province of Ourense where the Miño, Avia, and Arnoia rivers meet. It is not a place you simply pass through — this is a region worth seeking out, both for its wines and for its history.

The capital, Ribadavia, is the kind of small town that surprises. Inside a compact medieval centre you find a ruined fifteenth-century castle, one of the best-preserved Jewish quarters in Galicia, and a Praza Maior surrounded by old stone arcades. Wine bars fill the narrow streets below, and the local Ribeiro whites — crisp, aromatic, and low in alcohol — are some of the most food-friendly wines made anywhere in Spain.

The vineyards here have been producing wine since at least Roman times. The Cluny monks who accompanied Raymond of Burgundy in the twelfth century formalised viticulture across the comarca, and by 1579 the region had drafted its own quality ordinances — an early precursor to the modern DO system. Ribeiro received official Denominación de Origen status in 1932, among the earliest in Spain.

The landscape is as appealing as the wine. Steep granite slopes drop to river valleys, small villages cluster around Romanesque churches, and the roads wind past hundreds of small family plots. The comarca remains agricultural in the truest sense: wine is not a lifestyle product here but part of how people live.
