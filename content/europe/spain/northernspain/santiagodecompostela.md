Santiago de Compostela is one of the great pilgrimage cities of the world. For over a thousand years, Christians have walked the Camino de Santiago — the Way of Saint James — from all over Europe to reach the cathedral that stands at the city's heart and allegedly contains the tomb of the apostle James. Today the pilgrims still come, and the city that has grown up to receive them is one of the finest old towns in Spain, built from the golden-grey granite of Galicia and draped in the Atlantic rain that keeps everything green.

The Cathedral of Santiago de Compostela dominates the Praza do Obradoiro, one of Spain's great squares. Its elaborate Baroque facade (the towers are 18th-century additions to a Romanesque core) is both the endpoint of the Camino and the architectural centrepiece of the city. Around it lies a compact old town of cobbled streets, arcaded walkways, churches, palaces, and the kind of cheap, good restaurants that a large university population and a steady stream of arriving pilgrims tend to generate. The Rúa do Franco and Rúa de Vilar are the main eating and drinking streets.

Santiago is a university city as much as a pilgrimage one, and the student population keeps the nightlife and cultural life alive year-round. The city is the capital of Galicia, Spain's Celtic northwest, and that cultural identity — distinct from Castilian Spain — is palpable in the language (Galego, closely related to Portuguese), the music (bagpipes, called gaitas, are the Galician instrument), and the excellent seafood that dominates restaurant menus. The Atlantic coast is close, and the kitchen benefits accordingly.
