Stora Sjöfallet (Stuor Muorkke in Sami) is one of Sweden's oldest and largest national parks, covering some 1,278 square kilometres of mountain wilderness in Swedish Lapland, about 200 kilometres north of the Arctic Circle. The nearest town is Gällivare, about 200 kilometres to the east. This is remote country — one of the last truly wild areas in Europe, comparable in character to interior Alaska without the remoteness of Siberia.

The park's landscape is defined by high mountain plateaus, glacial valleys, and the lakes and rivers that drain west toward Norway. The Ádájåhkå and Lulealven river systems run through it. The original "great waterfall" that gave the park its name was significantly reduced when a hydroelectric dam was built in the 1920s, flooding parts of the valley — a loss that still stirs feeling among conservationists and the Sami people whose traditional lands were affected.

The main activities are hiking and fishing in summer, and ski touring in winter. The terrain varies from deep valleys to high tundra plateaus, with routes ranging from day hikes to multi-day traverses. In late May, June, and July, when the snow has settled into stable spring conditions and the days last around the clock, the high slopes offer exceptional backcountry skiing — steep descents of 500 to 1,000 metres in continuous daylight. The park is entirely above the tree line in its upper sections, with views that extend for tens of kilometres on clear days.

The Sami have herded reindeer across this land for centuries and continue to do so. Snowmobiles are prohibited except for reindeer herding. Hikers should treat the landscape accordingly.
