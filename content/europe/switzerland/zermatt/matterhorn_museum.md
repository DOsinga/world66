  The first ascent of the Matterhorn on 14 July 1865 ended in catastrophe.
  Edward Whymper's party of seven reached the summit, but on the descent four
  men fell 1,200 metres to their deaths. The rope between them — on display in
  the museum — had snapped, or been cut, or simply given way; accounts conflict.
  The accident made Whymper famous and the Matterhorn notorious, and began the
  modern era of controversy about the ethics of mountain guiding. Whymper spent
  the rest of his life defending himself against accusations that were never
  quite resolved.

The Matterhorn Museum sits entirely underground beneath the village centre, entered through a glass pavilion beside the church. The museum traces the history of Zermatt from a farming village to alpine resort, and centres on the first ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865 and its disastrous aftermath. The collection includes the original rope that broke on the descent, the equipment of the climbers who died, and the reconstituted interiors of Victorian-era Zermatt buildings.

The exhibition is thoughtfully done — serious about the mountaineering history without being dry, and honest about the tragedy and controversy that followed. There is a separate gallery on the geology and natural history of the Pennine Alps. Worth an hour; very good if it's raining and the mountains are invisible, which happens.
