Bahchisaray — whose name means "palace of gardens" in Tatar — was the capital of the Crimean Khanate from the 15th to the 18th century. When Russia absorbed Crimea, it became a provincial backwater, but it never lost the character that centuries of Tatar rule had given it. Today it is a city of around 30,000 people, sitting at the heart of a wide agricultural valley.

The [old quarter](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/old_quarter) is the reason to come. Its narrow, winding streets still feel medieval, lined with low stone houses, ancient mosques, and craft workshops. The undisputed centrepiece is the [Khan's Palace (Khansaray)](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/khans_palace), the only surviving palace of the Crimean khans — a remarkable complex of courtyards, fountains, and ornate audience halls that repays a long, slow visit. Pushkin's 1823 poem about the palace's Fountain of Tears made Bahchisaray famous across the Russian Empire.

A walk of about 3 km east along the Mariam-Dere gorge leads to two of the most spectacular sites in Crimea: the cliff-cut [Dormition Cave Monastery](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/dormition_monastery) and, above it on the plateau, the abandoned cave city of [Chufut-Kale](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/chufut_kale) — once the stronghold of Crimean Karaim Jews. Together they make a full half-day's excursion from the centre.

The town also holds a handful of surviving Crimean Tatar mosques — the [Big Khan Mosque](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/big_khan_mosque) in the palace complex, the neighbourhood [Orta Cami](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/orta_cami_mosque) and [Tahtalı-Jami](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/tahtali_jami_mosque) mosques in the old quarter, and the early 1500s [Zincirli Madrasa](/europe/ukraine/crimea/bahchisaray/zincirli_medrese) on the eastern edge of town. The Crimean Tatar food, particularly chebureki and lagman, is reason enough to linger an extra day.
