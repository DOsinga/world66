Literature on Donetsk specifically is sparse in English — the city's significance was underestimated until 2014. But the wider Donbas and eastern Ukraine have generated some important writing.

**The Orphanage** by Serhiy Zhadan (2017) — Ukraine's best-known living writer comes from the Donbas and his fiction is saturated with it. *The Orphanage* is set during the 2014–2015 conflict and follows a man trying to reach his nephew through a warzone that resembles the landscapes of his childhood. Painful, precise, and necessary. Zhadan writes about the industrial east with the authority of someone who grew up there.

**Mesopotamia** by Serhiy Zhadan (2014) — A looser, earlier novel set in the cities and towns of eastern Ukraine, full of the bars, markets, petty criminals, and ordinary people of a region that was neither Russia nor quite Western Ukraine. Gives a better sense of what pre-war Donbas felt like than any travel account.

**The Gates of Europe: A History of Ukraine** by Serhii Plokhy (2015) — A historian's account of Ukraine from ancient times to the present, indispensable for understanding how Donetsk came to matter in the way it does. Plokhy is scrupulous and readable; his chapters on industrialisation and Soviet history illuminate exactly why the Donbas became what it became.

**Chornobyl Prayer** by Svetlana Alexievich (1997) — Not about Donetsk, but about the Soviet world that made it. Alexievich's method of assembling voices into collective testimony illuminates the human cost of Soviet industrial ambition — a template for how to understand Donetsk's own history.
