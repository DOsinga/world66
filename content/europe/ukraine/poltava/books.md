**Poltava** by Aleksandr Pushkin (1829) — An epic poem about the Battle of Poltava and the figures caught up in it: Peter the Great, Charles XII, Hetman Mazepa, and the young Ukrainian woman Mazepa loved. Pushkin's portrait of Mazepa is hostile (he is cast as a traitor and villain), which made the poem politically controversial in Ukraine from the start. Read it to understand how Russia mythologised the battle — and why that mythology remains contested.

**Eneida** by Ivan Kotlyarevsky (1798) — The founding text of modern Ukrainian literature, written right here in Poltava. Kotlyarevsky took Virgil's *Aeneid* and transplanted it wholesale into the world of Ukrainian Cossacks: Aeneas becomes a Cossack chief, his crew a band of rowdy Zaporozhian soldiers. The result is ribald, funny, and full of precise detail about Cossack life, customs, and language. It reads like nothing else from its era.

**Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka** by Nikolai Gogol (1831–32) — Gogol grew up in the Poltava region and set these early stories in the Ukrainian countryside he knew as a child. They are comic, supernatural, and rich in Ukrainian folk culture: witches, devils, Cossack ghosts, and village life. Poltava and the surrounding villages feel recognisable in them even now.

**The Cossack Hetmanate** by Serhii Plokhy (various works) — Plokhy, the pre-eminent Western historian of Ukraine, has written extensively on the Cossack period and on the Battle of Poltava's aftermath. His *The Origins of the Slavic Nations* and *The Gates of Europe* both address what the battle meant for Ukraine's subsequent absorption into the Russian Empire. Essential background for understanding what you are looking at when you stand at the battlefield.
