  The Red Lodge became in 1854 the site of Britain's first reformatory school for girls, run by Mary Carpenter, a pioneering social reformer who believed that girls who had been imprisoned or criminalised needed education rather than punishment. Carpenter's work at the Red Lodge influenced national legislation on juvenile justice and helped establish the modern concept of reform schools.

The Red Lodge Museum on Park Row is one of Bristol's hidden gems — a small Tudor building largely invisible from the street, containing a remarkable set of 16th-century carved wood panelled rooms alongside a Georgian parlour. The house was built around 1590 as a gatehouse to the Great House below (which no longer exists), and the Elizabethan interiors on the ground floor are among the best-preserved of their kind in any English domestic building.

The carved stone chimneypieces, Elizabethan plasterwork, and oak panelling in the Tudor rooms are of exceptional quality. The house's later history — as a reformatory school for girls run by the social reformer Mary Carpenter in the Victorian era — adds another layer of interest. The museum is free to enter and rarely crowded; most visitors to Park Street and the university area walk straight past it without knowing it is there.
