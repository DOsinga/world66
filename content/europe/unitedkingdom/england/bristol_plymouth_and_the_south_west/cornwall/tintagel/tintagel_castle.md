  In 1998, archaeologists at Tintagel found a piece of slate with an inscription in Latin and a name that has been read as "Artognou" — close enough to "Arthur" to cause enormous excitement. The stone, known as the "Artognou Stone" or "Tintagel Stone," was dismissed by some scholars as a coincidence and celebrated by others as the closest thing to physical evidence for a historical Arthur ever found. The debate has never been fully resolved. The stone is now in the Royal Cornwall Museum in Truro.

Tintagel Castle is one of the most dramatic ruined sites in England — not because of the quality of the medieval masonry (it is modest) but because of its position, thrust out on a rocky headland above the Atlantic with the sea visible on three sides and cliffs falling away steeply beneath the walls. English Heritage maintains the site, and the new bridge across the gap between the mainland and the castle island, opened in 2019, makes the approach more straightforward than it used to be.

The medieval castle was built in the 1230s, possibly by the Earl of Cornwall, Richard of Cornwall, who had Arthurian associations in mind when he chose the site. Below the medieval ruins, excavations have revealed a substantial settlement from the 5th and 6th centuries — including fragments of fine pottery imported from the Mediterranean, suggesting a place of wealth and power in the post-Roman period. This is the period of the legendary Arthur, and the connection is not dismissed by serious archaeologists. The site is busy in summer; early morning visits are more rewarding.
