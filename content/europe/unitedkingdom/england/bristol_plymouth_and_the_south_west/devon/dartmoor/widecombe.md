Widecombe-in-the-Moor is a small granite village at the heart of the southern part of Dartmoor, better known to most people through the old folk song "[Widecombe Fair](/europe/unitedkingdom/england/bristol_plymouth_and_the_south_west/devon/dartmoor/widecombe/widecombe_fair)." The song tells the tale of a horse that died from the strain of carrying seven riders to the fair, and whose ghost still haunts the moor — a story that suits the windswept landscape rather well.

The village church, dedicated to St Pancras, is sometimes called the [Cathedral of the Moors](/europe/unitedkingdom/england/bristol_plymouth_and_the_south_west/devon/dartmoor/widecombe/st_pancras_church) for its strikingly tall tower, which can be seen from miles across the open moorland. Adjacent to it, [Sexton's Cottage](/europe/unitedkingdom/england/bristol_plymouth_and_the_south_west/devon/dartmoor/widecombe/sexton_cottage) is a 14th-century granite building run by the National Trust with information on the area. Two old pubs serve the village: the New Inn in the centre and the [Rugglestone Inn](/europe/unitedkingdom/england/bristol_plymouth_and_the_south_west/devon/dartmoor/widecombe/rugglestone_inn) half a mile to the south, a small, unspoiled local worth the short walk.

A short drive northeast across the moor brings you to [Grimspound](/europe/unitedkingdom/england/bristol_plymouth_and_the_south_west/devon/dartmoor/widecombe/grimspound), one of the best-preserved Bronze Age settlements in Britain — a complete enclosure wall with 24 hut circles still legible on the hillside. Arthur Conan Doyle used this part of the moor as the backdrop for The Hound of the Baskervilles.
