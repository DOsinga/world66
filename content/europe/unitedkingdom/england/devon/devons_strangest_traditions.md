Devon's countryside comes with an unusual number of stories attached. Every 5 November, the men of Ottery St Mary hoist burning barrels of tar onto their shoulders and run them through packed streets — a custom that cannot be insured, cannot be risk-assessed, and has continued uninterrupted for as long as anyone can document. On Dartmoor, a dwarf oak wood at 380 metres has a reputation for druids and the Wild Hunt, a Neolithic tomb is said to have been built by three spinsters before breakfast, and a folk song about Widecombe Fair still tells of a horse worked to death by seven riders and its ghost that haunts the moor ever since.

The through-line is a county that has never quite let go of its older explanations for things. A church perched on a volcanic plug near Tavistock has a legend that the devil moved its building stones back down the hill every night; Buckland Abbey keeps Sir Francis Drake's drum, said to beat on its own whenever England is in danger. None of it is provable, all of it is still told exactly the way it always was, and that persistence is the real attraction.
