Dorset has a strange relationship with its own rock. Above the village of Cerne Abbas, an enormous figure is cut straight into the chalk hillside, its origin and age still argued over. On the clifftop above Swanage stands a Gothic Revival clock tower that was built on the bank of the Thames in London, condemned as a traffic nuisance, and shipped down to Dorset in 1867 to be re-erected as a folly — it lost its clock in the move and has been telling no time at all for over 150 years. Nearby, a Victorian stone merchant carved a ten-foot globe of the world from 40 tonnes of Portland stone and surrounded it with improving Bible verses, while the cliffs below hide square chambers quarried straight out of the rock face and abandoned since a 1976 rockfall.

Follow the stone out to sea and you reach the Isle of Portland, a wind-scoured peninsula that has been quarried for centuries to build half of London's most famous landmarks — St Paul's Cathedral, Buckingham Palace, the United Nations headquarters — while a spent quarry on the same island has been turned into an open-air sculpture park. Dorset's stone built other places as often as it built itself, and the leftover holes became some of the county's oddest attractions.
