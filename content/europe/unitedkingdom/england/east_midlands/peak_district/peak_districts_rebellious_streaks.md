The Peak District's moorland looks placid enough, but a surprising amount of its history is about people and places refusing to fall in line. In 1932, ramblers walked onto the private grouse moor of Kinder Scout in deliberate defiance of the landowners who kept it closed — several were jailed, and the mass trespass is now credited as the spark that eventually forced open England's right to roam. Down in Castleton, Peak Cavern's entrance chamber housed a working community of rope-makers for centuries; its blunter old name, the Devil's Arse, was reportedly softened only for Queen Victoria's benefit.

Even the geology won't cooperate: Mam Tor sheds so much loose shale that the old road beneath it was abandoned for good in 1979, earning the hill its nickname, the Shivering Mountain. Add a churchyard in Hathersage where locals will point you to the reputed grave of Little John, and a pass at Winnats still tied to an 18th-century tale of robbery and murder, and the throughline is clear: this is a landscape that keeps breaking its own rules.
