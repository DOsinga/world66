  Julian wrote "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well" — possibly the most quoted phrase from any medieval English text. She wrote it after a series of visions she experienced in 1373 while believed to be near death. She spent the next 20 years in her cell at St Julian's Church working out what the visions meant. The result, "Revelations of Divine Love," is the earliest surviving book written in English by a woman.

The Church of St Julian of Norwich, on King Street south of the city centre, contains a reconstructed anchorite's cell where Julian lived from around 1373 until her death in the early 15th century. An anchorite was walled into their cell permanently — there was a window to the church and a window to the outside world, but no door. In exchange for this, the church provided food and the anchorite prayed and offered spiritual counsel to those who came to the window.

Julian's original cell was destroyed in the Second World War (a German bomb hit the church directly); the reconstruction is simple and moving. The church itself, heavily rebuilt after the bombing, is peaceful and welcoming. Julian remains an important figure in Christian mysticism, and visitors come from around the world to the cell. Entry is free.
