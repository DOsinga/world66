A folly is supposed to be pointless by definition, but England's collection of them turns out to have very specific reasons behind the excess. A Catholic gentleman jailed for his faith built a lodge where every wall, window, and inscription runs in threes, a coded tribute to the Holy Trinity. A linoleum baron commissioned a baroque dome the size of a small cathedral as a monument to his dead wife. A Buxton farmer, with nothing more sentimental in mind, put up a tower on an ancient burial mound simply to give local men work through a hard winter.

Others carry a more uncomfortable history — Richmond's Culloden Tower commemorates a battle that reads very differently today than it did to the men who built it — or nothing more complicated than a hunger for a good view, like the naval memorial folly on Shooter's Hill in Greenwich. Grief, faith, guilt, charity, ego: these six towers are a strange kind of monument to the private obsessions that money could buy.
