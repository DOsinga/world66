Exmoor's moorland hides some genuinely strange landmarks among the red deer and heather. A dry valley of jagged rock formations near Lynton comes with its own colony of indifferent feral goats — Wordsworth and Coleridge walked through it while dreaming up "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner," and it still looks like the setting for something supernatural. Down the coast at Combe Martin, a 17th-century house was built to the exact proportions of a deck of cards: 52 windows, 13 doors, four floors, four turrets. And at Culbone, England's smallest complete parish church sits three kilometres from the nearest road, reachable only on foot through ancient oak woodland.

What ties these together is Exmoor's habit of turning practical or ordinary things — a market crossing, a boundary stone, a river ford — into something the locals kept telling stories about for centuries. The stones at Tarr Steps are numbered so they can be put back after every flood; the Caratacus Stone was once thought cursed after a wheelwright who knocked it over died soon after; and Malmsmead's ford became a literary pilgrimage site the moment a Victorian novelist decided outlaws lived in the valley behind it. Exmoor rewards visitors who go looking for the odd detail as much as the view.
