Wordsworth and Beatrix Potter get most of the credit for the Lake District's sense of history, but the Romans were here first, and more of them stayed than you'd think. A fort at Hardknott still stands stark and windswept on its own pass. A short walk from Ravenglass leads to a bath house whose walls, at four metres, are the tallest surviving Roman masonry in the north of England — preserved only because a farmer turned it into a sheep pen for a few centuries. And at Ambleside, a fort called Galava marks the spot where lake, valley, and mountain pass all meet, exactly as a Roman engineer would have wanted.

The thread that ties it all together is the road, not the ruins. High Street, one of the range's highest fells, is named for the Roman route that once ran along its ridge, connecting the garrisons scattered around the Lakes to the wider network anchoring Hadrian's Wall. Walk it today and you are, in a literal sense, retracing a two-thousand-year-old commute.
