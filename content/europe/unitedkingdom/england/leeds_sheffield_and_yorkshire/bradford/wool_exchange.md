  When the Wool Exchange opened in 1867, Bradford handled around two-thirds of the world's commercial wool trade — every key British, French, German and American buyer kept an office on the floor. Charles Dickens gave a public reading in the hall in 1869 as part of its inaugural cultural programme; the famous Italian tenor Mario Lanza performed here a century later. Today the trading hall belongs to Waterstones, which sells paperbacks where wool brokers once haggled.

A grade-I listed Victorian Gothic palace by Lockwood & Mawson, opened in 1867 as the trading floor for what was then the wool capital of the world. The exterior is sandstone with sculptural niches holding statues of explorers, scientists and merchants (including Cobden, Salt and Columbus); the interior is a single soaring trading hall under a hammer-beam timber roof.

After the wool trade collapsed in the 1960s the building drifted, but it was sensitively converted in 2001 into a Waterstones bookshop on the ground floor with offices above. The shop is one of the most architecturally striking branches of any major British retailer — you can browse for paperbacks under sculpted portraits of Bradford's industrial founders. Free to enter during shop hours.
