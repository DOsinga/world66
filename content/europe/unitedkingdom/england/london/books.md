title: "Books"

---

London has inspired an enormous literature. A few books that will make you see the city differently — on the Tube, in the streets, in its history.

**White Teeth** by Zadie Smith. Smith's debut novel, set in Willesden and Cricklewood in north London, follows two families — one Bangladeshi, one West Indian — across several generations. It captures multicultural London with great energy and warmth, and the north London it describes — the chip shops, the pubs, the council estates, the corner shops — is still recognisable today.

**Saturday** by Ian McEwan. A single Saturday in February 2003 — the day of the anti-Iraq war march — seen through the eyes of a neurosurgeon living in a grand square near Fitzrovia. McEwan maps central London with precision: the streets, the traffic, the hospital, the rhythms of a particular kind of London life. The political anxiety of that moment gives it an edge that hasn't faded.

**The Buddha of Suburbia** by Hanif Kureishi. A coming-of-age novel set in south London's suburbs in the 1970s, following a mixed-race teenager moving between the dull commuter belt and the louche world of arts London. Funny, sharp, and still fresh — it captures the tensions between suburb and city, tradition and reinvention, that define London's outer rings as much today as then.

**Hawksmoor** by Peter Ackroyd. Ackroyd's 1985 novel interweaves eighteenth-century London with the present, connecting the strange geometric churches of Nicholas Hawksmoor (Spitalfields, Limehouse, Bloomsbury) with a series of murders. Dense, atmospheric, and genuinely unsettling — it will make you look at those churches very differently.

**Londoners: The Days and Nights of London Now** by Craig Taylor. An oral history of the city at the turn of the twenty-first century, assembled from interviews with more than two hundred Londoners: tube drivers, club promoters, immigrant workers, night-shift nurses, market traders, hedge fund managers. More than any single novel, this book gives you the texture of the city as actually lived. Read it on the Tube.
