Plenty of London churches are notable simply for surviving — the Great Fire, the Blitz, decades of redevelopment — but some carry a genuinely odd twist beyond their age. St Dunstan in the East is a Wren church that never got rebuilt after being bombed in 1941; instead of demolishing the ruin, the city planted it, and ivy now climbs the empty window tracery. Temple Church keeps the round nave the Knights Templar modelled on Jerusalem's Church of the Holy Sepulchre, with stone knights lying flat on the floor. St Giles-without-Cripplegate is a medieval parish church that somehow ended up boxed inside the concrete walkways of the Barbican Estate.

Others hide their oddity in the detail rather than the setting: St Stephen Walbrook pairs Wren's dome with a smooth circular altar by Henry Moore, St Magnus the Martyr keeps a scale model of the old London Bridge that once stood a few yards from its door, and St Augustine's Tower is the last surviving scrap of Hackney's medieval parish church, opened to the public a few afternoons a year purely so people can climb it for the view. Between them they cover most of what a City church can surprise you with — ruin, geometry, incongruity, and a good vantage point.
