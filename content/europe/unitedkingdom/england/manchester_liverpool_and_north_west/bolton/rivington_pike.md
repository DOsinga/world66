story: "The tower on Rivington Pike was built in 1733, but a beacon has stood on this hill for far longer. During the Spanish Armada scare of 1588, a fire beacon on the pike was part of the chain that carried the warning from the south coast northward across England — one link in a network of hilltop fires that could transmit news of invasion faster than any rider. The current tower replaced an earlier wooden structure, but it stands on the same site and serves the same symbolic function: a fixed point that reminds people of the hill's long visibility for miles around."

Rivington Pike is the most prominent landmark on the West Pennine Moors, a modest but shapely hill topped by an eighteenth-century stone tower that can be seen from much of Lancashire on a clear day. The walk up from Rivington village takes around forty minutes at an easy pace, following well-worn paths through heather and bracken with views opening up steadily as you climb. The summit rewards the effort with a panorama that on good days reaches to the Irish Sea to the west and the Pennines to the east.

The tower itself is a simple square structure, locked but accessible at the base for photographs. A reservoir complex built by Liverpool Corporation in the nineteenth century fills the valley below, and the whole landscape has a managed, slightly melancholy quality typical of upland water-catchment areas. The Rivington terraced gardens lie on the wooded slopes below the pike and can be combined with the summit walk in a half-day circuit that takes in both the open moorland and the hidden garden landscape.
