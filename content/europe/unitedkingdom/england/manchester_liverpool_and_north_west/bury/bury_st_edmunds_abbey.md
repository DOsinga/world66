The Abbey of Bury St Edmunds was once among the wealthiest Benedictine monasteries in England. Founded in the 7th century and rebuilt on a grand scale in the 11th, it housed the remains of the martyred Anglo-Saxon king Edmund, who had been killed by Danish Vikings in 869 and subsequently canonised. The shrine made Bury a major pilgrimage centre for centuries.

Henry VIII dissolved the abbey in 1539 and the townspeople systematically quarried its stone for building materials over the following decades — a common fate for suppressed monasteries, but particularly complete here. What survives today is nonetheless substantial: long sections of the original precinct wall, the Abbey Gate (a fortified 14th-century gateway of considerable grandeur), the Norman Tower that now serves as the bell tower for the cathedral, and the outline of the church foundations, which make plain the extraordinary scale of what once stood here. The foundations run to over 150 metres — longer than most English cathedrals. The Abbey Gardens, which surround these remains, are freely open and beautifully maintained.
