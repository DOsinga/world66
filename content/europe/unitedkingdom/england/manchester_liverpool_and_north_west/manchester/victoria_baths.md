  Victoria Baths opened in 1906 as the most elaborate public baths in England,
  with three pools (men's first class, men's second class, and women's), Turkish
  baths, and extraordinarily detailed tilework throughout. They were known locally
  as "a water palace." The baths closed in 1993 due to repair costs. A decade later,
  the building won the BBC's Restoration series in 2003 — the public vote brought
  £3 million in heritage lottery funding and saved it from permanent dereliction.

Victoria Baths is a red-brick and terracotta monument to the municipal ambition of Edwardian Manchester. The main facade, with its Islamic-influenced geometric tiles and Gothic detailing, gives no hint that this is a swimming baths rather than a town hall or museum. Inside, the original pool halls retain their galleries, changing cubicles, and — in the first-class pool — extraordinary stained glass windows.

The baths are open on selected weekends for tours and community events (check the website for current openings). Restoration work continues slowly, funded by heritage grants and events. Not all of the building is accessible, but what can be seen is spectacular and gives a clear picture of how seriously Victorian and Edwardian cities took the welfare of their citizens — and of the social stratification embedded in "first class" and "second class" pools separated only by a wall.
