  In 1652 the young George Fox climbed Pendle Hill and had a vision that led directly to the founding of the Quakers. He wrote in his journal that he was "moved of the Lord to go atop of it" and from the summit saw "a great people to be gathered." The Quaker movement he went on to build had millions of followers worldwide. The hill was already notorious for witches forty years before Fox arrived.

Pendle Hill rises to 557 metres and dominates the skyline across much of east Lancashire. The ascent from Downham is the most popular route — a steady climb of about an hour through fields and moorland that opens out to wide views across the Ribble Valley, the Forest of Bowland, and on clear days as far as the Lake District and Morecambe Bay. The top is grassy and slightly boggy; the trig point sits at the southern end of the flat summit plateau.

The hill is permanently associated with the Pendle Witch trials of 1612, when twelve people from the surrounding villages were accused of murder by witchcraft and taken to Lancaster for trial. Ten were hanged. The story is well told in local museums at Clitheroe and Lancaster, and a good deal of the Pendle tourism industry is built around it. None of which diminishes the hill itself, which is handsome and rewarding.
