  The tomb of St Cuthbert — the 7th-century monk and Bishop of Lindisfarne — has been in Durham Cathedral since 995, when monks fleeing Viking raids finally settled here after over a century of wandering with his uncorrupted body. For medieval pilgrims, Durham was one of the great shrines of northern Europe. The Venerable Bede, the greatest scholar of early medieval England, is also buried here, in the Galilee Chapel at the west end.
---

Durham Cathedral is one of the outstanding buildings of the Norman Romanesque world — its great cylindrical pillars, carved with geometric zigzag and chevron patterns, and its pioneering ribbed vaulting (one of the earliest in Europe) make it architecturally as well as spiritually significant. UNESCO listed it alongside Durham Castle as a World Heritage Site. The best first view is from the railway viaduct to the south, or from the riverbank path below — the cathedral and castle together on their wooded peninsula, seen from the water, is one of the great townscapes of England.

Inside, the **Galilee Chapel** at the west end is the most atmospheric corner: 12th-century Romanesque, low and intimate, with the simple tomb slab of the Venerable Bede and faded traces of original wall paintings. The cathedral treasury houses St Cuthbert's wooden coffin (c. 698), his pectoral cross, and other extraordinary relics. Climbing the **central tower** — 325 steps — gives views across the city and the surrounding hills. Visits are free, though a donation is encouraged. Harry Potter fans will recognise the cloisters and Galilee Chapel, both used in the films.
