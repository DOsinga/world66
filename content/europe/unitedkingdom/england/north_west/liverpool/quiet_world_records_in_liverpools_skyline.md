Everyone knows Liverpool for the Beatles and the football, which means its other, quieter habit of setting world records in brick and concrete goes almost unmentioned. A modest office building on Water Street, mocked by critics as "a great abortion" when it opened in 1864, is now credited as the ancestor of every glass-walled skyscraper in Chicago and New York. Albert Dock was Britain's first structure built entirely from cast iron, brick and stone — no structural timber at all, so it couldn't burn. The Royal Liver Building was the tallest in Britain when it opened in 1911, an early showcase for reinforced concrete at a scale nobody had tried before, and a few miles north the Stanley Dock Tobacco Warehouse is still the largest brick building on earth by floor area.

Liverpool Cathedral tops the list off with sheer size — Britain's largest cathedral, fifth largest in the world, designed by an architect who won the commission at 22 and didn't live to see it finished 74 years later. None of these records get shouted about locally; they're just what happens when a city with more money than patience keeps trying to build the biggest version of everything.
