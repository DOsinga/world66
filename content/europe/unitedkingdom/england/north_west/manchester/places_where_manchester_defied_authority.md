Manchester's tourist trail runs through Victorian civic grandeur, but the city's real distinction is a habit of producing people the establishment wanted to shut up. On St Peter's Field, cavalry charged sixty thousand peaceful reformists in 1819; the massacre is remembered now by a quiet stone memorial a short walk from the terraced house where Emmeline Pankhurst founded the suffragette movement eighty years later. In the reading bay of a medieval library, Marx and Engels worked out the ideas that became *Das Kapital*, a few streets from where Alan Turing, prosecuted by the same state he had helped save from the Nazis, is remembered on a park bench with an apple in his hand.

None of this is polished heritage-trail material — Peterloo waited two hundred years for a memorial, and Turing's took fifty. What connects these five places is that Manchester kept being where Britain's arguments with itself actually happened, not just where they were written up afterwards.
