"Forest" is doing a lot of legal, not botanical, work in the New Forest's name — it was created as a royal hunting preserve, not a wood, and the place has been shaped by people ever since. An iron marker in the trees near Brook marks where King William II was killed by an arrow in 1100, accident or assassination never settled. At Buckler's Hard, a quiet street of shipwrights' cottages runs down to slipways where Royal Navy warships, including Nelson's favourite ship, were built from New Forest oak. The ponies wandering the car parks and pub gardens aren't wildlife either — they're commoners' livestock, grazing on ancient legal rights that outrank the Highway Code.

Even the reliably natural-seeming spots have a catch: Blashford Lakes is a prime birdwatching reserve built entirely out of old gravel pits, and the wolves at New Forest Wildlife Park are the only ones you'll find in England, since the real ones were hunted out centuries ago. The Knightwood Oak, at least, is exactly what it looks like — just a 500-year-old tree that everyone agreed to call a Queen.
