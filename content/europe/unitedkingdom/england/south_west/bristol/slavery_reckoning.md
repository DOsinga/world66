Bristol's Georgian wealth came substantially from the transatlantic slave trade, and the city has spent the years since 2020 working out, often awkwardly and in public, what to do with that inheritance. The clearest flashpoint is the empty plinth on Anchor Road, bare since a Black Lives Matter crowd toppled Edward Colston's statue and rolled it into the harbour — the statue itself now lies on its side in M Shed, still wearing the placards protesters hung on it. A short walk away, Pero's Bridge carries the name of an enslaved man who lived and died a few streets from where it stands, its two counterweights deliberately shaped like the yokes used to restrain enslaved people.

None of this is contained to a single museum room. It runs through a concert hall that dropped its old name once that name's owner became too uncomfortable to keep, a sugar merchant's townhouse preserved exactly as his money furnished it, and a Quaker burial ground for a community organising against the trade nearly half a century before Britain outlawed it. Together they show a city that inherited its wealth from something it would rather not have, and is still, visibly, deciding how to live with that.
