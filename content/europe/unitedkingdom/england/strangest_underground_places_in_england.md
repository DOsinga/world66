England's underground doesn't get talked about as much as its castles and cathedrals, but plenty of it is stranger than what's above ground. A wealthy Liverpool tobacco merchant spent the last 25 years of his life secretly paying labourers to dig tunnels that led nowhere in particular. Beneath Nottingham sits a network of more than 800 hand-carved caves the city has been living, working, and sheltering in since the Saxons — its old name translates as "place of cave dwellings." And outside York, a nondescript bunker once housed a three-person crew whose job was to watch for a nuclear attack that, thankfully, never came.

Some of it is genuine engineering: a driverless postal railway that ran beneath London for 76 years, hand-dug wartime shelters that gave Ramsgate one of the lowest civilian casualty rates of the Blitz. Some of it is just eccentricity, and some — like Derby's sealed Victorian catacombs, still holding their original ledger stones — is the kind of atmospheric that no amount of restoration quite shakes off. All of it is a reminder that England's stranger history is sometimes the kind you have to go downstairs for.
