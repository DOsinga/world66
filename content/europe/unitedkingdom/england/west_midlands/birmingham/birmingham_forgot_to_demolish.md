Birmingham demolished its way through the twentieth century more thoroughly than almost any other British city — slum clearance, ring roads, and a genuine civic enthusiasm for starting over. Which makes it stranger that a handful of things slipped through. The Back to Backs on Hurst Street are the last of roughly 43,000 back-to-back houses that once packed the city's inner courts; everywhere else, they went. Newman Brothers Coffin Works closed in 1998 and was left almost exactly as the workforce found it that morning — lockers, machinery, and paperwork all still in place — and the Smith & Pepper factory in the Jewellery Quarter survived the same way, frozen since it shut down in 1981.

Further back, the survivors get older and odder. The Old Crown, a timber-framed pub in Digbeth, dates from the early 1500s and predates the Industrial Revolution that swallowed almost everything around it. Blakesley Hall, a black-and-white Tudor house in Yardley, is what a Birmingham village looked like before it became a suburb. Soho House, Matthew Boulton's home and the meeting place of the Lunar Society, outlasted the very industrial expansion its members helped set in motion. Curzon Street Station is the strangest case of all: opened in 1837 as the world's first intercity railway terminus, it has stood empty since 1854 and is now being folded into Britain's newest station, HS2, next door.
