Leeds built confidently in the Victorian boom, then kept finding new jobs for what it built rather than pulling any of it down. The domed Corn Exchange existed purely so grain merchants could inspect samples in natural light; it now shelters two galleries of independent shops and cafes under the same elliptical roof. A short walk away, the Art Deco headquarters of a defunct brewery has become the Tetley Gallery, showing contemporary art where the offices of Tetley's used to be. St Pauls House, built in the 1870s for a firm of cloth-cutters, still wears the minarets and coloured tiles its owner chose to make the works look like a Moorish palace.

None of it reads as conversion for its own sake. Salem Chapel gave up its pews for office desks, but is better known locally as the building where Leeds United Football Club was founded. The Carriageworks theatre takes its name from the Victorian carriage works that once stood on the site and now performs inside a former printing works next door. Even Leeds City Museum, a Cuthbert Brodrick design that started life as a mechanics' institute, did time as a civic theatre and a music college before settling into its current job. Leeds seems constitutionally unable to leave a good building standing empty.
