Some of Leeds' oddest sights are odd simply because they should not still be here. Kirkstall Abbey was founded in 1152 and stands almost fully intact, nave and tower included, despite four centuries of neglect since Henry VIII dissolved it. The Middleton Railway has been hauling passengers and freight since 1758, making it, by most tellings, the oldest continuously working public railway on Earth. Whitelock's Ale House has poured drinks from the same narrow, tile-lined room since 1715 and has outlasted both wartime bombing and the modern price of a craft pint.

Others survive by accident rather than defiance. The Bear Pit is the last visible trace of a Victorian zoo that closed within a few decades of opening; Thwaite Watermill kept its machinery through a century of industrial decline that flattened most of Leeds' other mills. Roundhay Park holds an odder record still: it is where, in 1888, Louis Le Prince shot what is now recognised as the world's oldest surviving film footage, seven years before the Lumière brothers are usually credited with inventing cinema. Taken together, these are the parts of Leeds that time simply failed to get around to.
