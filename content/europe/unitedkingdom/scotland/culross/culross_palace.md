story: "The 'Palace' of Culross was never a royal residence — the name derives from the Scots word 'pailyeoun,' meaning a large building, misapplied by enthusiastic later visitors. It was built between 1597 and 1611 by Sir George Bruce of Carnock, a merchant who made his fortune from the coal mines that ran under the Firth of Forth. Bruce had the extraordinary engineering confidence to sink a mine shaft that emerged from the sea floor below the water of the Firth — accessed at low tide by a small island. King James VI of Scotland visited and, emerging from the underwater shaft onto what appeared to be an island surrounded by water, demanded to know if his host was accusing him of witchcraft."

Culross Palace is the most complete surviving example of a prosperous merchant's house from late 16th and early 17th century Scotland, and the architectural centrepiece of the village. Built by the coal merchant Sir George Bruce between 1597 and 1611, the building is notable for its ochre-washed exterior, its crow-stepped gables, its pine-panelled rooms, and above all its remarkable painted ceilings — tempera paintings depicting allegorical, Biblical, and classical scenes in a style that blends Scottish vernacular tradition with Continental influences.

The building was left in a state of near-ruin by the 18th century but was rescued and meticulously restored by the National Trust for Scotland from the 1930s onwards. The interiors are furnished in period style with authentic pieces. The walled garden behind the palace has been replanted as a 17th-century herb and vegetable garden using historical records. Open April–October; check NTS website for current hours and admission prices.
