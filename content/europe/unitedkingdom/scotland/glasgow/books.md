---
title: "Books"
type: section
---

Glasgow has a rich literary tradition — it is one of the most written-about cities in Britain, with a particular fondness for crime fiction and working-class realism.

**Alasdair Gray, *Lanark*** (1981). The essential Glasgow novel, written over thirty years and mixing grim realist fiction about a Glasgow art student with a surrealist nightmare of bureaucratic dystopia. It is difficult and long and extraordinary. Begin here.

**William McIlvanney, *Laidlaw*** (1977). The first of McIlvanney's Glasgow detective novels featuring Jack Laidlaw, a philosophical cop navigating the city's criminal underworld. McIlvanney invented Tartan Noir and set the template for every Scottish crime novel that followed.

**Denise Mina, *Garnethill*** (1998). Mina's debut, set in the hill neighbourhood above Sauchiehall Street, follows a psychiatric patient who discovers a murder in her flat. Ferociously plotted, psychologically precise, and absolutely rooted in Glasgow's geography.

**Peter Capaldi (director), *Franz Kafka's It's a Wonderful Life*** — not a book, but for a sense of the Glasgow artistic milieu, this Oscar-winning short film captures the absurdist wit the city's creative community runs on.

**Andrew O'Hagan, *Our Fathers*** (1999). A novel about three generations of a Scottish family set against the decline of the post-war housing estates — the tower blocks that defined Glasgow's ambitions and broke them. Clear-eyed and elegiac.
