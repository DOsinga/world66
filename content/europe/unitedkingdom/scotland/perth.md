Perth sits on the River Tay at the southern edge of the Highlands, a position that made it one of the most strategically important towns in medieval Scotland. It was a royal residence throughout the Middle Ages and served as the effective capital of Scotland until the 15th century — hence the unofficial title "The Ancient Capital of Scotland." The town centre retains its medieval street pattern, flanked by two large parks (the [North Inch](/europe/unitedkingdom/scotland/perth/north_inch) and South Inch) where clan conflicts and the 1715 Jacobite uprising left their marks.

[St John's Kirk](/europe/unitedkingdom/scotland/perth/st_johns_kirk), the oldest building in the centre, is where John Knox preached the inflammatory sermon in 1559 that sparked the Scottish Reformation. The new [Perth Museum](/europe/unitedkingdom/scotland/perth/perth_museum), housed in the restored City Hall, reopened in 2024 and now displays the Destiny Stone — a fragment of the original Stone of Scone. A few miles north, [Scone Palace](/europe/unitedkingdom/scotland/perth/scone_palace) occupies the site where Scottish kings were crowned for centuries. The palace grounds are open to visitors and the state rooms contain a good collection of furniture and art.

Perth makes a natural gateway to the southern Highlands. Dunkeld and Birnam, 20 km north by train or road, are worth a half-day: the ruined cathedral, the Beatrix Potter Garden, and the woodland walks at the Hermitage all cluster in a small area. The town itself is notable for its flowers — a long tradition of public planting that gives even the commercial streets a distinctive seasonal character. [Branklyn Garden](/europe/unitedkingdom/scotland/perth/branklyn_garden) on the south bank of the Tay is one of Scotland's finest small gardens, spectacular in spring.
