  The Road Hole bunker — the 17th fairway's narrow pot bunker — has a claim to being the most feared single hazard in golf. Jack Nicklaus once took five shots to escape it. Tom Watson's approach shot into the same bunker in the 1984 Open cost him the championship. The bunker is named for the road behind the 17th green, which is itself in play — if you overshoot the green, your ball rolls onto a public road.

The Old Course at St Andrews is the most famous golf course in the world and is widely considered the spiritual home of the game. Golf has been played on these Links since at least 1457, when James II of Scotland banned it because his subjects were neglecting archery practice. The Royal and Ancient Golf Club was founded here in 1754 and still governs the game internationally. The course has hosted the Open Championship more times than any other venue.

Getting a tee time is the central challenge. The ballot for non-member tee times opens the evening before for the following day's play; you submit your application and the computer draws randomly. During summer, demand vastly outstrips supply. There is also a waiting-list ballot for advance booking. Green fees for a round on the Old Course are around £300 in high season, which places it firmly in luxury territory even for committed golfers.

If you have no interest in playing, the Old Course is still worth seeing. The entire course is **public land** — the Links is a common, and you can walk the fairways freely when play is not in progress (typically Sundays and certain other days). The 18th fairway and the Swilcan Bridge (the ancient stone bridge crossing the Swilcan Burn, photographed by virtually every player at the end of their round) are freely accessible at most times. The **R&A Clubhouse** at the edge of the 18th green is a wonderful building to look at from outside, though non-members cannot enter.

The **British Golf Museum** is directly adjacent, and for most visitors is a more accessible way to engage with the game's history. The town of St Andrews' golf-as-industry ecosystem — shops, hotels, academies, pilgrimage traffic — is itself a cultural spectacle worth observing.
