St Andrews Castle sits on a rocky promontory above the sea at the northern edge of the town, and it is more interesting than it looks from the outside. The building served as the fortified palace of the Archbishops of St Andrews from the 12th century onward, and several episodes of the Scottish Reformation played out here with particular violence.

The two structures most worth seeking out are underground. The **bottle dungeon** — a pit 7 metres deep cut directly into the bedrock, with an opening only 60 cm wide — held prisoners without any means of escape or rescue. Cardinal Beaton used it; some of its occupants were Protestant martyrs. The **mine and countermine tunnels**, dug during the 1546–47 siege, are the most extraordinary thing in the building: you can crouch and crawl through the countermine as it intercepts the besiegers' tunnel, exactly as they were dug nearly five centuries ago.

The castle also marks the spot where Cardinal David Beaton was murdered in 1546 — shot and hanged out of a window by a group of Protestant conspirators in retaliation for his burning of George Wishart. The conspirators then held the castle for a year before it fell to French forces supporting Mary of Guise. One of the Protestant defenders taken prisoner was John Knox, who spent the next nineteen months as a French galley slave before returning to Scotland to finish the Reformation. The **castle grounds** are open to the coastal path.

Admission is charged; Historic Environment Scotland runs the site. A joint ticket with the cathedral is available and represents good value.
