The ruins of St Andrews Cathedral stand at the eastern end of town on a headland above the sea, and even in their wrecked state they are impressive enough to justify the walk. Founded in 1158 and consecrated in 1318, the cathedral was the seat of the Primate of Scotland — effectively the headquarters of the Scottish church — and the most important religious building in the country. What you see now is essentially the shell: the west facade with its twin towers largely standing, the eastern gable intact, and the south wall of the nave open to the sky.

The **Cathedral Museum** (inside the priory precinct) is worth more time than people usually give it. Its prize exhibit is the **St Andrews Sarcophagus**, a Pictish carved monument from the 8th century that is one of the finest pieces of early medieval sculpture in Britain. The carving is intricate and remarkably well-preserved — a hunting scene with intertwining beasts on one face, David and the lion on another. Admission to the precinct and museum is charged; Historic Environment Scotland runs the site.

**St Rule's Tower**, the square tower of the earlier Romanesque church that the cathedral superseded, still stands to its full height next to the ruins. Climbing it (about 33 metres, via a tight spiral stair) gives the best view over the town — the grid of the three medieval streets, the castle headland to the north, and the East Sands beach stretching south. The view is the same one George Wishart's executioners would have had in 1546.
