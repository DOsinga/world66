  The Great Orme's Bronze Age copper mines were discovered by accident in 1987 when a contractor's digger broke through into ancient tunnels. Archaeologists subsequently found over 30,000 bones from cattle, sheep, and red deer inside the mine — the bones had been fashioned into tools and used as scoops and picks by miners working here around 1600 BC. The mines are the largest prehistoric copper workings ever found, and produced enough copper to make several million bronze axes.

The Great Orme is the defining feature of Llandudno — a vast limestone headland that dominates the coastline and rises to 207 metres above the sea. It is a National Nature Reserve, and the summit plateau has a character entirely unlike the busy resort town below: open grassland, wild goats (descendants of a herd given to Queen Victoria by the Shah of Persia), and on clear days a panoramic view that stretches from Snowdonia's peaks to the Isle of Man and the distant mountains of Ireland.

There are three ways up. The [Great Orme Tramway](/europe/unitedkingdom/wales/llandudno/great_orme_tramway) is the most enjoyable — a cable-hauled Victorian street tramway that clanks up through the streets of Llandudno before climbing the open hillside. The [Llandudno Cable Car](/europe/unitedkingdom/wales/llandudno/llandudno_cable_car) offers a more vertiginous ride from the Happy Valley gardens. Or you can drive or walk up the Marine Drive toll road. The summit has a visitor centre, a pub, a skiing slope of sorts (a dry ski run), and on a clear day, bragging rights to one of the finest views in Wales.
