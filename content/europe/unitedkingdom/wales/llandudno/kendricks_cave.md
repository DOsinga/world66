Kendrick's Cave is a small natural cave on the lower slopes of the Great Orme, named after the local quarryman Thomas Kendrick who discovered it in 1880. It became one of the most important Upper Palaeolithic sites in Britain after analysis of the finds revealed occupation going back to the end of the last Ice Age — around 10,000 BC. Among the objects found were animal bones, perforated teeth that had been worn as jewellery, and an engraved horse jaw bone that is now considered one of the oldest pieces of artwork in British history.

The cave itself is not open to visitors, but the finds are distributed between the Natural History Museum in London and the National Museum of Wales in Cardiff. The site is marked on the Great Orme, and the story of the cave is covered in the displays at the Great Orme's Bronze Age copper mine visitor centre. For anyone interested in the long prehistory of this headland — which has yielded evidence of human activity from the Stone Age through to the Victorian era — it is a significant piece of the picture.
