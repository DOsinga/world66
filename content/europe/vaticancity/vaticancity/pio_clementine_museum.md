The core of the Vatican's classical sculpture collection, founded in the late 18th century by Popes Clement XIV and Pius VI (hence the name). The galleries are arranged in a circuit of frescoed halls and octagonal courtyards in the Belvedere wing.

Two pieces dominate. The Laocoön group, rediscovered in 1506 in a Roman vineyard and bought by Pope Julius II within hours, shows the Trojan priest and his sons being killed by sea serpents — a writhing knot of human anatomy that influenced Michelangelo, Rubens and centuries of academic art. The Apollo Belvedere, a Roman copy of a lost Greek bronze, was for centuries considered the supreme example of male beauty in Western art; opinion has shifted, but the sculpture still arrests.

Smaller rooms hold the Belvedere Torso (another touchstone for Michelangelo), the Hermes of Praxiteles, the Sleeping Ariadne, and a series of imperial portrait busts. The Octagonal Courtyard at the centre is one of the more photographable spaces in the museum complex.
