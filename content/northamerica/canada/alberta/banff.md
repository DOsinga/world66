Banff exists because of an accident. In 1883, three Canadian Pacific Railway workers discovered hot springs on the eastern slope of Sulphur Mountain. The railway had a financial stake in tourism — they needed passengers — and within two years the federal government had protected the area as a national reserve. Canada’s first national park followed in 1887, and the grand Banff Springs Hotel opened in 1888 to receive the tourists the railway was bringing west. The town of Banff grew up to serve the park and the hotel, and it still does, though on a considerably larger scale.

At 1,372 metres above sea level, surrounded on all sides by peaks that rise to 3,000 metres and beyond, Banff is one of the most dramatically situated small towns in North America. The main street (Banff Avenue) is relentlessly commercial — gift shops, ski rental places, restaurants — but step off it in any direction and you are immediately in the landscape that justifies everything else. Elk wander the golf course. Bears cross the highway. The air tastes different at this altitude.

The surrounding national park covers 6,641 square kilometres and connects with Jasper, Yoho, and Kootenay national parks to form one of the largest protected mountain ecosystems on the continent. Most visitors in winter come for the skiing at three world-class resorts: Lake Louise, Sunshine Village, and Mt. Norquay. In summer the focus shifts to hiking, cycling, and driving the Icefields Parkway — arguably the most spectacular mountain road in Canada, running 230 kilometres north to Jasper.
