story: >
  Fort Normandeau was built in just ten days in April 1885, as news of the Riel Rebellion
  reached the Red Deer Crossing. The settlers who built it were genuinely afraid — the 1885
  uprising was violent and their community was exposed. In the end, the battle never reached
  them, and within a year the fort was abandoned. The site was rediscovered and excavated in
  the 1970s, and the current reconstruction is based on that archaeological evidence rather
  than guesswork.

About seven kilometres west of the city centre at the original Red Deer Crossing, Fort Normandeau is a reconstructed fur-trade and military post that tells the story of the 1885 North-West Rebellion from the perspective of central Alberta settlers. The site includes a log stockade, a period-furnished blockhouse, and exhibits on the Métis uprising and the Indigenous peoples of the region.

Costumed interpreters bring the post to life during summer months, demonstrating frontier skills and explaining the context of the rebellion. The location itself is atmospheric — the crossing on the Red Deer River was a critical junction on the trail between Calgary and Edmonton, and the river valley here is attractive enough to justify the drive even outside operating hours. Check the city's website for current seasonal hours before visiting.
