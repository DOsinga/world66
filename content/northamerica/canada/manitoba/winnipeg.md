Winnipeg sits almost exactly at the longitudinal centre of North America, at the confluence of the Red and Assiniboine Rivers — a meeting place used by Indigenous peoples for at least six thousand years before the Hudson's Bay Company built Fort Gibraltar nearby in 1738. Today it is the capital of Manitoba, a prairie city of 750,000, and the point where the Canadian Shield meets the Great Plains.

The city has a long, cold, unpretentious character. Locals call it "Winterpeg" for reasons that become obvious in January, when temperatures regularly hit minus thirty and the rivers freeze solid enough to skate on. But the summer is genuinely glorious, and the arts scene is remarkable for a city its size: the **Royal Winnipeg Ballet** is one of the oldest professional ballet companies in North America, the **Winnipeg Symphony Orchestra** fills Centennial Concert Hall, and **Folklorama** — two weeks of multicultural pavilions held each August — is the largest and longest-running festival of its kind in the world.

The **Canadian Museum for Human Rights**, a Daniel Libeskind building of stone, glass, and winding alabaster ramps, opened in 2014 as the first national museum of Canada built outside Ottawa. It anchors **The Forks**, the waterfront public space at the river junction, with its market hall, skating trails, and the world's longest naturally frozen skating trail stretching up to ten kilometres along the rivers in winter. A ten-minute walk north, the **Exchange District** — a National Historic Site of ornate early-20th-century brick warehouses — is the best collection of pre-1914 commercial architecture in Canada and the city's creative and arts hub.

Winnipeg is one of Canada's most culturally diverse cities per capita. The **North End** reflects waves of Ukrainian, Polish, and Jewish settlement; St. Boniface is the largest Francophone community in western Canada; and a large Métis and First Nations population gives the city an Indigenous cultural presence felt in galleries, restaurants, and public art. The food scene is better than the city's reputation: Filipino, Ukrainian, Icelandic, and Indigenous cuisines sit alongside an increasingly ambitious fine-dining scene. Perogies, Salisbury House "nips," and a BeaverTail at The Forks are all essential.
