  Moshe Safdie was a 23-year-old architecture student at McGill when he proposed Habitat 67 as his thesis project in 1961. Most professors thought it unbuildable. Six years later it was the centrepiece of Expo 67 — 354 prefabricated concrete modules stacked into 148 apartments, each with its own garden in the sky. The building was meant to show that high-density urban housing could feel like a private home. It still does, which is why the apartments now sell for millions of dollars.

Habitat 67 sits on the Marc-Drouin Quay of the St. Lawrence, about 15 minutes' walk from Old Montreal. Moshe Safdie designed it as a demonstration that high-density urban housing could be humane — each of the 148 apartments has its own outdoor garden terrace and a unique configuration, because no two modules stack in exactly the same way. The concrete block has aged beautifully and is now one of the most sought-after addresses in Montreal.

You cannot go inside without a booking (the residences are private, though occasional tours are organized), but walking around the exterior is free and fascinating. The interlocking concrete boxes, garden terraces, and the way the structure steps back from the river make it one of the great mid-century architectural objects in North America. Come in the morning when the light catches the garden terraces and the city skyline rises behind it.
