Prince Albert sits at the edge of two worlds. South of the city: the wide open prairie, the grain elevators, the big flat horizon. North of the city: boreal forest, a thousand lakes, wolves and bison, and one of Canada's great wilderness parks. The city itself — Saskatchewan's third largest, with about 35,000 people — has long served as the gateway between these two landscapes, and that position shapes everything about it.

Prince Albert National Park begins 51 km north, and it is the main reason most visitors come. The park protects nearly 4,000 square kilometres of spruce and birch forest, lakes threaded by canoe routes, and a free-roaming plains bison herd. It was designated a UNESCO Man and Biosphere Reserve — in part because of its ecological significance, in part because of Grey Owl, the English-born conservationist who lived and wrote here in a remote lakeside cabin and became one of the twentieth century's most influential voices for wilderness protection, even as he maintained a false identity as an Indigenous man for his entire public life.

The city itself rewards a day of slow exploration. The Diefenbaker House Museum preserves the home of John Diefenbaker, who represented Prince Albert in Parliament for nearly four decades before becoming Canada's thirteenth prime minister. The North Saskatchewan River bends through the city; the Rotary Trail runs along its bank. The Northern Lights Casino, owned collectively by the twelve First Nations of the Prince Albert Grand Council, has become one of the region's most significant entertainment and economic venues.

In winter the national park turns into a cross-country skiing and snowshoeing destination, the boreal forest going deep and quiet under heavy snowfall. Summer is for paddling — canoe routes fan out from Waskesiu Lake into the backcountry, leading eventually to the kind of country where moose stand in the shallows at dawn and white pelicans wheel overhead in the afternoon thermals.
