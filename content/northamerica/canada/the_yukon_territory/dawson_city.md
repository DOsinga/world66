Dawson City is the place where the Klondike Gold Rush still feels close. It sits where the Klondike River meets the Yukon, a long way north of Whitehorse, with wooden boardwalks, false-front buildings, gravel streets, old mining stories, and a summer rush of visitors who have come looking for the far-north frontier.

The town boomed in 1898, when prospectors, traders, saloon keepers, writers, and opportunists poured into the Klondike. The gold rush was short, but Dawson kept enough of its shape to make the past readable. The [Historic Klondike Gold Rush Town](/northamerica/canada/the_yukon_territory/dawson_city/historic_klondike_gold_rush_town) covers restored Parks Canada buildings, the [Dawson City Museum](/northamerica/canada/the_yukon_territory/dawson_city/dawson_city_museum) fills in the mining and town history, and [Dredge No. 4](/northamerica/canada/the_yukon_territory/dawson_city/dredge_no_4) shows how industrial the goldfields became after the stampede.

Dawson is not only settler nostalgia. The Tr'ondek Hwech'in have lived in this country for far longer than the gold seekers, and the [Danoja Zho Cultural Centre](/northamerica/canada/the_yukon_territory/dawson_city/danoja_zho_cultural_centre) is an important stop for understanding that side of the Klondike. The wider Tr'ondek-Klondike cultural landscape was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023.

Allow at least a full day if you are already in town, and longer if you want Bonanza Creek, river walks, galleries, or the climb or drive to [Midnight Dome](/northamerica/canada/the_yukon_territory/dawson_city/midnight_dome). Dawson is remote, seasonal, and rough around the edges, which is part of the point.
