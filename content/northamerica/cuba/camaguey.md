Camagüey has the most confusing street plan in Cuba, and the story goes that it is deliberate: after Henry Morgan sacked the town in 1668, it was rebuilt as a warren of curving alleys, blind corners and irregular squares so that raiders coming up from the coast would lose themselves in it. Whether or not that is the real reason — the alternative explanation is that the streets simply followed cattle tracks — the effect is the same, and the historic centre became a UNESCO site in 2008 largely because of it.

The other thing you notice is the **tinajones**: enormous earthenware jars, some of them man-sized, standing in courtyards all over the city. They were made here from the seventeenth century to catch rainwater in a place with no reliable river, and there are said to be more than fifteen thousand left.

This is Cuba's third largest city and a working provincial capital rather than a museum, with a serious cultural life — the Ballet de Camagüey is the country's second company, and whatever is on at the Teatro Principal is usually worth the ticket. **Plaza San Juan de Dios** is the best-preserved square, with its eighteenth-century hospital, and the Casino Campestre is the largest urban park in the country.

It sits on the central highway and the rail line halfway between Havana and [Santiago](/northamerica/cuba/santiagodecuba), which makes it the natural break on the long journey east.
