Guamá is a tourist complex built in the late 1960s on a string of small islands in the Laguna del Tesoro — "Treasure Lake" — inside the Zapata wetlands, the largest mangrove and swamp ecosystem in the Caribbean. The lake is reached by a 20-minute boat from Boca de Guamá at the head of the inlet, where the road from the Autopista Nacional ends and the crocodile farm sits at the trailhead.

The place is two things at once. It is a Taíno village reconstruction — 32 life-size bronze statues by the sculptor Rita Longa scattered through the cabins and along the wooden walkways, depicting pre-Columbian villagers fishing, weaving and grinding cassava. And it is a small lakeside resort with cabin accommodation, a restaurant, and trails through the swamp forest. The combination is strange, slightly faded, and unlike anywhere else in Cuba.

The wider Zapata wetlands are the real draw for naturalists. The peninsula holds the [Zapata Swamp National Park](/northamerica/cuba/guama/zapata_swamp), a Ramsar site supporting Cuban crocodiles, manatees, the endemic Zapata wren and Zapata sparrow, and hundreds of migrating bird species. The [crocodile breeding farm](/northamerica/cuba/guama/crocodile_farm) at Boca de Guamá runs a serious conservation programme for the critically endangered Cuban crocodile, found in the wild only here. Many visitors combine a day at Guamá with the nearby [Bay of Pigs](/northamerica/cuba/guama/bay_of_pigs) beaches at Playa Larga and Playa Girón, where good shore-diving and cenotes round out a weekend.
