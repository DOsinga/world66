  Dominican amber is scientifically unusual because it comes from the resin of an extinct
  species of Hymenaea tree that flourished here between 15 and 40 million years ago —
  significantly older than most Baltic amber. The specimens here have preserved mosquitoes,
  ants, spiders, and even small lizards in extraordinary anatomical detail. In 1993, the
  amber deposits of the Cordillera Septentrional directly behind Puerto Plata became
  internationally famous when Michael Crichton used them as the fictional source of
  dinosaur DNA in Jurassic Park.

The Dominican Republic holds some of the largest and most biologically rich amber deposits in the world, and this museum in a restored Victorian mansion near the central park is the best place to understand why that matters. The collection runs from polished commercial-grade pieces — the amber market on the ground floor sells these — up to scientific specimens containing insects, spiders, plant fragments, and small animals preserved in detail that no taxidermist could match. Some specimens are tens of millions of years old, older than most European amber deposits.

The building itself is worth the visit: a late 19th-century wooden house with high ceilings, shuttered windows, and the kind of faded grandeur that Puerto Plata's tobacco-boom architecture does well. Upstairs, the more significant specimens are displayed in cases with magnifying glasses and explanatory panels. A guide is usually available and can point out which pieces are exceptional — the untrained eye tends to miss the difference between a common ant inclusion and something genuinely rare. Allow an hour, and factor in time to browse the amber market downstairs if you want to buy. The prices here are not necessarily lower than the street market by the park, but the quality control is better.
