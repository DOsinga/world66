For most of the civil war Perquín, high in the Morazán hills near the Honduran border, was held by the FMLN — the guerrillas' de facto capital, with their clandestine transmitter Radio Venceremos broadcasting from the ridges around it. The **Museo de la Revolución Salvadoreña**, run by former combatants, is the reason to come: the transmitter itself, a wall of photographs, weapons, the wreckage of a helicopter shot down with the colonel who commanded the army's operations in the region aboard, and the craters left by the bombing.

Twelve kilometres south is **El Mozote**, where in December 1981 the Atlacatl Battalion killed around a thousand villagers, most of them children, in the worst massacre of the war. A memorial garden and a wall of names stand in the village, and guides from the community — some of them relatives — take visitors through what happened. It is not a light morning, and it is the single most important thing to see in the country.

The town itself is cool, pine-covered and a long way from anywhere: five hours from San Salvador, with the last stretch winding. People come for a night, walk in the hills, and go on to Honduras or back the way they came.
