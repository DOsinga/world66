Qaqortoq — formerly known by its Danish name Julianehåb — is the largest town in southern Greenland, with a population of around 3,000. It sits at the head of a deep fjord surrounded by hills of dark rock and patches of summer green, and its brightly painted houses clustered around the harbour give it a warmth that surprises visitors expecting a bleaker Arctic settlement.

Several old buildings sit around the [central square](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/mindebronden), which is anchored by the Mindebrønden, the oldest fountain in Greenland. The Cooper’s Shop dates from 1781. The [Qaqortoq Museum](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/qaqortoq_museum) displays Inuit utensils and Viking artifacts, and behind it stands an exact replica of a turf house from the early 1900s. The [town tannery](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/qaqortoq_tannery) processes skins and furs and sells high-quality sealskin coats and hats worldwide; tours can be arranged. The [Church of Our Saviour](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/church_of_our_saviour) is the handsome red wooden church on the hill above town.

A remarkable open-air project called [Stone and Man](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/stone_and_man) has turned the town’s rock faces into a sculpture gallery, with over thirty carvings made directly into the bedrock since 1993.

One popular excursion from Qaqortoq is the boat trip to [Hvalsey Church](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/hvalsey_church), the most famous and best-preserved Viking ruin in Greenland — the site of the last recorded event in Norse Greenlandic history. On the island of [Uunartoq](/northamerica/greenland/qaqortoq/uunartoq_hot_springs), hot-spring pools at around 35°C are warm enough for bathing year-round. Sitting in the water while icebergs drift past the inlet is one of those only-in-Greenland experiences. Qaqortoq is 300 mi/485 km southeast of [Nuuk](/northamerica/greenland/nuuk).
