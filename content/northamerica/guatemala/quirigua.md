Quiriguá is a modest site with one extraordinary thing about it: the stelae. The sandstone here splits into blocks that can be raised to ten metres and carved without shattering, and the rulers of the eighth century used them to record themselves at a scale nobody else in the Maya world managed. Stela E stands over ten metres above ground with several more buried below — the tallest carved stone monument in the Americas.

The carving is deep, elaborate and in unusually good condition, protected by the shade of the ceiba trees left standing across the plaza. Alongside the stelae are the zoomorphs: boulders worked into crouching mythological animals with rulers seated in their jaws, which have no real parallel anywhere else.

The site's moment came in 738, when its ruler K'ak' Tiliw Chan Yopaat captured and beheaded the king of [Copán](/northamerica/honduras/copan), the much larger city downstream that had dominated it. The monuments that follow are a small city celebrating loudly, for about a century, before the whole valley went quiet.

It sits in the banana plantations off the Atlantic highway, a few kilometres down a side road from the village of Los Amates — easy enough as a stop between Guatemala City and Río Dulce, and quiet on any day of the week.
