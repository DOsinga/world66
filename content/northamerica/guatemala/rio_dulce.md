Between [Lake Izabal](/northamerica/guatemala/lakeizabal) and the Caribbean the water runs through a limestone gorge with two-hundred-metre walls, hung with vines and full of herons. Boats make the run down to [Livingston](/northamerica/guatemala/livingston) in a couple of hours, and it is one of the great small journeys in Central America — worth doing slowly, in a lancha that will stop at the hot spring on the bank and the bird islands.

The town at the top end, officially Fronteras but universally called Río Dulce, is a scruffy bridge-and-boatyard place rather than a destination. It exists because the highway crosses here and because the river is a hurricane hole: yachts crossing the Caribbean sit out the season in the marinas upstream, which gives the town an odd mix of Guatemalan truck stop and international sailing community.

The Castillo de San Felipe guards the lake entrance a few kilometres west — a small squat Spanish fort, rebuilt more than once, put there in 1652 to keep pirates out of Izabal. You can walk its walls in fifteen minutes and swim off the point afterwards.

Boats leave when they fill rather than on a timetable. Turning up in the morning is the way to travel; the afternoon runs thin out.
