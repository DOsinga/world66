San Ignacio appears out of nothing. The road from Guerrero Negro runs through several hundred kilometres of volcanic desert and then drops into a basin of several thousand date palms planted by Jesuit missionaries in the eighteenth century, with a lagoon in the middle of it.

The **mission church**, finished in 1786, has walls more than a metre thick of lava block and is the best preserved on the peninsula. The village square in front of it is shaded by laurels and is, by some distance, the most pleasant place to sit in central Baja.

**Laguna San Ignacio**, an hour south-west on a rough road, is the reason most people come. Grey whales migrate here from the Bering Sea to calve between January and April, and this lagoon — the least developed of the three — is where the mothers routinely bring their calves alongside the small boats to be touched. It is a UNESCO site and the number of boats is strictly limited.

Inland, the **Sierra de San Francisco** holds the finest rock art in Mexico: human and animal figures painted on cave walls up to ten metres across, some of them several thousand years old. Reaching them means a guided trip by mule, arranged in the village, of between one and several days.
