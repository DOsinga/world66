Cobá was one of the great Maya cities of the Classic period, home to perhaps 50,000 people at its 9th-century peak and spread across some 70 square kilometres of low jungle 40 km northwest of Tulum. What survives today is only a fraction of the original — most of the city remains unexcavated, swallowed by forest — but the scale is unmistakable. The site's defining feature is its network of *sacbeob*, the raised white limestone causeways that radiated out from the centre and once linked Cobá to settlements as far as 100 km away.

The headline structure is **Nohoch Mul**, at 42 metres the tallest Maya pyramid in the Yucatán Peninsula. Climbing was permitted until 2020 but is now forbidden to protect the stonework. The other groupings — the Cobá Group around the lake, the painted-temple cluster at Las Pinturas, and the ball court — are scattered far apart, so most visitors rent a bicycle or hire a pedal-rickshaw at the gate to cover the distances. The forest setting is humid and largely shaded, alive with birdsong and the rustle of iguanas in the leaf litter.

Cobá is reliably less crowded than Chichén Itzá or the Tulum ruins, and the jungle atmosphere makes it the more rewarding of the three for many travellers. Go early in the day, both to beat the heat and to have the quieter outlying groups largely to yourself. Combine the visit with a cooling swim at one of the cenotes just outside the village — Choo-Ha, Tamcach-Ha, or Multun-Ha — which are clustered together a few minutes' drive south of the site entrance.

From Cancun, Cobá is about a two-and-a-half-hour drive via Tulum, making it a long but feasible full-day trip; it pairs naturally with the Tulum ruins or a Riviera Maya cenote. Bring water, insect repellent, and good shoes, and allow at least two to three hours on site.
