Palenque sits in the foothills of the Sierra Madre de Chiapas, where the highlands drop towards the Gulf Coast lowlands and the jungle gets thick and wet. Most travellers come for the ruins — the ancient Maya city of Palenque, set into a forested hillside at the edge of the modern town, is one of the finest archaeological sites in the Americas.

The modern town of Palenque itself is a small, functional place: a main square, a market, a cluster of hotels and restaurants aimed at the steady stream of visitors. It is not especially beautiful, and nobody comes for the town. But it is a convenient base, and the evening light on the zócalo, with the sound of birds returning to roost in the surrounding trees, has its own modest charm.

The ruins — officially the [Palenque National Park](/northamerica/mexico/palenque/palenque_ruins) — occupy a hilltop covered in jungle about 8 kilometres from the town centre. Only a fraction of the ancient city has been excavated; the rest lies under the trees. What you can see is extraordinary. The [Temple of the Inscriptions](/northamerica/mexico/palenque/temple_of_inscriptions) houses the intact tomb of the 7th-century ruler Pakal the Great, discovered in 1952 — the first pyramid tomb found in the Americas. The Palace, the Temple of the Sun, and the carved sarcophagus lid are among the most refined works of Maya art anywhere.

Beyond the ruins, the area has a few day trips worth taking: the [Misol-Há waterfall](/northamerica/mexico/palenque/misolha) is 20 kilometres down the road towards San Cristóbal, and the blue limestone pools at Agua Azul are 45 kilometres further. Both make easy half-day excursions. The region is also a gateway to the more remote sites at Bonampak (with the finest surviving Maya murals) and Yaxchilán, reached by lancha along the Usumacinta River.
