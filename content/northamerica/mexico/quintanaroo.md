Quintana Roo is where most visitors to Mexico land, and a great many never leave it. The state runs down the Caribbean side of the Yucatán peninsula from [Cancún](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/cancun) to the Belize border, and it holds the country's warmest sea, its second reef, and almost all of its all-inclusive hotels.

The coast divides sharply. **Cancún** is a planned resort city, built from nothing on a sandbar in the 1970s, and it works exactly as intended. **[Playa del Carmen](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/playadelcarmen)** is denser and more walkable; **[Tulum](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/tulum)** has the cliff-top ruins and a beach road of expensive hotels behind it. The islands are calmer — **[Isla Mujeres](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/islamujeres)** for a day, **[Cozumel](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/cozumel)** for the diving, **[Holbox](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/holbox)** for sand streets and whale sharks in summer.

Behind the beach the limestone is riddled with **cenotes** — collapsed sinkholes into the freshwater table — which give this coast the best cavern diving and snorkelling in the Americas.

South of Tulum the development thins out fast. The **Sian Ka'an** biosphere reserve blocks the coast road, and beyond it [Mahahual](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/mahahual), [Xcalak](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/xcalak) and [Bacalar](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/bacalar) — the last on a lagoon of improbable blues — are what the Riviera Maya looked like thirty years ago. [Chetumal](/northamerica/mexico/quintanaroo/chetumal) on the border is a working city with an excellent Maya museum and no tourism to speak of.
