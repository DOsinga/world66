**The Tailor of Panama** — John le Carré. A wickedly funny spy novel set in late-1990s Panama City, just after the U.S. handover of the canal. The central character is a corrupt British expat tailor who feeds an MI6 agent invented intelligence about the canal's future. Le Carré captures the post-Noriega Panama of money laundering, bewildered diplomats, and a city torn between its old elite and its new offshore banking class.

**The Path Between the Seas** — David McCullough. The definitive English-language history of how the canal got built: the French disaster under Ferdinand de Lesseps, the U.S.-engineered Panamanian independence from Colombia, the engineers, the yellow fever, the Black West Indian workers who actually dug it. Long, but worth the time if you want to understand why the modern city looks the way it does.

**The Golden Land** — Eric Jay Dolin. A more recent narrative history of the conquistadors who first crossed the isthmus, including the founding and destruction of Panamá Viejo by Henry Morgan. Useful for context before walking the ruins.

**Empire's Crossroads** — Carrie Gibson. A wide-angle history of the Caribbean that gives the Panamanian isthmus its proper place as a hinge between two oceans. Good background for understanding why everything from pirates to bananas to drug money has flowed through this city.

**Getting to Know the General** — Graham Greene. Greene's memoir of his strange friendship with Panamanian leader Omar Torrijos in the late 1970s. Greene was an honoured guest during the canal handover negotiations; the book is short, gossipy, and a vivid portrait of a Panama on the cusp of independence from the United States.
