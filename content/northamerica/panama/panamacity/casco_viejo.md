The old colonial heart of Panama City, founded in 1673 after pirates burned the original settlement at Panamá Viejo. Casco Viejo (also called Casco Antiguo or San Felipe) is a compact grid of seven or eight blocks on a small peninsula jutting into the Pacific, fortified on the seaward side by stone bastions that still survive. UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site in 1997.

The architecture is a layered jumble of Spanish colonial, French (from the canal-building era), Caribbean, and Republican-era Art Nouveau. Pastel townhouses with iron balconies sit next to crumbling brick shells; rooftop bars look down on plazas where children play. Twenty years ago much of it was a dangerous slum and many buildings were ruins; aggressive restoration since the late 1990s has turned it into the city's most desirable neighbourhood for hotels, restaurants, and bars, while still leaving enough decay for the place to feel alive rather than restored to death. Walk it at sunset, when the heat drops and the bay reflects the skyline of the new city across the water.

The main plazas to find your bearings by are **Plaza de la Independencia** (with the cathedral), **Plaza Bolívar**, **Plaza Herrera**, and **Plaza de Francia** at the southern tip.
