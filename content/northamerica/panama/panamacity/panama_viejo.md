The ruins of the original Panama City, founded in 1519 by the conquistador Pedro Arias Dávila and abandoned after Henry Morgan's raid in 1671. Panamá Viejo was the first Spanish settlement on the Pacific coast of the Americas, and for 150 years it was the staging post for the Spanish conquest of Peru: the Inca gold and silver was shipped here from Callao, then carried overland by mule train across the isthmus to Portobelo on the Caribbean side for transport to Spain.

What survives today is a UNESCO-listed archaeological park: the stone tower of the cathedral, fragments of the bishop's palace, a few city walls, and the foundations of various convents and monasteries, scattered across a grassy peninsula on the eastern edge of the modern city. There is a good site museum at the entrance and a viewing platform inside the cathedral tower. Allow two hours; come early in the morning before the heat builds. The contrast between the ruined stone walls and the glittering skyscrapers visible across the bay is the point.
