Christ Church is the most visited parish in Barbados, stretching along the island's south coast from the outskirts of Bridgetown to Grantley Adams International Airport. The calm, turquoise Caribbean Sea here is gentler than the Atlantic surf on the east coast, making it the island's natural choice for swimming, snorkelling, and watersports of every kind.

The parish divides neatly into two halves. The western end is the tourist heartland: the broad sandy beaches at Accra (Rockley) and Dover draw everyone from families on week-long packages to Bajans spending a Sunday in the sea. St. Lawrence Gap — a short, lively strip of restaurants, bars, and clubs — is the social hub, filling up from early evening and keeping going until the small hours. South of that, the coast quietens down and the beaches get wilder.

Oistins anchors the eastern end of the parish. It is a working fishing town first and a visitor attraction second, and that tension is precisely what makes it worth visiting. On Friday and Saturday evenings the beachside fish market transforms into the Oistins Fish Fry — dozens of stalls selling fresh mahi-mahi, snapper, and flying fish served with macaroni pie and rum punch, soundtracked by soca and reggae. It is the most honest introduction to Bajan food culture on the island.

Between the beach strips and the fish fry, Christ Church holds a few surprises: the Newton Slave Burial Ground, a sobering heritage site that is among the most significant of its kind in the Caribbean; the Graeme Hall Nature Sanctuary, a Ramsar-listed wetland where herons and egrets stalk the mangroves; and the Church of the Chase Vault, whose story of mysteriously moving coffins made it one of the 19th century's most famous unsolved mysteries.
