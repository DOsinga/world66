  A Cuban cigar factory employs a "lector" — a reader who sits on a raised
  platform and reads aloud to the rollers for hours each day: newspapers,
  novels, political texts. The practice began in the 1860s and continues today.
  The rollers traditionally got to choose what was read, which meant that
  19th-century cigar workers were among the best-informed labourers in Cuba,
  debating the novels of Zola and the politics of independence during their
  rolling breaks. Some factory names — Romeo y Julieta, Montecristo — came
  directly from the books being read aloud.

The Partagás factory, founded in 1845 and operating from its current building on Industria Street behind the Capitol since 1900, is one of Havana's oldest and most famous cigar manufacturers. The building is a handsome neoclassical structure with a colonnaded facade; the factory floor is where the real interest lies.

Tours of the factory are available — they take you through the stripping, sorting, and rolling floors, where workers produce cigars by hand using techniques unchanged for a century. The sight of skilled rollers working with practiced economy, the smell of curing tobacco, and the sound of the lector are collectively unlike anything else in the city. Tours last around an hour; book in advance as places are limited. There is a shop on site selling the full Partagás range at official prices.
