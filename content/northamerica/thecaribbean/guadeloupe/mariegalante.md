Marie-Galante is a round, flat island about 25 km south of Guadeloupe's main island, reachable by ferry in roughly an hour from Pointe-à-Pitre. It is quieter and more rural than Guadeloupe proper — a place of sugarcane fields, ox carts still used on some farms, and beaches that haven't been packaged for mass tourism. The islanders call it "the island of a hundred windmills," a reference to the sugar mills that once dominated the landscape; some forty stone towers still stand scattered across the interior, remnants of a sugar economy that shaped everything about the island.

The three main towns each have a distinct character. Grand-Bourg, on the south-west coast, is the capital and the livelier of the three, with a proper market, a seafront square, and most of the island's services. Saint-Louis, to the north-west, is a quieter fishing town with easy access to the best beaches. Capesterre, on the south-east coast, sits near the island's second distillery and is the most traditional of the three.

The beaches are the draw. Anse de la Feuillère, on the north-west coast, is a kilometre of white sand backed by sea grapes — calm, clear, and rarely crowded. The beaches around Saint-Louis, including Anse Canot and Anse Moustique, are popular with islanders at weekends. The water here is warmer and calmer than the Atlantic-facing beaches of Guadeloupe, with coral close to shore in many spots.

Rum is serious business on Marie-Galante. Three distilleries still operate on the island — Bellevue, Bielle, and Capesterre (also called Poisson) — and each produces a distinct agricole rum from fresh-pressed sugarcane juice. All three welcome visitors, offer tours of the distillery, and give generous tastings at the end. The rum from Marie-Galante has a reputation for being among the finest in the French Caribbean; buy a bottle at the distillery to take home.

The pace is slow, the roads quiet, and the population around 12,000. Plan to stay at least two nights — a day trip from Guadeloupe is possible but doesn't do the island justice. Creole is still the primary language in everyday life, and traditional culture — music, food, crafts — feels more intact here than on Guadeloupe itself.
