Saint-Pierre was once the cultural capital of the Caribbean — the "Paris of the Antilles," a prosperous colonial city of 30,000 people at the foot of Mount Pelée. On 8 May 1902, the volcano erupted with a superheated pyroclastic surge that killed virtually the entire population in minutes. The ruins have never been rebuilt. Walking through the overgrown streets and crumbled facades, you are essentially walking through a frozen moment in 1902.

Today Saint-Pierre is a small town rebuilt among and beside the ruins. The [Musée Vulcanologique Frank Perret](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/musee_vulcanologique) displays artifacts recovered from the disaster — clocks stopped at 8am, fused glass, a church bell melted out of shape. The [ruins of the theatre](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/ruins_of_the_theatre), the [cathedral](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/cathedral_ruins), and the [military hospital](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/ruins_of_the_hospital) are woven into the fabric of the living town. The [dungeon where Auguste Cyparis survived](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/cyparis_dungeon) — the only known survivor of the initial blast — is one of the most affecting small sites you will find anywhere.

Offshore, the harbour holds at least ten [ships sunk by the 1902 eruption](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/wreck_diving). Divers rank this among the best wreck diving in the world: intact hulls and cargo at 15–50 metres, encrusted with decades of coral growth, in clear Caribbean water. If you dive, this is the reason to come. The [Depaz Distillery](/northamerica/thecaribbean/martinique/saintpierre/le_figuier_rum_distillery), on the lower slopes of Mont Pelée just above town, makes some of Martinique's finest agricole rum and has its own haunted history: the founding family died in the eruption, and Victor Depaz built the distillery on the same ground the pyroclastic flow had crossed.
