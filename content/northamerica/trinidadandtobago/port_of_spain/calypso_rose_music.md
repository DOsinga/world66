  Calypso has functioned as a form of political commentary since the 19th century, when calypsonian singers (called "kaisonians") used the music to critique governors, plantation owners, and colonial authorities who could not easily prosecute them for the opinions expressed in song. The "Calypso Wars" of the 1930s and 1940s — competitions between singers at Carnival time — were simultaneously musical contests and political debates. Lord Kitchener, Mighty Sparrow, and David Rudder are the canonical figures; their recordings are the best introduction to the tradition.

Calypso is the original music of Trinidad Carnival, developed by Afro-Trinidadian musicians over two centuries as a vehicle for social commentary, satire, and storytelling. The Calypso Monarch competition during Carnival week is a serious, intensely contested event where singers are judged on both the quality of their vocal performance and the sharpness of the social commentary in their lyrics.

Soca — "soul calypso," developed in the 1970s by Lord Shorty as a more rhythmically driven, energetic variant — has largely taken over Carnival's party music function, while traditional calypso remains a separate competition tradition. Live calypso and soca can be heard year-round at various venues, but the season is concentrated in the weeks before Carnival. The NAPA concert hall and various bars and clubs host calypso and soca performances; check local listings during your visit.
