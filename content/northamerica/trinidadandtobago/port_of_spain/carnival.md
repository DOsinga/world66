  The steel pan was invented specifically for Carnival — and then banned from it. In 1934 and 1935, colonial authorities prohibited the pan bands from participating in Carnival marches, fearing violence between rival groups. The ban lasted until after World War II, when returning Trinidadian servicemen, many of whom had served in the British forces, pressed successfully for the reinstatement of the bands. The first post-ban Panorama competition was held in 1963, the year after independence.

Trinidad Carnival is the oldest and, many argue, the greatest Carnival in the world. It takes place in the two days before Ash Wednesday — usually February — and transforms Port of Spain into something between a massive street party and a competitive performance festival. The pre-Lenten tradition was brought to Trinidad by French planters in the 1780s; after emancipation in 1834, freed Africans joined and eventually took over the festival, bringing their own music, costumes, and rituals.

The festival season begins weeks before the main days with fetes, parties, and J'ouvert — the mud-and-paint street party that starts at 2am on Carnival Monday and ends at dawn. The main event is the parade of the bands on Monday and Tuesday, when masqueraders in elaborate costumes (mas) move through the city streets in groups of hundreds or thousands. Thousands more watch from the grandstands at the Queen's Park Savannah. The musical competitions — Calypso Monarch, Soca Monarch, Panorama (steelband), and the Carnival King and Queen pageants — are serious, contested events watched by large audiences.

If you want to experience Carnival properly, register with a band months in advance, buy a costume, and join the parade. Just watching from the street is also worthwhile, but participating is transformative.
