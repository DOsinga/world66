story: >
  The Savannah was originally part of the Peschier Estate, a sugar plantation that occupied most of what is now uptown Port of Spain. The city purchased it in 1817, and for over a century it served as a racecourse, a cricket ground, and an open common. During Carnival the Savannah is the main competition stage — the massive grandstands hold tens of thousands of spectators watching the parade of the bands, the Calypso Monarch competition, and the crowning of Carnival King and Queen.

The Savannah is a 32-hectare oval green space at the north end of the city — one of the largest roundabouts in the world, technically, though nobody calls it that. It is the true heart of Port of Spain's public life: joggers and walkers circle its 3.7-kilometre perimeter at dawn, cricketers take over the grass on weekends, and in the evenings a row of food stalls along the southern edge sell corn soup, oysters, doubles, and local snacks to a steady crowd.

The northern edge of the Savannah is framed by the **Magnificent Seven** — seven grand colonial-era mansions built in the early twentieth century, ranging from a Scottish baronial castle to a Baroque French chateau to a Moorish-inspired confection. They are strange and extravagant and wonderful, a physical record of the competition among Trinidad's wealthy elite to out-eccentric each other in brick and stone. Several are now government buildings, but their exteriors are freely visible and worth a slow walk.

The Emperor Valley Zoo and the Royal Botanic Gardens are on the Savannah's northern edge. The National Academy for the Performing Arts (NAPA), a striking contemporary building, occupies the northeast corner.
