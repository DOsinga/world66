  The Red House got its name by accident. In 1897, the city painted the buildings red to celebrate Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee, and the name stuck before the paint had time to fade. The original building was burned to the ground six years later during the Water Riots of 1903, when a protest over a new water rate law turned violent. The rebuilt Red House — completed in 1907 in Beaux-Arts style — has been the seat of Trinidad's parliament ever since, and a further violent siege occurred in 1990 when a group of Islamist militants took the prime minister and dozens of MPs hostage for six days.

The Red House is the seat of Parliament of Trinidad and Tobago, and one of the most architecturally distinctive government buildings in the Caribbean. The current building, completed in 1907, was designed in Beaux-Arts style with a prominent red facade — not coincidentally, since the colour had already given the building its popular name. It stands at the corner of Abercromby Street and Knox Street, facing Woodford Square.

The building can be visited when the Parliament is not in session; guided tours are available. The debating chambers are modelled loosely on Westminster, though the atmosphere is distinctly Trinidadian. The building was closed for an extensive restoration that was completed in recent years, and the facade looks better than it has in decades.
