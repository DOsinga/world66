Valdez sits at the head of a deep fjord in Prince William Sound, boxed in by the Chugach Mountains and approached through one of Alaska's most dramatic road corridors. It is a working port, the southern terminus of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline, and a town whose modern story is marked by the 1964 earthquake and the 1989 Exxon Valdez oil spill.

The landscape is the real reason to come. [Columbia Glacier](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/valdez/columbia_glacier) is reached by boat through iceberg-filled water, while [Worthington Glacier](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/valdez/worthington_glacier) is one of Alaska's easiest road-access glaciers. The drive in through [Keystone Canyon](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/valdez/keystone_canyon) passes sheer walls, waterfalls, and the old route toward the Interior.

Valdez works best if you give it a full day rather than treating it as the end of the highway. Take a glacier or wildlife cruise, stop properly in the canyon, and leave time for the [Valdez Museum](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/valdez/valdez_museum), where the town's gold rush, pipeline, earthquake, and oil-spill history come together.

Compared with [Seward](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/seward), Valdez feels more remote and less polished. That is part of the appeal: the harbour still belongs to fishing boats, snow piles high in winter, and the mountains press close enough to make the town feel borrowed from the weather.
