Wrangell is one of Alaska's oldest and least-visited towns, sitting on the northern tip of Wrangell Island in the Inside Passage. It is the only place in Alaska to have flown three flags — Russian, British, and American — and it was built on land the Tlingit people had occupied for centuries before any of them arrived. The [Stikine River](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/wrangell/stikine_river), one of the fastest-flowing navigable rivers in North America, empties into the sea nearby and provides access deep into the wilderness interior of British Columbia.

The town's greatest asset is what it lacks. Wrangell gets far fewer cruise ships than Ketchikan or Juneau, which means you encounter genuine community life rather than a performance for tourists. [Petroglyph Beach](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/wrangell/petroglyph_beach), a short walk from the ferry terminal, is scattered with ancient rock carvings you can examine freely at low tide — no fence, no fee, no crowd. [Chief Shakes Island](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/wrangell/chief_shakes_historic_site), a small islet in the harbour, holds a reconstructed Tlingit clan house with carved posts from the 1790s. The [Nolan Center Museum](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/wrangell/nolan_center) tells the full layered history of the place.

For wildlife, the [Anan Creek Wildlife Observatory](/northamerica/unitedstates/alaska/wrangell/anan_wildlife_observatory) is among the best bear-watching sites in Alaska — accessible by boat or floatplane and less crowded with visitors than the more famous McNeil River or Katmai sites. Black bears and brown bears fish for pink salmon from late June through August while bald eagles and harbour seals work the margins. Because Wrangell is small, permits are more readily available than at the big-name sites.
