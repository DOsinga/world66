  At its peak in the 1870s and 1880s, Folsom had one of the largest Chinese communities in the Gold Country, many of whom worked in the granite quarry at the state prison. When they died, many could not be buried in the main city cemetery due to racial exclusion laws. The Chung Wah Benevolent Association established this separate ground, and it remains one of the few intact Chinese cemeteries from the Gold Rush era still accessible in the Sierra foothills.

The Chung Wah Cemetery, tucked into a quiet corner of old Folsom near the American River, is the burial ground of the Chinese immigrants who came to the Gold Country during and after the 1849 rush. Many worked in the mines, on the railroads, and — in Folsom specifically — as forced labourers in the granite quarrying operation at the state prison.

The cemetery is small and easy to miss but worth finding. A number of the original grave markers survive, and the site offers a corrective to the standard Gold Rush narrative: this was a multicultural rush, and the Chinese community paid some of its highest costs in places exactly like this. The cemetery is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. It sits off Natoma Street, close to the American River.
