Nearly half of Sanibel Island is protected inside this 6,300-acre wildlife refuge, named for the Pulitzer Prize-winning cartoonist and conservationist J.N. Darling who campaigned for its protection in the 1940s. The refuge is one of the most visited in the National Wildlife Refuge system, and justifiably so — the birdwatching is exceptional. Roseate spoonbills, great blue herons, anhingas, white ibis, and dozens of shorebird species feed through the mangrove-edged tidal flats visible from the five-mile Wildlife Drive. Alligators are common along the water's edge.

Wildlife Drive is open to cars, bicycles, and pedestrians during daylight hours (closed Fridays). The best wildlife viewing is in the two hours after sunrise and before sunset, when activity peaks and the light is good for photography. Kayak and canoe launch points along the refuge give access to the interior mangrove channels, where the stillness and the bird calls can be striking. The visitor center on Refuge Road has a small natural history exhibit and knowledgeable staff who can tell you what's been spotted that week. Pistol shrimp snap their claws in the shallows at Ding Darling, producing pressure bubbles that reach temperatures hotter than the sun's surface — the refuge hosts one of the best-studied populations.
