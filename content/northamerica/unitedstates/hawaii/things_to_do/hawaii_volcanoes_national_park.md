Kilauea is one of the most continuously active volcanoes on earth, and you can walk right up to the evidence. The park covers 333,000 acres of the Big Island, from the summit calderas down through rainforest and lava fields to the coast. The Kilauea Overlook and the Jaggar Museum viewpoint offer views into the smoldering Halemaumau Crater. The Chain of Craters Road drops 3,700 feet to the coast, passing lava flows that buried the old road in the 1980s and 90s. At the end, where lava meets the sea, the coastline looks like the earth is still being made — because it is.

Bring water, wear sturdy shoes, and check the park's volcanic activity updates before you go. Summit areas can be hazardous when volcanic emissions are high.

story: >
  In 2018, a months-long eruption in the lower East Rift Zone destroyed more than 700 homes in the Leilani Estates neighbourhood and added 875 acres of new land to the Big Island's coastline. The eruption completely drained Halemaʻumaʻu Crater, which had held an active lava lake since 2008. By 2021 the lava lake had reformed.
