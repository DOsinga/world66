title: 'Books'

---

**The Devil in the White City** by Erik Larson (2003) tells two parallel stories from the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition: the architect Daniel Burnham racing to build the "White City" fairgrounds, and the serial killer H.H. Holmes operating a murder hotel a few blocks away. It is the best entry point into Gilded Age Chicago and makes the lakefront landscape feel layered with history.

**Native Son** by Richard Wright (1940) is set in the Black Belt of Chicago's South Side and remains one of the most powerful American novels about race, poverty, and urban constraint. Wright drew on the same streets and tenement conditions documented by sociologists at the University of Chicago. Essential for understanding the city's divided geography.

**The Jungle** by Upton Sinclair (1906) follows Lithuanian immigrants in the meatpacking yards of Packingtown on the South Side. Sinclair intended to expose the exploitation of immigrant workers; what shocked the public instead was the graphic description of what went into the meat. The novel prompted the Pure Food and Drug Act within months of publication. Much of the physical landscape is gone, but the Back of the Yards neighbourhood survives.

**Adventures of Augie March** by Saul Bellow (1953) opens with one of the great first sentences in American fiction and follows a Jewish kid from Humboldt Park through Depression-era Chicago and beyond. Bellow grew up in the city and the novel is saturated with specific North Side geography -- streets, neighbourhoods, and the particular social texture of immigrant Chicago between the wars.

**Never a City So Real** by Alex Kotlowitz (2004) is a collection of portraits of Chicagoans -- a union organizer, a judge, a gang intervention worker -- that adds up to a vivid and compassionate account of what makes the city work and what tears it apart. Kotlowitz also wrote *There Are No Children Here*, about two boys growing up in the Henry Horner housing projects, which is equally essential.
