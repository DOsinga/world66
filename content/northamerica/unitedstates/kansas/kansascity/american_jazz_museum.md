  Kansas City's jazz scene in the 1930s thrived partly because of the corrupt political machine of Boss Tom Pendergast, who kept the city wide open — the clubs never closed, the liquor kept flowing through Prohibition, and musicians could find work every night. Charlie Parker, Count Basie, and Big Joe Turner all came up in this environment. Pendergast went to prison in 1939; the scene never quite recovered.
---

The 18th and Vine district is where Kansas City's great jazz era happened, and the American Jazz Museum tells that story with real depth. The permanent exhibition covers the music's development from its African American roots through the Kansas City sound of the 1930s, with excellent audio-visual installations and personal artefacts from the major figures who played here.

The museum shares its building with the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum, and a combined ticket is the best value. Together they form one of the most significant cultural complexes dedicated to African American history in the country. Check the Blue Room jazz club next door for live performances on weekends.
