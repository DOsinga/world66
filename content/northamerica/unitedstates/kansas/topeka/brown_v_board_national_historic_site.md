story: "The case began when Oliver Brown tried to enrol his seven-year-old daughter Linda in Sumner Elementary, the all-white school five blocks from their home, rather than the Monroe Elementary for Black children 21 blocks away. When the NAACP consolidated several similar cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and Washington DC under the Brown name, and the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in 1954 that racially segregated schools were unconstitutional, this modest brick building in Topeka became one of the pivots of American history."
---

The former Monroe Elementary School, where Linda Brown was denied enrollment because she was Black, is now a national historic site commemorating the Supreme Court decision that ended legal school segregation. The exhibits are well-done and the building itself — with its small classrooms, narrow hallways, and period furnishings — makes the human scale of the story vivid. This is the most significant historical site in Topeka.

The permanent exhibition walks visitors through the history of school segregation in Kansas, the lives of the families involved in the case, and the legal battle that culminated in the 1954 ruling. The National Park Service has done careful work with the interpretation — the tone is honest about the violence and humiliation of the Jim Crow system without being didactic. Allow at least 90 minutes to do the exhibits justice. The site is free to enter.
