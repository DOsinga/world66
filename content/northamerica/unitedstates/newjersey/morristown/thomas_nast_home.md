  Thomas Nast invented many of the images Americans now take as given: Santa Claus in red, the Republican elephant, the Democratic donkey, and Uncle Sam in his top hat. He drew all of these in Morristown, at Villa Fontana on MacCulloch Avenue. His savage cartoons in Harper's Weekly also helped bring down Boss Tweed — Tweed reportedly offered Nast $500,000 to stop, and when refused, said "them damn pictures" were more dangerous than any article.

Villa Fontana on MacCulloch Avenue was the home of Thomas Nast, the 19th-century cartoonist who shaped American political imagery for generations. Nast lived here from 1872 until he left for Ecuador (where he died of yellow fever in 1902), and produced some of his most famous work in Morristown. The house is a private residence and not open for interior tours, but the exterior and its history make it worth the short detour from the Green.

Nast's legacy is considerable beyond his art: his attacks on the Tammany Hall corruption machine, conducted in cartoon form through the 1870s, were credited by Boss Tweed himself with being the biggest threat to his power. The nearby MacCulloch Hall Historical Museum has Nast materials in its collection, including original cartoons.
