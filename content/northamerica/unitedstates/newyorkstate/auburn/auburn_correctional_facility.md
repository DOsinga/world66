Auburn Correctional Facility, opened in 1817, is one of the oldest prisons in the United States still in operation. It is historically significant as the birthplace of the "Auburn System" of prison discipline — a regime of enforced silence, congregate labour, and strict routine that became the dominant model for American prisons throughout the 19th century and influenced prison design worldwide.

The prison is not open to public tours, but its grey stone walls and guard towers are visible from State Street and form a distinctive part of the city's streetscape. The facility's history is covered in Auburn's other historical institutions, particularly the Seward House Museum, which has material on 19th-century penal philosophy in which Auburn played a central role.

The prison was also the site of the world's first electrocution execution in 1890, an event that drew international attention and debate. The story of the electric chair's development — involving Thomas Edison, George Westinghouse, and the execution of William Kemmler — is one of the stranger chapters in American legal history, and Auburn's role in it is part of the city's legacy.
