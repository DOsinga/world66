Midtown Manhattan runs from 34th Street to 59th Street and contains the skyline New York is famous for: the Empire State Building, Rockefeller Center, the Chrysler Building, the United Nations, Grand Central Terminal, and the chaotic, neon-drenched spectacle of Times Square. It is the largest central business district in the United States, and by day it moves at a pressure that can feel overwhelming — densely packed sidewalks, constant horn noise, a sense that everyone is late for something important. The sights are concentrated enough that walking them is practical: the Empire State Building observatory (350 Fifth Avenue) is thirty blocks south of Central Park, and the two endpoints make a reasonable half-day route.

The cultural anchors are substantial. **MoMA** (11 West 53rd Street) holds one of the greatest collections of modern art in the world, from Monet's *Water Lilies* to Warhol's soup cans. **Rockefeller Center** is best experienced around Christmas but is worth seeing year-round for its Art Deco architecture, the sunken ice rink plaza and the views from the Top of the Rock observation deck. **Grand Central Terminal** on 42nd Street was nearly demolished in the 1960s before a landmark preservation campaign saved it; its main concourse, with its vaulted ceiling painted with a celestial map, is one of the great interior spaces in the country. Times Square itself is a genuine spectacle of American commercial culture — best seen at night, ideally from the steps of the TKTS booth on the southern end of the plaza.
