**The Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass** by Frederick Douglass (1845). Douglass escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 and eventually settled in Rochester, where he published his abolitionist newspaper *The North Star* for 16 years. His autobiography — written in clear, controlled, devastating prose — is one of the foundational texts of American literature, and reading it before visiting Mount Hope Cemetery, where he is buried, gives the place a different gravity.

**Kodak and the Lens of Nostalgia** by Nancy Martha West (2000). An academic but readable study of how Kodak's marketing invented the modern idea of the snapshot and the "Kodak moment" — the sense that life should be photographed and preserved. Understanding the argument helps explain why the George Eastman Museum is more than just a collection of cameras.

**Sister Carrie** by Theodore Dreiser (1900). Not set in Rochester, but the novel's protagonist passes through upstate New York on her way from small-town Wisconsin to Chicago and New York, and Dreiser's portrait of American industrial ambition and urban restlessness in the 1890s illuminates exactly the world that Kodak and Bausch + Lomb were building in Rochester at the same moment.

**The Women's Hour: The Great Fight to Win the Vote** by Elaine Weiss (2018). The final push for the 19th Amendment, culminating in the 1920 Tennessee ratification vote. Rochester was central to the suffrage movement — Susan B. Anthony spent most of her life there — and this book puts her legacy in the context of a national political struggle that went down to the wire.
