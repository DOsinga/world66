---
title: "Fort Buford State Historic Site"
type: poi
---

A few kilometres west of Fort Union, on the North Dakota side of the Missouri, Fort Buford was a US Army post established in 1866 — one of a string of forts built to protect overland routes and, eventually, to enforce the confinement of the Plains peoples to reservations. It is remembered above all as the place where Sitting Bull and his band of Hunkpapa Lakota surrendered on 19 July 1881, after four years of exile in Canada following the Battle of the Little Bighorn. The original adjutant's office still stands, now serving as the visitor centre and museum.

The site is small and quiet, which suits it well. The museum holds the original document of Sitting Bull's surrender, a collection of period weapons and uniforms, and exhibits that treat the events of 1881 with appropriate complexity — neither celebrating the surrender as a victory nor papering over what it meant for the people involved. An interpretive trail leads through the remains of the fort's foundations to the military cemetery, where soldiers, civilians, and Native Americans are buried in the same ground.

Fort Buford is managed by the State Historical Society of North Dakota and charges a modest admission fee. It is open from May through September. Combined with a visit to Fort Union (the sites are linked by a short scenic drive), it makes for a full and worthwhile half-day.
