story: >
  Oregon was the first state on the Pacific Coast to pass Japanese exclusion laws, beginning in 1923. After Pearl Harbor, Executive Order 9066 in February 1942 required all people of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast to report for incarceration — including Oregon's 4,000 Japanese Americans, most of whom were US citizens. The plaza, opened in 1990, was the result of decades of campaigning by the Japanese American community and was designed in collaboration with survivors of the incarceration.
---

The Japanese American Historical Plaza sits at the north end of Tom McCall Waterfront Park, near the Burnside Bridge, a quiet memorial amid the activity of the riverside promenade. Twelve monumental basalt stones are inscribed with haiku by poet and incarceration survivor Lawson Inada, tracing the history of Japanese immigration to Oregon from 1890 through the World War II incarceration and its aftermath.

The plaza is deliberately understated — stone, water, and poetry, without a single photograph or explanatory panel inside the memorial space itself. Background context is available on panels at the periphery. The Burnside Bridge pedestrian path is directly above; the Saturday Market operates nearby on weekends. Worth spending 20 minutes reading the inscriptions.
