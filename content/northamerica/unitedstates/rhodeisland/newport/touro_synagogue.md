  In 1790, George Washington wrote a letter to the Hebrew Congregation of Newport that has become one of the foundational documents of American religious liberty. Washington promised that the new government would "give to bigotry no sanction, to persecution no assistance." The letter was a direct response to the congregation's own letter welcoming the President — a correspondence that cemented Touro Synagogue's place in American constitutional history.

Touro Synagogue, consecrated in 1763, is the oldest surviving Jewish house of worship in the United States and a National Historic Site. Designed by Peter Harrison, the finest colonial architect in America, in a Sephardic style influenced by Dutch and English synagogues, the building is at once a religious monument and a masterpiece of Georgian architecture. The pale interior, with its gallery supported by twelve Ionic columns for the twelve tribes of Israel, is one of the most beautiful 18th-century rooms in the country.

The Washington letter of 1790, displayed here in replica (the original is in the collection of the Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati), makes Touro a pilgrimage site for students of American civil liberty. The congregation it was written to still uses the building — Touro Synagogue is an active synagogue as well as a historic site. Tours run several times daily in summer; the combination of the architecture, the history, and the living congregation makes this one of Newport's most rewarding stops.
