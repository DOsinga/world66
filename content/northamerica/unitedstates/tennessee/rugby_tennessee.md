Rugby is one of America's most unusual small towns — a Victorian village frozen in time on Tennessee's remote Cumberland Plateau. Founded in 1880 by Thomas Hughes, the British author of *Tom Brown's School Days*, it was meant to be a cooperative utopian colony where younger sons of English gentry could escape rigid class hierarchies and build something new in America.

The colony flourished briefly, then dwindled. But unlike most nineteenth-century utopian experiments, Rugby didn't disappear — it simply went quiet. Of the 66 original Victorian buildings, 17 still stand along unpaved lanes shaded by old trees, creating a streetscape unlike anywhere else in the American South. The architecture is unmistakably English: board-and-batten cottages, a [Gothic Revival church](/northamerica/unitedstates/tennessee/rugby_tennessee/christ_church_episcopal), [a library still stocked with the volumes the colonists brought from England](/northamerica/unitedstates/tennessee/rugby_tennessee/thomas_hughes_library).

[Historic Rugby](/northamerica/unitedstates/tennessee/rugby_tennessee/historic_rugby_visitor_centre), the non-profit that has managed the site since 1966, has restored the key buildings and runs guided tours through the village. Five buildings operate as museums, including Thomas Hughes's own house [Kingstone Lisle](/northamerica/unitedstates/tennessee/rugby_tennessee/kingstone_lisle). The town was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1972. It's a quiet, contemplative place — best visited on a weekday when you can have the lanes largely to yourself. The surrounding [Big South Fork](/northamerica/unitedstates/tennessee/rugby_tennessee/big_south_fork) gorge country gives the place its outdoor dimension.
