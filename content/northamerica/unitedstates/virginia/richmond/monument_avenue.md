  At its peak, Monument Avenue had five Confederate monuments: Robert E. Lee (1890), J.E.B. Stuart (1907), Jefferson Davis (1907), Stonewall Jackson (1919), and Matthew Fontaine Maury (1929). A sixth monument — to tennis star Arthur Ashe, a Richmond native — was added in 1996 after years of debate. Following the 2020 racial justice protests, all the Confederate statues were removed except Lee's, which was removed by the state in 2021 after a long legal battle. The pedestals now stand empty.

One of the most significant boulevards in America for what it no longer contains. Monument Avenue was developed in the 1890s as a grand processional street lined with bronze monuments to Confederate leaders — a deliberate act of civic mythmaking that shaped Southern identity for over a century. The architecture of the surrounding houses is genuine and impressive: an intact streetscape of late-Victorian and Edwardian townhouses in a variety of styles, with the wide tree-lined median running for over a mile.

Today the pedestals stand empty, their contents removed during and after the 2020 protests. Only Arthur Ashe's statue remains — added in 1996 after enormous controversy. The empty pedestals are more interesting than the full ones would be: they make the argument visible, the history legible.
