Harpers Ferry is a small town of about 300 residents wedged into the confluence of the Shenandoah and Potomac Rivers at the eastern tip of the West Virginia panhandle, and it carries more history per square meter than almost anywhere in the eastern United States. The federal armory that stood here made it the strategic prize that John Brown chose to seize in October 1859 in his attempt to spark a slave rebellion — an event that is usually cited as one of the immediate causes of the Civil War. The raid failed, Brown was hanged, and within two years the country was at war.

The [Harpers Ferry National Historical Park](/northamerica/unitedstates/westvirginia/harpersferry/national_historical_park) covers most of the lower town, preserving the 19th-century buildings in a state of deliberate historical arrest. The armory itself was burned by Confederate forces early in the war and never rebuilt, but [John Brown's Fort](/northamerica/unitedstates/westvirginia/harpersferry/john_browns_fort) — the small fire engine house where Brown made his last stand — survives and has been moved back to approximately its original location. The views from [Jefferson Rock](/northamerica/unitedstates/westvirginia/harpersferry/jefferson_rock), a shale outcrop above the town where Thomas Jefferson stood in 1783 and pronounced the scenery "worth a voyage across the Atlantic," remain as impressive as ever.

Harpers Ferry is also on the Appalachian Trail, which crosses the Shenandoah River here and passes through the town. Long-distance hikers are a regular presence on the streets, and the town's position at the junction of the AT and the C&O Canal towpath makes it a popular destination for cyclists and hikers on multi-day journeys. [Maryland Heights](/northamerica/unitedstates/westvirginia/harpersferry/maryland_heights), the ridge across the Potomac, offers excellent views of the entire river confluence and is among the best short hikes in this part of the mid-Atlantic.

The town itself is tiny and the lower historic area gets crowded on summer weekends. A Thursday or Friday visit is considerably more pleasant. The surrounding countryside — the Shenandoah Valley to the south, the Blue Ridge to the east — is beautiful and makes Harpers Ferry a logical base for a day or two of driving through what was, in 1862, the most contested landscape in North America.
