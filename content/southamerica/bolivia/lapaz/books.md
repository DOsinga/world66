The most famous book set in La Paz is *Marching Powder* by Rusty Young (2003), the account of Thomas McFadden, a British drug smuggler who spent years in San Pedro prison in the middle of the city. San Pedro is unusual in that inmates live in a self-governing community, buy or rent their cells, run businesses, and — for a period — hosted tourist visits arranged with the guards. McFadden became a guide; Rusty Young came as a tourist and ended up staying months to write the book. It reads like a thriller, but the prison is visible from the street and the institutional details are real. Whether you find it fascinating or troubling probably depends on your mood.

*The Fat Man from La Paz* (2000), edited by Rosario Santos, is an anthology of Bolivian fiction translated into English — the best single-volume introduction to Bolivian literature available outside Spanish. The stories range across the country and across the century, but several are set in La Paz and give a texture to daily life that no guidebook manages. For political context, *Evo's Bolivia: Continuity and Change* by Linda Farthing and Tom Kohl (2016) is the most clear-eyed account of what the Morales years actually changed and what they didn't — useful reading if you want to understand why Bolivia's politics feel so vivid to the people who live there.
