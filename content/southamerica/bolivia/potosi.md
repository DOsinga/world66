Potosí was once the largest and wealthiest city in the Americas. Founded in 1545 following the discovery of silver in the Cerro Rico — the "rich mountain" that looms above the city — it grew with astonishing speed: by the early 17th century it had a population rivalling London or Paris, and the phrase "worth a Potosí" entered the Spanish language as a synonym for fabulous wealth.

The silver ran thin in the early 19th century and Potosí contracted sharply, which paradoxically preserved much of its colonial architecture. A tin boom gave the city a second life, but it was never again what it had been. UNESCO recognised it as a World Heritage Site. The [Casa de la Moneda](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/casa_de_la_moneda) — the old royal mint — is the great attraction: an extraordinary colonial building that tells the story of silver, slavery, and empire in considerable depth. The [Cathedral Basilica](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/cathedral_basilica) and the [Iglesia de San Francisco](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/iglesia_de_san_francisco) anchor a colonial centre that retains dozens of baroque churches and grand mansions. The [Convent of Santa Teresa](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/iglesia_de_santa_teresa) and the [Museo Nacional de Etnografía](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/museo_nacional_de_etnografia) are the other key museums.

Tours into the [Cerro Rico mines](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/cerro_rico) — still worked today under difficult conditions — are among the most sobering travel experiences in South America. You will come out understanding the human cost of all that silver. The [Khari Khari Lakes](/southamerica/bolivia/potosi/khari_khari_lakes) southeast of the city are artificial colonial reservoirs, worth the walk for the high Andean landscape and the industrial history.

Be aware that Potosí sits at over 4,000 metres — the highest major city on earth. Altitude sickness is common; give yourself a day or two to acclimatise before doing much. Drink coca tea, move slowly, and avoid alcohol on the first night.
