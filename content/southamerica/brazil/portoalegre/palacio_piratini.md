The Palácio Piratini — named for a battle in the Farroupilha revolution of 1835 — is the official residence and working palace of the governor of Rio Grande do Sul, and it faces Praça da Matriz with the severe confidence of a building that knows it is important. The facade is neoclassical, four stories of stone with a central portico; the whole ensemble is handsome if not exactly joyful. It was built between 1898 and 1921, replacing an earlier and more modest government building, and for most of the 20th century it functioned as the political centre of what was frequently Brazil's most politically volatile state.

Guided visits to the interior are offered on certain days and are worthwhile: the state reception rooms contain good examples of 19th- and early 20th-century Brazilian decorative arts, historical paintings, and some surprisingly decent locally made furniture. The ceremonial guard in traditional gaúcho dress does a changing-of-the-guard at set times that is more ceremonially earnest than militarily impressive, which is rather endearing. Even without going inside, the palace's presence on the square — flanked by the cathedral on one side and the theatre on the other — is one of those civic arrangements that makes you understand why Brazilians from other states regard Porto Alegre as unusually European.
