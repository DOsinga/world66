story: The Jesuit missions of the 17th and 18th centuries were among the most ambitious social experiments in the Americas: self-governing indigenous communities organised by Jesuit priests according to utopian principles, producing art, music, and architecture of remarkable quality. When the Jesuits were expelled from South America in 1767, the missions were abandoned and the indigenous communities scattered. What remains at São Miguel das Missões is the skeleton of one of the greatest architectural achievements of colonial South America.

São Miguel das Missões is about 480 kilometres west of Porto Alegre — too far for a comfortable day trip, but close enough for an overnight journey that is thoroughly worth the effort. The site is the ruin of one of the great Jesuit reduction settlements of the 17th and 18th centuries: a vast stone church and the remnants of a planned town built by Jesuit missionaries and the indigenous Guaraní people they worked with, designed according to Renaissance urban principles in the middle of the South American pampas. It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983.

The church ruin is extraordinary. The facade — what remains of it — stands to its full original height, with elaborate baroque stonework still intact three centuries after it was carved. The carved details (animal heads, religious figures, indigenous decorative motifs integrated into the European baroque vocabulary) are among the finest examples of the hybrid aesthetic that the Jesuit missions produced. The sound-and-light show presented on the ruins most evenings is genuinely excellent: one of the best such productions in South America, it uses the ruins themselves as its stage and tells the story of the missions with real emotion. Allow at least one full day; two is better. The small on-site museum has excellent context.
