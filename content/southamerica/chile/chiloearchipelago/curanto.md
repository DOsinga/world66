  Curanto predates European contact. The Huilliche people of Chiloé cooked in stone-lined pits long before the Spanish arrived, using the volcanic rock of the island to retain heat. The Spanish and later German settlers adopted the technique and added their own ingredients — smoked pork, sausages — to the indigenous combination of shellfish, fish, and potato. The dish now eaten across southern Chile is a 500-year fusion cooked the same way it always was.

Curanto is Chiloé's signature dish: a feast of shellfish, fish, smoked pork, chicken, potato, and a distinctive local dumpling called milcao (made from raw and cooked potato), all cooked together in a stone-lined pit covered with giant nalca leaves and earth. The steam from the shells slowly cooks everything below in a process that takes several hours. The result is a communal meal for many people, traditionally served at celebrations and community events.

In restaurants (where it's usually made in a large pot rather than a pit, and called curanto al palo if made over open fire), you typically receive a bowl of broth followed by the solid ingredients on a plate. The milcao dumplings are starchy and filling and impossible to find anywhere outside Chiloé. The best curanto is in Castro's market area and at restaurants in Ancud that specialise in traditional food. Ordering it commits you to a large meal — plan accordingly.
