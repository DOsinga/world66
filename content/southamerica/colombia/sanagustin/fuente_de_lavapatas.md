  Lavapatas translates roughly as "wash feet" — local legend holds that the name came from indigenous people washing their feet in the carved channels. The actual ceremonial purpose was almost certainly more significant, likely involving ritual offerings of water flowing through the carved serpent and human figures. The channels are fed by a natural stream, so the water still flows through them today, over carvings made more than a thousand years ago.

The Fuente de Lavapatas, inside the main Parque Arqueológico, is one of the most unusual things at San Agustín. Rather than a statue or burial mound, it is a riverbed carved with channels, serpent figures, human faces, and geometric patterns — a hydraulic ceremonial installation through which water was directed in specific ways, apparently as a ritual act.

The carving is intricate and remarkably well-preserved for something that has had a stream running over it for over a millennium. Standing at the edge of the channel and looking down at the figures beneath the flowing water is a genuinely strange experience — the faces look back at you through the current. It's one of the most intimate and unusual pre-Columbian monuments in South America, far less frequently discussed than the great Andean sites but no less remarkable.
