Lake Titicaca sits at 3,800 metres above sea level on the Altiplano plateau, straddling the border between Peru and Bolivia. It is the highest navigable lake in the world, and the largest lake in South America by volume. The air is thin and the light is intense. First-time visitors often spend the first day or two moving slowly and drinking coca tea, waiting for their bodies to adjust.

The Peruvian side of the lake is accessed from [Puno](/southamerica/peru/puno), a straightforward city with a daily market and a busy boat jetty. From Puno, the three main island destinations — the [Uros floating islands](/southamerica/peru/laketiticacaarea/sights/uros_islands), [Taquile](/southamerica/peru/laketiticacaarea/sights/taquili_island), and [Amantani](/southamerica/peru/laketiticacaarea/sights/amantani_island) — are all reachable by boat. The Uros islands are made from cut totora reed and are the most visited; they are genuinely interesting but heavily touristic, with islanders accustomed to the rhythm of morning arrivals and afternoon departures. Taquile is hillier and quieter, known for its distinctive textile traditions and cooperative social structure. Amantani, furthest from Puno, is the most agricultural and the most authentically remote — families take in overnight guests and the sunsets from the hilltop shrines are exceptional.

Inland from the lake, the [Sillustani funerary towers](/southamerica/peru/laketiticacaarea/sights/sillustani_funerar) stand on a promontory above a small turquoise lake about 35 km north of Puno. These cylindrical chullpas, built by pre-Incan and Incan cultures to house the remains of nobility, are among the most photogenic and historically significant sites in the region and are an easy half-day trip.
