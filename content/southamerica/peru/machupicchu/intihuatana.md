The Intihuatana is the most sacred object at Machu Picchu — a carved granite pillar rising from a platform near the top of the ceremonial sector, aligned precisely to cast no shadow at noon on the equinox. The Inca used it as both astronomical instrument and ritual focal point: shamans performed ceremonies here during the solstices when the sun appeared to pause in its path.

The stone is a single piece of rock carved from the natural bedrock, shaped so that each corner points to a cardinal direction. On 21 June — the winter solstice in the Southern Hemisphere — the sun rises directly over the tip of the pillar at the moment of sunrise. The engineering precision required to achieve this, on a steeply terraced mountaintop with no modern measuring tools, is difficult to comprehend.

Unfortunately, the stone was damaged in 2000 when a beer advertisement was filmed at the site using a crane, which fell and chipped the pillar. Visitors are no longer permitted to touch it. The platform itself is elevated above the main plaza and offers good views of the entire site and the Urubamba valley below.
