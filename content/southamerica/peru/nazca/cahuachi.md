  Cahuachi covers about 370 acres but is mostly unexcavated. Estimates suggest that the visible mounds represent only a fraction of the total site, which may contain hundreds of buried structures. The Nazca people did not apparently live here permanently — researchers believe Cahuachi was a pilgrimage centre, occupied intensively during festivals and largely empty the rest of the year, which partly explains why so little household refuse has been found despite the enormous scale of construction.

The ceremonial capital of the Nazca culture, about 28 km west of Nazca town. Cahuachi was built over several centuries, starting around 100 BC, and consists of more than 40 earth mounds topped by adobe structures — temples, burial platforms, and ceremonial spaces — spread across the desert plain. At its peak it was one of the largest sites in South America, but unlike most great pre-Columbian capitals it appears to have been a place of ritual rather than urban settlement.

The site is under ongoing excavation by Italian and Peruvian archaeologists, and most of the mounds are still unexcavated. What you see is evocative rather than spectacular — largely earthen forms in a vast desert landscape — but the scale is impressive once you understand what lies beneath. The Antonini Museum in Nazca town provides context: most of its collection comes from Cahuachi. Tours combine both in a half-day itinerary.
