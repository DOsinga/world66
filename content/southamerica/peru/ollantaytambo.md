Ollantaytambo is one of the few surviving examples of Inca urban planning still inhabited today. The town sits at the western end of the Sacred Valley, 72 km from [Cusco](/southamerica/peru/cuzco) by road, where the Urubamba River narrows and the valley walls close in dramatically. What makes Ollantaytambo unusual is that the streets of the original Inca settlement are still in use — you walk down narrow alleys between stone walls built in the 15th century, past doorways and irrigation channels that predate the Spanish conquest by half a century.

The [Ollantaytambo fortress](/southamerica/peru/ollantaytambo/ollantaytambo_ruins) dominates the town from its position above the main square. A massive complex of terraces, temples, and ceremonial structures, it was one of the last places where Inca resistance held off the Spanish — in 1537, Manco Inca defeated Hernando Pizarro's forces here in one of the few Inca military victories of the conquest. The fortress remains an extraordinary sight: the giant stone terraces rise in tiers from the valley floor to the ruins of a solar temple at the summit, and across the valley, you can see the [Pinkuylluna](/southamerica/peru/ollantaytambo/pinkuylluna) storage warehouses clinging to the opposite hillside.

The town is also the main rail embarkation point for [Machu Picchu](/southamerica/peru/machupicchu). Trains for the Inca Trail and the scenic rail journey to Aguas Calientes depart from here, which means Ollantaytambo functions as a staging post for thousands of Machu Picchu visitors who arrive the night before to rest and avoid the Cusco crowds. Staying overnight rather than rushing through is worth it — the town at dusk and dawn, once the day-trippers have gone, has a quiet and genuinely ancient quality that is surprisingly easy to feel.
