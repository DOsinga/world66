The Sacred Valley — Valle Sagrado de los Incas — is the broad agricultural valley of the Urubamba River, running roughly northwest from Pisac through Urubamba to Ollantaytambo before plunging into the gorge that carries the river down to Machu Picchu and beyond. It sits at 2,800 to 2,900 metres — substantially lower than [Cuzco](/southamerica/peru/cuzco) at 3,400 — which makes it warmer, more agriculturally productive, and noticeably easier on the lungs of newly arrived travellers.

The valley was the heartland of the Inca empire's food supply: terraced cornfields stepped up the hillsides, irrigated from glacier melt, producing the choice white maize that still grows in the same fields today. The Incas built some of their most ambitious sites here. The fortress and town of [Ollantaytambo](/southamerica/peru/sacredvalley/ollantaytambo) is the only Inca settlement still inhabited on its original grid, with stone water channels running down the centre of each street. The terraced citadel of [Pisac](/southamerica/peru/sacredvalley/pisac) rises above its market town. The concentric agricultural terraces of [Moray](/southamerica/peru/moray) and the geometric salt pans of [Maras](/southamerica/peru/sacredvalley/maras) are nearby.

For most travellers, the Sacred Valley is the bridge between Cuzco and [Machu Picchu](/southamerica/peru/machupicchu). The train to Aguas Calientes leaves from Ollantaytambo, and many people now choose to spend a few nights in the valley before continuing — partly to acclimatise more gently than in Cuzco itself, and partly because the valley deserves time of its own. [Urubamba](/southamerica/peru/urubamba) makes a practical base; Ollantaytambo and Pisac are smaller and more atmospheric.

The standard sights — Pisac ruins, Ollantaytambo fortress, Moray, Maras — can all be ticked off in a single long day on an organised tour from Cuzco, but doing it that way misses most of what makes the valley special. Several days of slower travel give time to walk in the high villages, visit weekly markets, eat in valley restaurants, and use the warmer air to climb back up to higher Inca sites in better shape than coming straight from Cuzco. Pisac's Sunday market is the most famous in southern Peru; Chinchero's weaving cooperatives produce some of the country's finest textiles; the Saturday market at Urubamba is the practical workhorse where local people actually shop.
