The fortress of the Chachapoyas stands at 3,000 metres on a narrow ridge above the Utcubamba valley, its perimeter walls rising up to 20 metres from the mountainside. Inside are 421 structures, almost all of them circular — the Chachapoyas built round where almost every other Andean culture built square — many decorated with geometric stone friezes. The site spans 584 metres from end to end and covers six hectares.

Three narrow gateways pierce the outer wall, each designed so that attackers had to enter in single file. Inside, the most dramatic structure is El Tintero ("the inkwell"), an inverted stone cone 5.5 metres high that may have served as a solar observatory. The Torreón, a 7-metre tower at the southern end, likely had a defensive or ceremonial function. Stone water canals, still visible in places, brought water from mountain springs.

The site was built from around the 6th century AD and reached its full extent between 900 and 1100. The Inca conquered the Chachapoyas in the 1470s and used Kuélap as an administrative centre; after the Spanish conquest it was gradually abandoned and the forest closed over it. Work to excavate, stabilise, and study the site is ongoing — you will see sections still under restoration.

Allow three to four hours to walk the full site. Bring water and sun protection; the ridge is exposed and the altitude affects those not yet acclimatised. A small site museum near the entrance provides context on the Chachapoyas civilisation.
