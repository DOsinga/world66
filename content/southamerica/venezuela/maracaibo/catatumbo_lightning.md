story: >
  The Catatumbo Lightning was known to sailors long before meteorologists explained it. Spanish navigators called it the "Lighthouse of Maracaibo" and used it for navigation — the storm is so consistent that ships could orient themselves by it in the darkness of the Gulf of Venezuela. Alexander von Humboldt documented it on his South American expedition in the early 1800s. In 2016, the Catatumbo region was recognised by the Guinness Book of Records as the place with the highest concentration of lightning on earth.
---

The Catatumbo Lightning is one of the great natural phenomena of the Americas. At the mouth of the Catatumbo River where it empties into Lake Maracaibo, a meteorological convergence produces almost continuous lightning storms for up to 10 hours a night, roughly 260 nights a year. From the city of Maracaibo, the horizon to the southwest glows and flashes with the distant storm — a natural light show visible for over 400 kilometres.

The phenomenon is caused by warm, moisture-laden air from the lake colliding with the cold air off the Andes, creating ideal conditions for thunderstorm development. The lightning is mostly cloud-to-cloud, which gives it a diffuse, flickering quality rather than the sharp bolts of a conventional storm. Watching it from the lake itself, by boat, is the best experience — organised overnight boat tours depart from Ologa on the western shore, and the darkness of the lake amplifies the effect dramatically.
